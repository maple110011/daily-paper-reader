POSTERIOR CONVERGENCE WITHOUT FORCE CONVERGENCE: RESOLUTION-STABLE SAMPLING FOR ROUGH BAYESIAN INVERSE PROBLEMS 

ZHILIANG DENG ∗ AND XIAOMEI YANG †

Abstract. Bayesian posteriors can converge under model refinement even when the exact sensitiv-ities used by gradient-based samplers do not. We study this mismatch for discretely scale-invariant rough potentials and its consequences for Metropolized Hamiltonian proposals. For Weierstrass truncations, adjacent classical-force increments grow geometrically in the rough regime, whereas fixed additive quotients converge uniformly and fixed multiplicative quotients converge locally away from the origin. The matched Jackson quotient is distinguished not by convergence alone but by dilation covariance and exact finite closure at the intrinsic scale. Uniform negative-log-likelihood approximation is shown to imply explicit total-variation, Hellinger, and bounded quantity-of-interest convergence, with a Gaussian forward-map criterion for Bayesian inverse problems. At the algorithmic level, measurable kick–drift–kick maps are triangular shears, so exact Metropolis correction does not require differentiability of the proposal field. Local uniform field convergence yields convergence of fixed-length proposals, acceptance functions, and bounded-Lipschitz kernels on common compact sets, while the first classical HMC half-kick has no fixed-step refinement limit. Numerical experiments verify the predicted force-refinement rates, show that posterior stabilization can coexist with severe resolution-dependent retuning of exact-gradient HMC, and demonstrate forward convergence without sensitivity convergence in two-dimensional Darcy flow. The computations are used to assess refinement consistency rather than to claim a universal efficiency ordering among proposal fields. 

1. Introduction 

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) relies on a smooth geometric picture: a differentiable potential defines a force, a reversible numerical integrator follows an approximate Hamiltonian trajectory, and a Metropolis step removes the remaining time-discretization bias. This framework supports sharp results on tuning, geometric integration, ergodicity, and dimension-dependent scaling; see [5, 13, 8, 1, 2, 12, 6]. Extensions to nonsmooth targets cover convex composite energies, truncation boundaries, finitely many kinks, and discontinuity interfaces [3, 14]. The present paper concerns a different obstruction: roughness that persists and intensifies as model resolution increases. The central uncertainty-quantification issue is a mismatch between probability-level and sensitivity-level approximation. Forward maps, likelihoods, and posterior measures may converge under refinement while the corresponding exact sensitivities do not. Posterior well-posedness therefore does not by itself imply that a fixed-step gradient-based sampler has a refinement limit. We use Weierstrass-type refinements as an analytically transparent model; their classical regularity and nondifferentiability properties are well known [9, 11]. Each newly added scale has potential amplitude of order aN but derivative amplitude of order ( ab )N . In the rough regime ab > 1, the potentials converge uniformly while adjacent classical-force increments grow. Fixed additive and multiplicative quotients remain stable because they evaluate the potential at a nonzero scale. The Jackson quotient [10], taken at the intrinsic ratio b, has two further properties: dilation covariance and exact finite closure under the discrete scale recursion, consistent with the use of q-differences in scale-structured problems [7, 17, 18]. These properties distinguish exact matching structurally, not by convergence alone; neither the theory nor the experiments claim that every unmatched quotient fails or that matching is uniformly optimal. The paper makes four contributions. First, it quantifies the separation between posterior refinement and force refinement for the Weierstrass family. Second, it derives total-variation, Hellinger, and bounded quantity-of-interest bounds from uniform negative-log-likelihood approximation and gives a Gaussian forward-map criterion. Third, it proves exactness of Metropolized kick–drift–kick proposals driven by measurable fields and propagates local field convergence to fixed-length proposals, acceptance functions, and Markov kernels on common compact sets. Fourth, it identifies the loss of classical HMC consistency at the first half-kick and derives the necessary refinement-dependent step-size scale. The numerical study is organized around refinement diagnostics rather than a claim of universal sampling efficiency. A canonical experiment verifies the predicted classical growth and finite-scale decay rates. Two one-dimensional inverse problems compare posterior stabilization with the resolution-dependent step sizes required by exact-gradient and finite-scale proposals. A two-dimensional Darcy-flow problem combines a finite-volume solver with discrete-adjoint sensitivities and tests whether forward convergence can coexist with sensitivity nonconvergence. Additional ESS-per-second and ESJD diagnostics are collected in Section C for completeness, but they are not used to establish a general performance advantage for any one finite-scale quotient. The experiments support three conclusions tied directly to the analysis. First, Figure 1 verifies the predicted growth of exact-force increments and decay of fixed finite-scale increments. Second, Figures 2 and 3 and tables 1 and 2 show that posterior stabilization can coexist with severe step-size reduction for exact-gradient HMC, whereas fixed finite-scale proposals retain a nonvanishing tested proposal scale. Third, Figure 5a shows that forward convergence need not imply sensitivity convergence in a two-dimensional inverse problem. These experiments do not establish a universal ESS, ESS-per-second, or wall-clock advantage for the matched Jackson field. The additional performance diagnostics collected in Section C give different orderings in different examples and depend on implementation and tuning. Their role is diagnostic rather than evidential for the main refinement claims. The principal numerical evidence concerns refinement rates, resolution-dependent proposal scales, and forward–sensitivity separation, which are the quantities directly connected to the theoretical results. The theory establishes exactness and fixed-length kernel convergence on admissible compact sets, not a resolution-uniform spectral gap or mixing-time bound. The computations likewise do not imply superiority over all derivative-free or gradient-free samplers. 5. Conclusion 

This paper isolates a refinement regime in which probability-level approximation and sensitivity-based computation have different limits. Uniform approximation of the negative log-likelihood yields convergence of posterior measures in total variation and Hellinger distance, together with convergence of bounded quantities of interest. For the Weierstrass family, however, adjacent exact-force increments RESOLUTION-STABLE SAMPLING UNDER REFINEMENT 21 

grow like ( ab )N in the rough regime, so posterior convergence alone does not justify a fixed-step gradient-HMC limit. Metropolized finite-scale proposals separate the proposal geometry from the unstable exact derivative. Fixed additive and multiplicative quotients converge under uniform potential refinement, and measurable kick–drift–kick maps remain exact after Metropolis correction. The matched Jackson quotient contributes additional structure through dilation covariance and exact closure at the intrinsic scale; these are structural properties and do not imply universal numerical dominance. The numerical results are consistent with this scope. They verify the opposite force-refinement rates, show that stable one-dimensional posteriors may still require severe resolution-dependent reduction of the exact-gradient step size, and demonstrate forward convergence without sensitivity convergence in two-dimensional Darcy flow. They do not establish a general ESS or computational-efficiency advantage for the matched proposal. The results therefore support a precise conclusion: stable posterior approximation can coexist with unstable gradient-based computation, while finite-scale proposal fields provide a refinement-consistent alternative. The analysis concerns local, fixed-length proposal and kernel convergence rather than resolution-uniform long-time mixing.
