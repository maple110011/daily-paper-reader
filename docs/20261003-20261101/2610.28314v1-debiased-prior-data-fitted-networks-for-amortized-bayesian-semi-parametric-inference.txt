## ABSTRACT 

Prior-data fitted networks (PFNs) have recently gained popularity as an end-to-end approach to amortized Bayesian inference. Yet, their statistical behavior for semi-parametric targets, such as means under missingness or causal effects, remains poorly understood. In this work, we develop theoretical foundations for debiased PFNs to perform inference on finite-dimensional targets in general semi-parametric settings. First, we show that, despite being Bayes-optimal with respect to the target, an idealized PFN can exhibit a posterior concentration rate that implicitly depends on the concentration rates of (latent) nuisance functions and can become slower as the prior becomes more diverse. Then, to speed up the target posterior concentration rates, we introduce two generic debiasing strategies based on efficient influence functions: (i) a label correction and (ii) a prior correction. Finally, we show that the debiased PFNs (i) & (ii) possess concentration rates that asymptotically match a convergence rate given by traditional frequentist bias-corrected estimators. In this way, we establish a novel connection between amortized Bayesian inference with PFNs and frequentist semi-parametric efficiency theory . Under additional assumptions, this allows us to guarantee local and global optimality of PFN-based estimators (namely, the Bernstein–von Mises property and minimax optimality, respectively). Therefore, our work provides guidelines for designing debiased PFNs for semi-parametric inference with principled and provable asymptotic guarantees. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Prior-data fitted networks (PFNs) have emerged as a powerful approach for building tabular foun-dation models (M¨ uller et al., 2022; Hollmann et al., 2023; 2025). PFNs combine pretraining with synthetic data and in-context learning (Brown et al., 2020; Dong et al., 2024) to perform an approxi-mate amortized Bayesian inference (ABI) (Gershman & Goodman, 2014; Le et al., 2017). Hence, a PFN (usually a transformer-based neural network) is trained once on datasets sampled from a prior over data-generating mechanisms, and then, at inference time, it maps a new dataset directly into a target posterior distribution. PFNs have been applied widely across various machine learning (ML) tasks (M¨ uller et al., 2025). For example, PFNs were adopted to tasks like time-series forecasting (Dooley et al., 2023; Ansari et al., 2024; Taga et al., 2025), survival analysis (Seletkov et al., 2026; Qi et al., 2026; B¨ ohm et al., 2026), Bayesian optimization (M¨ uller et al., 2023), and explainability (Fonseca & Stoyanovich, 2026; Brockschmidt et al., 2026), among others. Since recently, PFNs were also used for semi-parametric inference tasks 1 such as causal inference (Robertson et al., 2025; Balazadeh et al., 2025; Mahajan et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2026; Javurek et al., 2026; Reuter et al., 2026; Melnychuk et al., 2026). These developments point toward a broader future role of PFNs as general-purpose engines for automating any statistical inference task. Surprisingly, the statistical properties of PFN-based inference remain only partially explored. Our contributions 2 are three-fold:  

> 1

We start by restating that a PFN aims to exactly recover the Bayes-optimal posterior wrt. its prior (Nagler, 2023). Importantly, however, such Bayes optimality does not by itself guarantee favorable concentration properties for a particular finite-dimensional target. We show that the corresponding target posterior concentration rate is governed by two closely related features of the underlying semi-parametric inference task. First, even though a PFN can directly output a posterior for the finite-dimensional target, the concentration rate implicitly depends on the posterior concentration rates of (latent) nuisance functions. Second, informally, these rates can become slower as the prior becomes more diverse.  

> 2

Then, to speed up the target posterior concentration rate, we carefully tailor two generic debiasing strategies (Ray & van der Vaart, 2020; Yiu et al., 2025) specifically for PFNs (see Fig. 1): (i) a 

one-step posterior label correction that modifies the PFN’s target label using an efficient influence function; and (ii) a prior correction along a least-favorable direction that alters the PFN’s prior in a target-specific direction. Importantly, unlike the post-hoc approach of Melnychuk et al. (2026), our strategies (i)–(ii) are applied at the pretraining stage and thus do not introduce additional correction steps at test time.  

> 3

Finally, we show that the concentration rates of the resulting debiased PFNs asymptotically match the convergence rates achieved by well-established frequentist bias-corrected estimators. Hence, the corresponding target posteriors also concentrate at the same rates. This allows us to establish a 

direct connection between PFN-based ABI and frequentist semi-parametric efficiency theory .Furthermore, under additional assumptions, our debiasing strategies ensure local and global optimality guarantees in the form of semi-parametric Bernstein–von Mises property (Freedman, 1999) and minimax optimality (Balakrishnan et al., 2026), respectively. To instantiate our theory, we later use three running examples of inferential targets: (A) mean value 

(classical statistics setting), (B) mean value under missingness at random (MAR) (prediction-powered inference setting (Angelopoulos et al., 2023; van der Laan & van der Laan, 2026)), and (C) weighted average treatment effect (WATE) (causal inference setting (Hirano et al., 2003)). In sum, our main contribution is providing guidelines for debiasing PFNs for semi-parametric inference tasks with principled and provable asymptotic properties.

## 2 RELATED WORK 

In the following, we discuss existing works on (a) the statistical properties of PFNs and (b) semi-parametric inference with PFNs. In Appendix A, we provide further details on related works. 

Statistical properties of PFNs. Nagler (2023) has laid general statistical foundations of PFNs and argued whether transformers are suitable for consistent ABI. Multiple subsequent works then studied downstream aspects of the pretrained PFNs: e. g., the application of PFNs for downstream inference (Nagler & R¨ ugamer, 2025; Ng et al., 2025), and the error decomposition of PFN predictions (H¨ ulskamp et al., 2026; Fortini et al., 2026). Other works explored fundamental limits of PFNs pretraining itself: Kim et al. (2024); Ching et al. (2026) showed that transformer-based PFNs can in principle achieve minimax rates for H¨ older smooth functions; Ma et al. (2025) demonstrated the adaptivity of PFN wrt. different smoothness levels; and Reuter et al. (2025) empirically showed the learnability of latent variables with PFNs. Our work falls into the latter category: We establish the asymptotic properties of idealized PFNs used as semi-parametric inference engines .

Semi-parametric inference with PFNs. Recent work has adapted PFNs to causal inference (Bal-azadeh et al., 2025; Robertson et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2026; Javurek et al., 2026); yet, these works primarily fit PFNs in a na¨ ıve way and, at best, only establish estimation consistency (without further discussing the asymptotic properties). The closest to our theoretical work is Melnychuk et al. (2026), who show that na¨ ıve PFN-based inference for the ATE can suffer from prior-induced confounding bias (Hahn et al., 2018; Linero & Antonelli, 2023) and who apply a post-hoc one-step posterior correction. Our work differs from the former: We characterize posterior concentration rates for general finite-dimensional semi-parametric targets, and we incorporate debiasing directly into PFN pretraining, thereby avoiding post-hoc correction at test time. To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to offer theoretical foundations for PFNs used in semi-parametric inference .

## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

Setup. To validate our theory, we evaluate the de-biased PFN with (i) label correction and (ii) prior correction (Sec. 4.2) for the ATE (Example C with w ≡ 1). In Appendix G.2, we further provide the results for mean value estimation (Example A). For ATE estimation, we use an adapted synthetic prior Π over nonlinear structural causal models from TabPFN v1 (Hollmann et al., 2023). For each 

dx ∈ { 5, 10 } and n ∈ { 256 , 512 , 1024 , 2048 }, we train a standard PFN (Eq. (6)) and two debiased PFNs (i)-(ii) (Eq. (12),(15)) with the same architecture, training data, and optimization budget (thus, all three PFN variants are comparable). We provide all the implementation details in Appendix F. 

Evaluation. Given the Bayes-optimality of the idealized PFNs (Remark 1), we want to evaluate the performance of PFNs based on a modified prior ˇΠ that consists of preselected worst-case distributions (i. e., those that yield worst-case posterior in Theorem 1; see further details in Appendix F). While the modified evaluation prior ˇΠ has approximately the same support, now the Bayes-optimality does not apply, and, hence, we can empirically validate our theoretical findings. Our evaluation then proceeds in three parts. (1) As a sanity check, we evaluate the amount of observed confounding in sampled datasets (with the training/ evaluation priors Π/ ˇΠ, and after debiasing applied to the training labels and prior). Following Remark 2 in Appendix D, this allows to check the potential amount of the first-order bias ( Fψ ) in different datasets. Then, (2) to verify the results of Theorems 2 and 4, we evaluate the L2 risk for different PFNs based on the evaluation prior ˇΠ, namely, RL2 (ψ; ˇΠ, n ) = 

E ˇP ∼ ˇΠEDn∼ ˇP ⊗n || ψ − ˇψ|| L2(qθ∗ ,Dn). Finally, (3) to showcase Theorem 3, we measure Π/ ˇΠ-prior-averaged empirical total variation between the PFN-based posteriors and a frequentist A-IPTW estimator with the ground-truth nuisance functions. 

Results for part (1). We defer the results for part (1) to Appendix G.1. Therein, (a) the evaluation prior ˇΠ contains a significantly larger spread of the observed confounding ∆ than the training prior Π

(hence making it “worst-case”); and (b) both debiasing strategies target this mismatch, yet in different ways: (ii) prior correction spreads ∆ of the training targets at least as widely as under ˇΠ, whereas (i) label correction leaves the spread nearly unchanged. The latter is expected, as (i) corrects the training labels with a data-dependent one-step term rather than modifying the prior. 

Results for part (2). Fig. 5 shows the results. Here, our both debiased PFNs often outperform the standard PFN for sufficiently large n (i. e., n ≥ 1024 for dx = 5 and n ≥ 2048 for dx = 10 ), and achieve much faster concentration rates ˆα (especially for dx = 5 ). This confirms the asymptotic 10 Preprint optimality of both debiasing strategies based on a worst-case evaluation prior ˇΠ (as suggested by Theorems 2 and 4). 

Results for part (3). The results are in Appendix G.1. There, based on the evaluation prior ˇΠ, the total variation of all the PFNs grows with n, which suggests that the second-order remainder and amortization errors are not negligible for the considered n. Still, our debiased PFNs are closer to the efficient normal approximation (A-IPTW estimator) than the standard PFN (especially, (ii) prior correction).

## A EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

A.1 PRIOR -DATA FITTED NETWORKS 

Amortized Bayesian inference & in-context learning. Amortized Bayesian inference (ABI) replaces repeated, dataset-specific posterior computation by a learned inference map that shares computation across related inference problems (Gershman & Goodman, 2014; Le et al., 2017). Closely related ideas appear in neural processes, which learn distributions over functions conditional on observed context sets (Garnelo et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2019). More recently, the emergence of 

in-context learning (ICL) in transformers has provided a particularly flexible mechanism for such amortization: A pretrained model adapts to a new task through its context alone, without updating its parameters (Brown et al., 2020; Dong et al., 2024). Several theoretical perspectives have been proposed to explain ICL, including implicit Bayesian inference (Xie et al., 2022), meta-learning, and the in-context implementation of statistical learning algorithms. The Bayesian and ICL viewpoints become especially closely connected when pretraining tasks are themselves sampled from a prior: conditioning on the in-context dataset then corresponds to learning a posterior predictive rule (M¨ uller et al., 2022). 

PFNs. Prior-data fitted networks (PFNs) instantiate this connection between ABI and ICL explicitly (M¨ uller et al., 2022). Rather than fitting a model separately to each observed dataset, a PFN is pretrained on synthetic datasets sampled from a user-specified prior over data-generating processes. By predicting held-out quantities conditional on the remaining observations, the network learns to approximate the corresponding Bayesian posterior-predictive distribution; after pretraining, inference on a new dataset reduces to a forward pass through the network. A prominent realization is TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2023), which brought the PFN paradigm to tabular classification. Its substantially extended successor supports both classification and regression, categorical and missing features, and datasets with up to thousands of observations, while achieving strong predictive performance on small- and medium-sized tabular datasets (Hollmann et al., 2025). The PFN paradigm has since been extended beyond generic tabular prediction, including Bayesian optimization (M¨ uller et al., 2023), time-series forecasting (Dooley et al., 2023; Ansari et al., 2024; Taga et al., 2025), and, more recently, survival analysis (Seletkov et al., 2026; Qi et al., 2026; B¨ ohm et al., 2026); see M¨ uller et al. (2025) for a broader perspective. 

PFNs for semi-parametric inference. To our knowledge, explicit applications of PFNs to semi-parametric target estimation have so far concentrated on causal inference . Balazadeh et al. (2025) introduced CausalPFN, which pretrains on observational datasets generated under unconfoundedness and directly amortizes estimation of average and heterogeneous treatment effects. Furthermore, Robertson et al. (2025) proposed Do-PFN, which instead pretrains across heterogeneous structural causal models and interventions, thereby learning to predict interventional outcomes without requiring the causal graph at test time. Going beyond these specific constructions, Ma et al. (2026) developed CausalFM, a general framework for constructing PFN priors from structural causal models covering back-door, front-door, and instrumental-variable settings. Importantly, they formalized conditions under which such priors yield identifiable and consistent Bayesian causal estimators, highlighting that an inappropriate causal pretraining prior may remain biased even asymptotically. Subsequent work further incorporated partial causal-graph information (Reuter et al., 2026) or extended PFN-based amortization to causal sensitivity analysis (Javurek et al., 2026). Again, all the above-mentioned works (at best) established the consistency of estimation without further studying the asymptotic properties of the PFNs .A.2 SEMI -PARAMETRIC INFERENCE 

Frequentist estimation. Classical semi-parametric inference constructs estimators of a low-dimensional target while treating other components of the data-generating process as infinite-dimensional nuisance functions (Kennedy, 2024). In causal inference, in particular, prominent examples include outcome-regression plug-in estimators, inverse-probability weighting, and esti-mators that combine the two. A central example is an augmented inverse probability treatment weighted (A-IPTW) estimator. Rather than relying solely on estimated nuisance functions, A-IPTW augments a plug-in estimate by an influence-function-based correction. Under standard regularity conditions and sufficiently accurate nuisance estimation, this construction yields √n-consistent, asymptotically normal, and semi-parametrically efficient estimation of finite-dimensional functionals 16 Preprint such as the ATE (Newey, 1994; Robins, 2000; Bickel et al., 1993; Tsiatis, 2006). On the other hand, targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE) provides an alternative implementation of the same first-order debiasing principle and is asymptotically equivalent under suitable conditions (van der Laan & Rubin, 2006). Modern work increasingly combines such debiased (or orthogonal) estimators with flexible machine-learning estimates of the nuisance functions (e.g., Shi et al., 2019; Hatt & Feuerriegel, 2021; Chernozhukov et al., 2022; Hines & Hines, 2025). This separation between flexible nuisance learning and a target-specific correction is particularly important for our setting: We design our debiasing strategies such that the flexibility of PFNs does not come at the cost of slower target estimation rates. 

Minimax optimality. Minimax theory characterizes the fundamental statistical difficulty of an estimation problem by asking for the smallest worst-case error achievable over a specified model class (Le Cam, 2012; van der Vaart, 2000; Tsybakov & Zaiats, 2009). A minimax lower bound thus establishes a rate below which no estimator can uniformly improve . At the same time, an upper bound can be obtained by analyzing existing estimators (e. g., debiased estimators from above). When the two bounds match, the corresponding estimator is minimax optimal . In semi-parametric problems, however, there is no “one-size-fits-all” optimal rate: The minimax rate generally depends on both the target functional and the restrictions imposed on the nuisance functions. Recent work has therefore derived lower bounds for specific classes of functionals: e. g., the integrated squared density and expected conditional covariance (Balakrishnan et al., 2026); weighted average treatment effects (Jin & Syrgkanis, 2025b); and general linear functionals with mixed-bias property (Jin & Syrgkanis, 2025a). These results motivate our target-specific analysis: In Appendix E, for each estimand, we compare the rate achieved by our debiased PFN with the corresponding minimax lower bound. 

Bayesian estimation. Unlike the frequentist estimators, Bayesian semi-parametric inference places a prior on the unknown components of the data-generating process and propagates the resulting posterior uncertainty to the target estimand. Flexible nuisance models used for this purpose include Gaussian processes (Alaa & van der Schaar, 2017; 2018) and Bayesian additive regression trees (Chipman et al., 2010); see Li et al. (2023); Linero & Antonelli (2023) for broader discussions. 

Bernstein–von Mises property of Bayesian estimators . For finite-dimensional functionals of an infinite-dimensional model, however, posterior consistency of the nuisance functions alone does not ensure valid uncertainty quantification for the target. The relevant asymptotic property is provided by semi-parametric Bernstein–von Mises (BvM) theory (Hartigan, 1983; Bickel & Kleijn, 2012). A BvM result establishes that, after appropriate centering and √n scaling, the marginal posterior for the target approaches a normal distribution whose variance coincides with the semi-parametric efficiency bound. Consequently, Bayesian credible sets acquire an asymptotically valid frequentist interpretation . Establishing such a result is delicate because regularization or prior-induced bias (Hahn et al., 2018) in the nuisance components can propagate to the target and dominate its √n

fluctuations. This interaction between nuisance contraction, prior bias, and target-level posterior behavior is precisely the aspect of semi-parametric Bayesian theory that is most closely connected to our analysis. 17 Preprint

In the following, we provide the implementation details on prior datasets generation for training and evaluation, and on the implementation of PFNs. Code is available at

F.1.1 EXAMPLE C (ATE) 

Training prior. We adapt the prior of the TabPFN v1 (Hollmann et al., 2023) for P (x) and π(x),combined with Gaussian processes with a Mat´ ern kernel ( β = 1 .5) for μa(x). Specifically, for each prior draw P ∼ Π, the covariates X ∈ Rdx (with dx ∈ { 5, 10 }) follow the cause distribution of a random draw from the released multilayer-perceptron-based structural causal model prior of TabPFN v1 , standardized on a reference sample (and we redraw pathological draws with the rejection rule of TabPFN v1 ). Then, the propensity score is given by an independent draw from the same prior: its standardized output, scaled by 1.5, is used as the logit of π(x), and π(x) is clipped to 

[0 .02 , 0.98] (i. e., strong overlap with κ = 0 .02 ). The outcome regressions are μ0(x) = f (x) and 

μ1(x) = f (x) + b + 1 .5 t(x), where f and t are independent unit-variance Gaussian process paths with a Mat´ ern kernel ( β = 1 .5, lengthscale √dx, approximated with 4096 random Fourier features) and b ∼ N (0 , 52). Finally, A | X ∼ Bernoulli( π(X)) and Y = μA(X) + ε with ε ∼ N (0 , 1) .Hence, under the training prior Π, the propensity score is drawn independently of the outcome regressions. For every P , the ground-truth ATE and the efficient variance Veff (P ) are computed on a reference sample of 220 covariate draws (or 222 , if the Monte Carlo standard error exceeds 5% of the efficient standard deviation at n = 2048 ). We draw 69 ,632 populations ( 65 ,536 for training and 

4096 for validation), each with one dataset of 4096 rows; for n < 4096 , the datasets are the first n

rows of the stored datasets, so the training data are nested across n.

Evaluation prior. The evaluation prior ˇΠ is obtained from Π by a targeted selection: For each population, the propensity logit is additionally shifted by ±1.5 μ0(x), where the sign alternates over populations (i. e., an equal mixture of both directions). Hence, the treatment assignment strongly depends on the outcome regression, which yields a substantially larger amount of observed confounding than under Π (as we will see later in Fig. 6), while the support stays the same. For both the training and the evaluation priors, we draw M = 200 populations (disjoint from training and validation), with the same population and dataset seeds for Π and ˇΠ, and D = 200 datasets of 4096 

rows per population (nested across n). The ground-truth ATE and Veff (P0) of each population are computed on a reference sample of 221 covariate draws. F.1.2 EXAMPLE A (M EAN VALUE )

Training prior. Similarly to Fig. 4, we use a logistic Gaussian process with a squared-exponential kernel ( β = ∞), namely, P (z) ∝ exp {sf f (z)} on [0 , 1] , where f is a unit-variance Gaussian process path with lengthscale 0.2 (approximated with 1024 random Fourier features), and the amplitude 

sf = 0 .1 makes the prior over-smoothing. Every density is normalized on a grid of 4097 points, and the datasets are sampled with the inverse cumulative distribution function. Hence, the ground-truth mean ψ(P ) and the variance Var P (Z) are exact (up to the grid). Unlike Example C, we draw fresh prior samples at every training update (i. e., without a saved training bank). 

Evaluation prior. The worst-case evaluation prior ˇΠ tilts the same paths along the EIF direction, namely, ˇP (z) ∝ exp {sf f (z) ± γ(z − 1/2) }, where the sign alternates over populations, and 

γ = 0 .544 equals three prior standard deviations of the slope of sf f along z − 1/2. Our experiments are designed to empirically verify our theory in a controlled setting where the ground-truth is known, rather than to benchmark against existing estimators. Hence, we compare the PFNs that differ only in the training priors / training labels and, thus, do not include frequentist estimators with estimated nuisance functions (e. g., A-IPTW or TMLE) or post-hoc corrections of PFN posteriors (Melnychuk et al., 2026). For computational reasons, we train a single PFN of each type (i. e., standard, debiased with (i) label correction, and debiased with (ii) prior correction), so our error intervals are conditional on the trained networks. Finally, in our experiments, we only cover the ATE (Example C) and the mean value (Example A) estimation under a single synthetic prior Π, yet the former is sufficiently complex to empirically validate our theoretical results. 38 Preprint
