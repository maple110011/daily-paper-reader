## Abstract 

Prior-data fitted networks (PFNs) show that pretraining on large populations of synthetic tasks can enable generalization to unseen datasets with little or no downstream fine-tuning. This paradigm is effective for transformer-based tabular and relational foundation models such as RDB-PFN, but remains largely unexplored for graph-based relational models built on message passing. We pretrain the graph-based relational foundation model Griffin on large collections of synthetic relational databases and ask whether curriculum learning, which benefits transformer-based relational learners, also improves message-passing representations. Rather than fine-tune on downstream tasks, we use the pre-trained encoder as a general-purpose relational representation learner and pass its latent representations to a frozen in-context predictor (TabICL); no component is trained on the target task. On 16 real-world relational classification tasks, evaluated over five seeds against raw-feature and untrained-encoder baselines, a purely synthetic curriculum that begins with schema-guided data and continues with schema-agnostic data gives the strongest frozen in-context performance, outperforming real-data-only pretraining and every unordered single-stage mix-ture of the corpora; when appended after real-data pretraining, it yields the best encoders overall. These results provide initial evidence that PFN-style synthetic pretraining transfers to graph-based relational foundation models. 

## 1 Introduction 

Relational databases (RDBs) power most enterprise workloads, yet relational foundation models still lag behind their text and vision counterparts. A central obstacle is data: high-quality RDBs are private, scarce, and structurally heterogeneous, precluding internet-scale pretraining. Prior-data fitted networks (PFNs) [1, 2] address this scarcity by pretraining on an effectively unlimited population of synthetic tasks. Transformer-based models such as RDB-PFN [3] have recently extended this idea from single tables to full relational databases. Whether the same paradigm transfers to graph-based relational foundation models built on message passing, which encode the schema’s foreign-key (FK) structure as an explicit inductive bias [4, 5], remains largely unexplored. We study this question by pretraining Griffin [5], a graph-centric relational foundation model, on large corpora of synthetic relational databases generated with PluRel-style pipelines [6]. We then evaluate the resulting encoders as frozen relational representation learners that feed a frozen in-context tabular predictor, TabICL [7]. Concretely, we ask: 

RQ1. Does synthetic pretraining data help a message-passing relational encoder at all? 

RQ2. How does synthetic pretraining compare to pretraining on real relational data? 

RQ3. Does curriculum learning [8, 9] over the synthetic distribution improve the learned represen-tations? 

RQ4. What is the role of schema-guided versus schema-agnostic synthetic data? 

RQ5. Does more pretraining data help?

Across seven pretraining curricula and 16 real-world relational classification tasks, extended with three unordered controls, two baselines, and five seeds per configuration, our results answer all five questions in the affirmative, with two nuances. Schema-guided data performs poorly on its own but provides a useful first stage before diverse schema-agnostic data. Although the purely synthetic curriculum outperforms real-data-only pretraining, the strongest encoders arise when it follows real-data pretraining, indicating that synthetic and real data are complementary. 

## 2 Method 

PFN-style pretraining for relational data. A PFN approximates Bayesian prediction by training on tasks sampled from a prior over datasets and adapting to new datasets in a single forward pass [1]. RDB-PFN [3] instantiates this idea for RDBs using a transformer over inputs linearized by Deep Feature Synthesis (DFS). Each entity is flattened with a fixed bank of task-agnostic aggregations (mean, max, mode, sum, and so on) over related rows. This linearization becomes intractable as the hop count grows and, even at low hop counts, can compute many aggregations irrelevant to the downstream task. We discuss RDB-PFN not as a competing baseline, but as evidence that the PFN paradigm works for transformer-based relational models. We ask whether it also works when a message-passing architecture consumes relational structure natively and learns task-relevant multi-hop aggregation rather than enumerating it. 

Griffin as a latent relational representation learner. We use Griffin [5], a heterogeneous message-passing network (MPNN) over the FK graph of an RDB, as our relational encoder. Given a temporal subgraph around a target row, Griffin performs relation-aware message passing with reversed FK edges and produces a latent target-entity embedding that summarizes its multi-table context. We pretrain Griffin on supervised prediction tasks from collections of synthetic databases, giving the encoder an inductive bias toward predictive multi-table structure. Unlike RDB-PFN, which adapts a transformer over DFS features to new databases in context, our pipeline separates representation learning from prediction: Griffin learns latent multi-table representations, while a frozen in-context head predicts on unseen data. 

Frozen in-context prediction with TabICL. TabICL [7] is a prior-fitted tabular model that predicts query labels from an in-context set of labeled examples without gradient updates. We keep it fully frozen and use it as both the prediction head and evaluation engine. For each downstream task, we embed rows with the frozen pretrained Griffin encoder, provide the resulting representation–label pairs as context (sizes {512 , 1024 , 2048 }), and let TabICL predict held-out labels. Because no component is trained on the target task, downstream performance directly reflects the quality of the pretrained relational representations. This yields an end-to-end prior-data-fitted pipeline for RDBs: synthetic encoder pretraining followed by frozen in-context prediction, with no real relational data required for training. Two controls using the same frozen head—raw target-row features and an untrained Griffin—anchor this measurement (Section 3). 

## 3 Experimental Setup 

Synthetic pretraining corpora. We generate two complementary corpora (Table 5). Schema-guided ( SG ): 500 databases with synthetic content on a fixed real-world SAP sales (SALT) schema. The schema is the four-table FK graph of the real rel-salt database; PluRel generates the content, and rows are split temporally. Each database contains 7 genuine multi-class classification tasks on real categorical sales fields: 3 at the item level and 4 at the document level, including tasks with many classes. Schema-agnostic ( SA ): 9,143 databases from three generator sub-families, B (5,623), C (1,408), and D (2,112). All three use the same PluRel generator without a fixed schema and differ only in size and depth settings. They share one task template: mask a feature column of an entity table and predict it from the row’s remaining columns and its FK neighborhood. Each task is binary classification, with roughly 6 tasks per database. B, C, and D differ in structure rather than task type. B is small and shallow (2 to 4 tables, single FK depth); C has the smallest schemas, often only 2 tables; and D is large and dense (5 to 8 tables, about 12 relationships, with many FKs converging on a hub and additional cross-table FKs), placing the greatest demands on multi-hop message passing. Thus, SG provides task and semantic realism on a fixed schema, whereas SA provides structural

Runs initialized on real data are always evaluated only on their held-out families, so there is no leakage. 

Training, checkpoint selection, and seeds. Each pretraining stage, whether initial or continued, is an independent run with the configuration in Table 6: a 100k-step ceiling, 5k-step floor, evaluation every 250 steps, and patience of 20–30 evaluations. Each stage ends at its best validation checkpoint. Thus, all configurations receive the same per-stage budget; unordered controls use one stage under the same rule, so ordered and unordered runs differ in data order rather than per-stage budget. We train and evaluate every configuration with five seeds, varying model initialization, mini-batch order, and downstream in-context sampling while fixing database-pool order and the validation split. We report the mean and standard deviation over the five end-to-end runs. Appendix H gives the full protocol.

Curriculum and schema guidance matter (RQ3, RQ4). PT SG alone ranks last among the pretrained encoders (0.605), only 0.015 above the untrained one. Five hundred databases on one fixed schema yield a narrow encoder. Yet schema-guided data is far from useless. The only difference between PT SA (0.614) and PT SG → PT SA (0.648) is the schema-guided opening stage, and that stage delivers a consistent gain across all four families ( +0.076 on Com1, +0.031 on Com2, +0.005 on Othr1, +0.012 on Othr2), clear on the commerce families and within seed noise on the Othr families. The unordered controls show that the order of the stages, not merely the presence of both corpora, is what matters. One shuffled stage over all SG and 1,400 SA databases gives 0.626, slightly above 

PT SA on all 9,143 SA databases (0.614) but 0.022 below the ordered curriculum and behind it on every family; after real-data initialization the unordered mixtures reach 0.631 and 0.628 against 0.655 and 0.672 for the ordered curricula, the latter no better than real data alone (0.634). Since every stage runs under the same budget and stopping rule, extra optimization cannot explain the gain (Appendix D discusses the remaining data-volume caveat). Opening on semantically realistic tasks over a fixed real-world schema, and then broadening to structurally diverse schema-agnostic data, is an effective simple-to-diverse curriculum for message-passing encoders. This mirrors curriculum effects previously observed in transformer-based relational PFNs [9]. 

## 5 Limitations and Future Work 

Our study covers only classification; extending the pipeline and the SA task template to regression is a natural next step. We also explore only a small curriculum space. Promising variants include placing real data after the synthetic stages ( SG → SA → real) and ordering SA sub-families by structural complexity ( SG → B → C → D). Because the unordered controls use 1,400 rather than all 9,143 SA 

databases, a full-union single-stage run and a second SA stage on top of PT SA would complete the compute-matched comparison; per-task and supervised baselines also remain future work. Finally, we intentionally keep the in-context head frozen. Jointly training the encoder and head end to end, as transformer-based RDB-PFN effectively does, may reduce the remaining gap between representation quality and predictor capacity. Like other relational foundation models, ours uses orders of magnitude less compute than language models; our claim is limited to transfer of the recipe at this scale.

## E Task-level Observations 

seznam-charge is an outlier for every curriculum. All nine runs evaluated on Com1 score below 0.5 on seznam-charge (0.226 to 0.435). Since a consistent below-chance AUROC indicates a systematically inverted ranking rather than an uninformative one, this points to a mismatch between the in-context examples and the query rows for this task, for example a temporal distribution shift, rather than to a failure of any particular pretraining recipe. That the raw-feature baseline is the lowest of all (0.226) and the untrained encoder is also inverted (0.351) confirms that the inversion originates in the task’s rows and split rather than in any encoder. The task depresses the Com1 average of every run equally, so it does not change any ranking, but the family averages should be read with it in mind. Notably, the two strongest curricula are the least affected ( PT C2O2 → PT SG → PT SA at 0.435 and 

PT SG →SA at 0.330, versus 0.245 for PT SG and 0.252 for PT SA ). 

airbnb-destination is insensitive to pretraining. Every pretrained run scores between 0.840 and 0.854 on airbnb-destination, and so does the raw-feature baseline (0.838); only the untrained encoder falls to 0.767. The signal for this task appears to be captured by the frozen TabICL head from almost any reasonable representation, so it contributes little to separating the curricula.

## F Secondary Ablations 

The two ablations in this section were run with a single seed and are reported as secondary observations only; the seeded comparisons of Appendix C do not depend on them. 

Context size. We evaluated every curriculum with TabICL context sizes 512, 1024, and 2048. For every curriculum, the 1024 configuration outperformed both its 512 and its 2048 counterpart, so we report 1024 throughout. We read this as a sweet spot between the informativeness of the in-context set and the effective context utilization of the frozen head: 512 examples appear to under-specify the tasks, while 2048 did not convert the additional examples into better predictions. 

Early-stopping patience. The reported PT SG run uses a patience of 40 evaluations. An otherwise identical run with a patience of 20 evaluations performed worse across the board. The explanation is mechanical: with a longer patience the run survives longer plateaus and therefore has more opportunities to save a better best-on-validation checkpoint. Since checkpoint selection is best-on-validation, extra patience can only match or improve the selected checkpoint at the cost of compute. We consequently used patience values of 20 to 30 evaluations for the longer multi-stage runs as a compute compromise.

Schema-guided corpus ( SG ). The 500 SG databases share the schema of the real rel-salt SAP sales database: its four tables and its real FK graph. Table content is generated by PluRel [6] and then post-processed so that table cardinalities match the real database (500 to 5,000 rows per database, Table 5). Schema-agnostic corpus ( SA ). The 9,143 SA databases come from one PluRel generator that draws a fresh schema for every database; sub-families B, C, and D are the same generator run under three configuration settings that control the number of tables, the FK depth, and the row and column budgets (Table 5), not three separate generators. Every task instance masks one feature column of an entity table and predicts it from the row’s remaining columns and its FK neighborhood; targets are binarized ( k=2 ). Rows are split deterministically into 75 % training, 10 % validation, and 15 % test. 

Regenerability. Each synthetic database is a deterministic function of an integer seed and a fixed generator configuration, so both corpora, and every subset used in this paper (including the 1,400-database SA subset of the unordered controls, which is selected by a pool shuffle with seed 42), can be regenerated exactly from the generator scripts without storing the data.

Multi-database streaming. Pretraining iterates over thousands of preprocessed databases. Because the full corpus does not fit in memory, at most 1,000 databases are resident at a time under a least-recently-used eviction policy. This constraint is particularly relevant for the schema-guided SALT corpus, whose item-level tasks generate substantially more training subgraphs per database than B, C, or D. 

Validation and stage chaining. Validation is constructed from a fraction 10 −3 of training rows using a fixed split seed (12,345), stratified across tasks. Validation AUROC for binary targets is evaluated every 250 steps for checkpoint selection. Early stopping occurs after 20–30 evaluations without improvement, subject to a 5,000-step minimum. Each curriculum stage initializes from the best validation checkpoint of the preceding stage. Data order is fixed using a shuffle seed of 42. Synthetic pretraining uses binarized targets ( k = 2 ), whereas real-data pretraining retains the original target cardinality. All stages use the shared encoder and optimization configuration reported in Table 6. A stage is launched as an independent pretraining job that loads the previous stage’s best checkpoint and otherwise uses the identical step ceiling (100k), floor (5k), evaluation interval, patience, batch size, and learning rate, so a multi-stage curriculum differs from a single-stage run only in the data each stage sees. Frozen downstream evaluation. After pretraining, the selected Griffin checkpoint is frozen and used to generate relational representations for each downstream task. These representations are passed to a frozen TabICL-v2 predictor with an ensemble of eight estimators. We evaluate context sizes of 512, 1,024, and 2,048; the main results use 1,024, which performs best across the evaluated curricula. Neither Griffin nor TabICL undergoes gradient-based adaptation on the downstream tasks. Concretely, for each task the frozen encoder embeds every target row from a 2-hop temporal subgraph sampled with the pretraining settings (fanout 20, few-shot fanout 3, four message-passing layers, hidden size 512, reversed FK edges).
