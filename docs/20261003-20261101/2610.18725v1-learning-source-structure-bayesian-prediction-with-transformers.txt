# ABSTRACT 

A transformer trained on sequences whose source parameters are drawn afresh each time can learn only what the sequences share: the source family and its prior. The Bayesian predictor that knows both, which we call informed Bayes, is the unique minimizer of the training loss and is computable from the family alone; we use it as a quantitative, falsifiable reference. Where earlier work compared transformers with Bayes on sources whose contexts can be learned independently, we build Markov sources with up to 64 symbols in which observations in one context inform prediction in another: product sources with known or hidden encodings, transition rows sharing an unknown base law and depth, and a mixture of three families. Informed Bayes is evaluated numerically from the row integrals of the layered simplex architecture. Our main result is that informed Bayes predicts, from the family and prior alone, how a trained transformer behaves in four tests that go beyond the learning curve: the loss that remains under a source outside the training family (predicted 2.237 bits, measured 2.309), the doubling of the extra loss when two independent components change rather than one (0.345 predicted, 0.350 measured), the benefit of other contexts when a token appears for the first time, and the depth at which copying an earlier continuation starts to pay. On the learning curves themselves, small transformers match informed Bayes when the shared structure is visible in the tokens, and hidden structure needs more layers and training, with gaps below 0.01 bits per token for the largest models. We explain why the imitation is possible and where it must fail: informed Bayes decomposes into sufficient statistics that attention selects and accumulates and an estimator, fixed by the prior, that the feed-forward network learns; in the architecture-as-prior framework, training pays the complexity of informed Bayes under the architecture’s prior, and finite-memory universal prediction bounds how closely a bounded network can reach it. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

A transformer that is trained on many sequences can learn what their sources have in common. Suppose every sequence comes from a source with freshly drawn parameters. Then training cannot reveal the parameters of the next sequence. It can reveal the family of sources, and how the parameters within one source are related: each token may be a pair whose two parts follow independent chains, or all contexts may share a preference for certain tokens. A predictor that knows such a relation can pool observations across contexts. How well does a transformer learn to use this shared structure? There is a natural yardstick. The informed Bayesian predictor , informed Bayes for short, is told the source family and the prior. It is not told the parameters of the sequence at hand; after each token it updates its posterior belief about them and averages the next-token prediction over this belief. When 1arXiv:2610.18725v1 [cs.LG] 13 Oct 2026 Preprint sequences are drawn from the family and the prior, no predictor that sees the same tokens has a smaller expected log loss. The transformer is trained to minimize this same loss and has the memory to approximate the informed Bayes. We ask two questions. How close does a transformer, which must learn the family from examples, get to informed Bayes, even in shared structure? And can this correspondence predict the transformer’s behavior from what we can infer for informed Bayes? Earlier work points out the following. The prediction over a new test sequence (prompt) was called in-context learning and has been explained as inference about a hidden source (Xie et al., 2022). Networks trained on samples from a prior learn to predict like the posterior (M¨ uller et al., 2022). Regression experiments find Bayesian behavior within and across task families (Panwar et al., 2024). Information-theoretic work separates what is learned across tasks from what is inferred within a new task (Jeon et al., 2024). Meta-trained transformers and LSTMs were shown to reach the universal predictor of their training class (Grau-Moya et al., 2024). For sequential sources, the closest studies use Markov chains with independently drawn transition rows (Edelman et al., 2024; Bondaschi et al., 2026), smoothing across context orders (D’Angelo et al., 2026), variable-order context trees (Zhou et al., 2026), and hidden causal structures (Wei et al., 2026). None of this guarantees that a trained network reaches the Bayesian optimum; we measure in bits how close it gets when contexts are tied together (Appendix A compares these papers in detail). In the experiments reported in this paper each of the sources tests one kind of shared structure. Coins test whether the prior on one unknown bias is learned. In product sources every token is a pair of independent coordinates. The table that maps pairs to token labels, the encoding , is either known or hidden. In the shared-base source all transition rows are built from one unknown base law and one unknown depth, so tokens seen in one context help in every other context. The uniform-base source, with a known uniform base law, is a control, and in pooled training the family itself is unknown. The Bayesian predictors are built from the row integrals of the layered simplex architecture (LSA) (Feder et al., 2026). Our main finding is that informed Bayes predicts, in bits, how a trained transformer responds in four tests that deviate from the training conditions (Section 4; Table 1). In each test we first work out what informed Bayes does, from the family and prior alone, and then measure the transformer. Two tests change the test source: permuting the token labels of a product source, where Bayes predicts the loss that remains however long the sequence is, and replacing one or both transition matrices mid-sequence, where Bayes predicts that two new matrices cost twice as much as one. Two stay inside the training family: whether tokens seen in other contexts help when a token appears for the first time, and at which source depth copying an earlier continuation starts to pay. The transformer meets all four predictions. The precondition for these predictions is that the transformers learn the shared structure at all, and Section 3 shows that they do. Two-layer models are close to informed Bayes when the structure is visible in the tokens: for coins, for the known encoding and for the uniform base. When the encoding or the base law is hidden, two layers are not enough, and eight-layer models trained on four times as many sequences close most of the remaining gap. Section 5 asks why the transformer can imitate (approximately) the informed Bayes: the training objective is minimized by informed Bayes whose prediction structure is a function of some statistics of the prefix that attention can select and accumulate, leaving only a map from, e.g., counts to a forecast to be learned. In the framework where an architecture is a prior over predictors (Feder et al., 2025), training pays the complexity of informed Bayes under that transformer architecture’s prior, while informed Bayes pays the complexity of the source under the family’s prior. What the sources examples discussed here add is that these statistics are global rather than local, which explains which sources need more capacity and training, and where the residual gap sits.

In every test the transformer keeps its weights, informed Bayes keeps the training family and prior, and neither is told anything about the test. # 6 DISCUSSION 

Training on freshly drawn source parameters teaches transformers what sequences share, and their predictions approach informed Bayes, with larger models and more training needed when the encoding or base law is hidden. From the family and prior alone, informed Bayes predicts the costs of a wrong factorization and a source change, the benefit of other contexts for a new token, and when copying starts to pay. A trained network can fail each prediction, so these tests measure what it has learned and give circuit and training-dynamics studies a target (Ravent´ os et al., 2023; Falck et al., 2024). 9Preprint The same account makes predictions for language models, where the family is not specified: docu-ments sharing latent structure across contexts are the shared-base case, and the unseen-context test says when a model should generalize to a context never seen continued; retrieval of a first-occurrence continuation is the copying test, with a depth-dependent onset; the mismatch test says that a model keeps paying its training family’s price on out-of-distribution text however long the prompt, and the change test that adaptation to a shift is as slow as a stationary posterior. When pretraining data are not fresh draws from a stable class (Ravent´ os et al., 2023; Chan et al., 2022; Falck et al., 2024) there is no informed Bayes to imitate, and that is where the correspondence should break. Our evidence is limited to synthetic sources, alphabets of at most 64 symbols, at most 26 million parameters, some baselines with one training run, and shared-base tests at α = 1 ; the hidden-encoding and shared-base comparisons change size, training amount and schedule together. Agreement in loss does not establish posterior computation. Section 5 suggests a design to test: learned selection, exact accumulation, and the family’s closed-form estimator.

# A RELATED WORK : B AYESIAN PREDICTION AND LEARNED SOURCE STRUCTURE 

This paper brings together four lines of work: Bayesian accounts of in-context learning, transformer prediction on sequential sources, hierarchical models that share information across contexts, and universal prediction under log loss. The Bayesian interpretation itself is established in prior work. Our use of it is quantitative: for fully specified structured source priors, we compute the informed posterior predictive distribution at every number of observed tokens and ask whether it predicts both transformer loss and the response to controlled interventions. A.1 BAYESIAN ACCOUNTS OF IN -CONTEXT LEARNING 

Xie et al. (2022) model pretraining data as a mixture of latent concepts and explain in-context learning as implicit Bayesian inference of the concept generating the prompt. Their analysis gives asymptotic guarantees for a hidden-Markov construction. M¨ uller et al. (2022) take a complementary amortized-inference approach: prior-data fitted networks are trained on samples from a specified prior and learn its posterior predictive map. Together, these works establish the central possibility used here: training examples can convey a prior, while the prompt updates beliefs about the latent parameters of a new example. Several subsequent results sharpen that connection. In regression, Panwar et al. (2024) find that sufficiently capable transformers track tractable Bayesian predictors across individual function families and mixtures of families, while also documenting departures from the Bayesian rule outside the training families. Zhang et al. (2025) show that perfect pretraining implements Bayesian model averaging under their dynamic prompt model, and separate the resulting in-context error from approximation and pretraining errors. Jeon et al. (2024) decompose Bayes-optimal prediction error into learning the task distribution across sequences and learning the current task within a sequence. This distinction corresponds directly to our setup: the transformer must acquire the source family and prior during training, then infer the freshly drawn parameters from the test prefix. Bai et al. (2023) provide a different, constructive route: a transformer can run several statistical procedures in context and select among them using validation. Our pooled predictor averages source families with their posterior probabilities, so both constructions address algorithm selection but implement different decision rules. Bayesian behavior cannot be inferred from good average loss alone. Ravent´ os et al. (2023) show that the effective in-context estimator in linear regression changes with pretraining task diversity. Falck et al. (2024) derive a necessary martingale condition for Bayesian prediction in exchangeable settings and find violations in language models. We therefore treat informed Bayes as an optimal, numerically testable reference rather than as an assumption about a trained network. Relative to these works, our focus is next-token prediction from dependent discrete sequences, with the complete training prior specified and with transformer and Bayesian losses evaluated on the same prefixes. The source interventions in Section 4 test additional consequences of the same posterior predictive rule rather than relying on a single agreement in average loss. A.2 MARKOV SOURCES , INDUCTION HEADS , AND LEARNED ESTIMATORS 

The copying mechanism associated with Markov prediction has a well-developed mechanistic literature. Elhage et al. (2021) identify induction-head circuits that use an earlier occurrence of a token to retrieve its continuation. Olsson et al. (2022) connect the formation of such heads to a sharp change in in-context learning during training, with causal evidence in small attention-only transformers and correlational evidence in larger models. Singh et al. (2024) causally isolate interacting subcircuits that drive induction-head formation, while Aoyama et al. (2026) relate formation time to repetition, continuation reliability and the amount of training data. These works motivate our experiments on token order, but our claims concern predictive distributions and losses, not the presence of a particular attention circuit. Random Markov sources make the learned estimator explicit. Edelman et al. (2024) show a training progression from uniform to unigram and then bigram prediction, and identify statistical induction heads that estimate bigram probabilities in context. Rajaraman et al. (2024) show that a constant-depth transformer can represent the in-context conditional empirical distribution for Markov sources of 15 Preprint arbitrary fixed order, while their attention-only construction composes induction heads. Bondaschi et al. (2026) show that a single-layer Mamba can implement Laplace smoothing for Markov chains and compare this behavior with transformers. Aky¨ urek et al. (2024) likewise find that transformers trained on random formal languages use in-context n-gram statistics. These papers establish counting and smoothing of context-conditioned continuations as learned sequential algorithms. Other work lets the dependency structure itself vary. For variable-order Markov chains, Zhou et al. (2026) use context-tree weighting as the reference, construct a transformer that implements it exactly, and find that two or more layers can learn the task empirically. Wei et al. (2026) study prompts with randomly sampled causal dependencies and show that a two-layer transformer can implement, and trained models can approximate, Bayesian model averaging over those structures. Their numerical transition kernel is shared across the data set, whereas our source parameters are drawn afresh for every sequence. D’Angelo et al. (2026) show that induction heads need not merely count exact matches: finite attention scales interpolate across partial context matches, while a beginning-of-sequence token supplies Dirichlet-like pseudo-count smoothing. Their hierarchical Dirichlet experiment also shows that trained models use suffix-related evidence from other contexts. It compares learned smoothing with count-based baselines; our comparison evaluates the full posterior predictive distribution under a different shared hierarchy. D’Angelo et al. (2025) show that transformers can select the relevant lag when examples with different Markov orders are interleaved, locating an explicit circuit for context-dependent structural selection. Work on training dynamics explains how such rules arise. For example, Bietti et al. (2023) analyze the staged formation of associative memories, and Nichani et al. (2024) prove that gradient descent can learn latent causal structure in a simplified prediction problem. Our sequential experiments start from these results and change what must be inferred. Product sources test a known or sequence-specific hidden factorization; shared-base sources couple all transition rows through an unknown base law and depth; pooled training adds uncertainty over the source family. Rather than compare only with empirical counts or a generic smoothing rule, we marginalize the stated prior and compare the transformer with that finite-sample posterior predictive distribution. The result complements representational and optimization analyses: it identifies the prediction that full use of the training distribution would produce and separates that output-level question from the circuit and training path that realize it. A.3 HIERARCHICAL PRIORS , SHARED STRUCTURE , AND TRANSFER 

Sharing statistical strength across contexts is classical in language modeling. MacKay & Peto (1995) couple multinomial contexts through a hierarchical Dirichlet model, and Teh (2006) use hierarchical Pitman–Yor processes to share information across n-gram contexts while retaining power-law behavior. These models supply direct precedents for our shared-base source: observations from one transition row can improve prediction in another because the rows have common latent parameters. Our row prior and latent variables differ, but the pooling principle is the same and belongs to this hierarchical Bayesian tradition. Transformers have also been studied as learners of an unknown population prior. Teh et al. (2025) train transformers for Poisson empirical Bayes and prove that sufficiently wide models can approach an oracle that knows the prior, while small models perform competitively in experiments. At the level of pretraining distributions, Chan et al. (2022) show that properties such as within-sequence burstiness and token meaning across sequences determine whether in-context learning emerges, and Ravent´ os et al. (2023) show that task diversity changes whether a network behaves like inference over a finite task set or like a continuous prior predictor. Deora et al. (2025) explain selection among Markov orders through posterior weights that trade data fit against model complexity. Elmoznino et al. (2025) interpret next-token loss as prequential coding and connect in-context hypothesis choice to an Occam tradeoff. These results all emphasize that the structure of the training distribution, rather than sequence data alone, determines the learned rule. There are also direct precedents for two applications in Section 4. Genewein et al. (2023) train memory-based sequence models, including transformers, on piecewise stationary sources and compare them with Bayesian algorithms that infer latent change points and segment parameters. Our source-change experiment freezes predictors trained on stationary sources, gives them no switching model, and tests the additive response implied by two independent components. Comparing early and cumulative log loss also belongs to the catch-up and switching literature (van Erven et al., 2007; 16 Preprint 2012). Our ordered-versus-shuffled experiment uses this accounting to measure when copying an earlier continuation pays for fixed trained predictors; it does not propose a switching distribution. The present paper makes that dependence directly measurable for sequential sources. Every sequence redraws its local parameters, while the training distribution fixes which coordinates, transition rows, and source families are related. The informed Bayesian predictor therefore isolates the value of each relation. The unseen-context experiment measures transfer through a shared base law; the source-change experiment tests additivity across independent latent components; the relabeling experiment measures the cost of retaining a factorization outside its assumed family; and the pooled experiment performs posterior averaging over source families. These are controlled consequences of the stated source priors, rather than a claim that hierarchical pooling or Bayesian model selection is new. A.4 UNIVERSAL PREDICTION AND COMPUTABLE SOURCE PRIORS 

Under log loss, the cumulative loss of a sequential probability assignment is a codelength. ABayesian mixture is optimal in expectation under its stated prior. Universal and minimax codes instead control redundancy or regret over a source class (Merhav & Feder, 1998; Rissanen, 1984; Clarke & Barron, 1990; Xie & Barron, 2000). The Krichevsky–Trofimov probability gives a canonical universal code for unknown symbol probabilities (Krichevsky & Trofimov, 1981), while context-tree weighting averages efficiently over variable-memory sources (Willems et al., 1995). Mixtures across source classes and hierarchical universal coding extend this principle to uncertainty over the model itself (Ryabko, 1984; Feder & Merhav, 1996); classical work also connects compression to selection of Markov order (Merhav et al., 1989; Csisz´ ar & Shields, 2000). Bayesian redundancy and cumulative prediction risk are also characterized through mutual information (Haussler & Opper, 1997). The minimum-description-length principle supplies a complementary model-selection interpretation (Rissanen, 1978). Our regret definitions and family mixtures use this established prediction–compression framework. A separate strand asks what a predictor with limited resources can achieve: Meron & Feder (2004) characterize the regret of finite-state universal predictors of individual binary sequences as a function of the number of states, Ingber & Feder (2006) design deterministic finite-state predictors non-asymptotically, and Dar & Feder (2011) extend the finite-memory analysis to continuous sequences. Our comparison of transformers of increasing depth and training with the unconstrained Bayesian predictor is the analogous question for a different resource, and Appendix C makes the connection explicit. Feder et al. (2025) propose an information-theoretic framework for modern machine learning in which a randomly initialized architecture is a prior over predictors and learning is universal prediction with respect to that prior. The cost of learning a target predictor is its architecture-based complexity ,the negative log of the prior mass that the architecture places near the target, and the distribution of this cost over targets—the architecture’s complexity spectrum —is what distinguishes one architecture from another. Feder et al. (2026) carry the program out exactly for the layered simplex architecture: its prior on large-alphabet distributions has a computable Bayesian mixture, a computable learning curve and a complexity spectrum that is broad where a symmetric Dirichlet prior is narrow. The present paper uses that architecture in the opposite role. The LSA prior is here the source prior, and its row integrals are the building blocks from which informed Bayes is assembled for known and hidden products, shared latent bases and depths, and mixtures over source families; where these combinations no longer have closed forms, we use controlled numerical marginalization and validate the resulting predictive probabilities as described in Appendix D.1.3. The transformer, meanwhile, is the architecture whose prior over predictors is being probed, and informed Bayes is the target whose architecture-based complexity training has to pay. Appendix C.2 states this two-level picture. Thus universal coding, the Bayesian interpretation of in-context learning, and Markov prediction by induction mechanisms all precede this work. Our contribution is to join them in a set of source families for which the learned structure has a quantitative price: informed Bayes predicts the learning curves, and the same source model predicts transfer, adaptation, misspecification loss, and the emergence of copying. 17 Preprint Table 2: The six sources. # C WHY A TRANSFORMER CAN APPROXIMATE AN INFORMED BAYES : THE MECHANISM IN DETAIL 

This appendix expands Section 5: first the objective, then the decomposition of informed Bayes into selection, accumulation and estimation, what each of the present sources adds to it, why and how the shared structure is selected, and what the decomposition predicts about capacity, training and context. The mechanistic statements are hypotheses; the learning curves of Section 3 are the evidence we have for them. C.1 THE OBJECTIVE , CAPACITY -EXPRESSIBILITY AND TRAINING 

Identity (8) says that the population training loss is the loss of the informed Bayes plus the expected divergence of the network’s forecast from it. Two things separate a trained network from that target. The first is its capacity, or expressibility: the architecture must contain a function of xt that approximates QB (· | xt) on the prefixes the prior generates. The second is training: the population loss is estimated from a finite number of sequences, and the optimizer must find the approximation. The regret curves of Figure 1 separate the two only partly, but both are visible: the hidden-encoding source is matched at 1024 tokens by eight layers and not by two, and doubling the training of the eight-layer models still lowers their loss. In the language of Jeon et al. (2024), the weights learn the family and the prior across sequences; the activations then carry, for each sequence, whatever statistics of the prefix the posterior needs. The required capacity question has a classical form. Universal prediction with a finite number of states asks how much regret a K-state machine must pay against the best constant or Markov predictor of an individual sequence; Meron & Feder (2004) bound the achievable regret as a function of K for deterministic and randomized machines under the 0–1, square and self-information losses, Ingber & Feder (2006) give non-asymptotic constructions with deterministic machines, and Dar & Feder (2011) extend the analysis to continuous sequences, where regret against the empirical mean decays as a power of K. In each case a resource-limited predictor pays a regret that vanishes only as the resource grows, and the rate is a property of the class. The transformer gaps of Section 3 are the same object for a different resource: depth and width, and the amount of training, in place of states. The ordering of sources by the capacity they need (Appendix C.6) is a first, qualitative version of such a rate for these classes. 20 Preprint The reason informed Bayes is the right target, and not merely a convenient one, is its regret guarantee. For a smooth k-parameter family with a positive prior, the cumulative regret of QB against every θ is about k 

> 2

log 2 t plus a constant that depends on the prior mass near θ (Merhav & Feder, 1998); for a finite mixture over m hypotheses it is at most log 2 m in total. A predictor that ignores part of the structure pays the regret of a larger family, and one that assumes a wrong family pays a loss that does not vanish with t (Section 4.1). The structure is therefore not optional for a network trained on the family: the objective rewards exactly those computations that use it. C.2 TWO UNIVERSALITIES : THE SOURCE FAMILY AND THE ARCHITECTURE 

The regret guarantee just stated concerns the inner problem, prediction within one sequence, where the class is the source family and the price of not knowing θ is paid in tokens. A trained transformer faces a second, outer problem, and the framework of Feder et al. (2025) gives it the same form. A randomly initialized architecture defines a prior ρ over the predictors it can realize; training on sequences from the family is universal prediction with respect to ρ; and the price of reaching a target predictor Q is its architecture-based complexity, − log 2 ρ(predictors within ϵ of Q), paid in training sequences rather than in tokens. Here the target is informed Bayes itself. A network trained on 

N sequences of length n therefore carries, at test time, a residual expected loss of the order of the architecture-based complexity of QB divided by N n , on top of the inner regret k 

> 2

log 2 t that QB

pays against θ. The decomposition of Jeon et al. (2024) into across-task and within-task terms is this two-level structure stated for Bayes-optimal meta-learning; the framework adds that the across-task term is determined by how much of the architecture’s prior mass lies near QB .Three consequences connect this picture to the experiments. First, the gap ∆( t) of (4) measures the outer cost that training has not yet paid, at each inner time t; a network with enough training and capacity drives it to zero at every t, and the shared-base and hidden-encoding curves of Figure 1 show it doing so. Second, the ordering of sources by required depth is an ordering of the targets QB by their architecture-based complexity under the transformer prior. The decomposition QB = F ◦ S

of Appendix C.3 says why: a target whose statistic S is one attention head’s worth of selection and counting, and whose estimator F is a smooth map of a few numbers, sits in a region of predictor space that shallow transformers reach with high prior mass; a target that needs a partition inferred from a count matrix before F can be applied sits where only deep compositions reach, and the prior mass there is small until the depth is available. Third, the same framework explains a transformer’s preference among predictors that fit the training data equally well: it prefers those of low architecture-based complexity, so when the family leaves a choice open, as the pooled and depth posteriors do, the network’s tie-breaking is the architecture’s, not the data’s; Appendix C.5 discusses where this can and cannot be distinguished from the Bayesian Occam factor of the family. The training-size data already in hand give a first test of the outer cost. If the architecture can represent 

QB , the residual gap should fall roughly as the inverse of the number of training sequences; if it cannot, the gap should stall at a floor set by capacity. Both regimes appear. For the hidden encoding, the two-layer gap at t = 1024 falls only from 0.105 to 0.091 bits when training grows four-fold from 6.4 to 25 .6 million sequences (Table 6), a floor; the eight-layer gap falls from 0.0057 to 0.0024 

bits when training doubles from 12 .8 to 25 .6 million, close to the inverse-proportional rate. For the shared-base source the four-layer gap at (L, t ) = (0 , 1024) changes by less than 0.0002 bits under the same four-fold increase while the eight-layer gap is still falling (Appendix D.6). The pattern is what the framework predicts: a target of low architecture-based complexity under the deeper prior is reached at the universal rate, and one of high complexity under the shallow prior is not reached at all. A dedicated scaling study, with several training sizes per architecture, would turn this into a measured exponent. Two remarks on scope. The complexity spectrum of the layered simplex architecture is computable (Feder et al., 2026), and that is why the LSA prior serves here as the source prior: informed Bayes can be evaluated. The complexity spectrum of a transformer is not computable in the same sense, and the present paper does not attempt it; what it measures is, for six computable targets, how much depth, width and training the architecture needs to reach them, which is the empirical shadow of that spectrum. The meta-learning experiments of Grau-Moya et al. (2024) are the same measurement for a different target, the context-tree-weighting mixture and, aspirationally, Solomonoff’s mixture, and their finding that transformers and LSTMs reach the same loss says that both architectures place adequate prior mass near that target at the scale they used; which of the two does so with the 21 Preprint smaller complexity, and hence with fewer sequences, is the question the framework poses and their length-generalization results begin to answer. The estimator is what the prior fixes: the same counts lead to different predictions under different priors, and the coin experiment of Section 3.1 is the case with no selection at all, where the two-point-prior model and the uniform-prior model must and do learn the two different maps of (24). Accumulation is exact and costs no parameters. Estimation has to be learned on the count configurations that the prior actually produces; the network is never trained on configurations it will not see, and it is trained most on the ones that occur most, which is why the residual gap depends on t in the way described in Appendix C.6. C.4 WHAT IS DIFFERENT ABOUT THE PRESENT SOURCES 

In the independent-row chain, and in the coins, regression tasks and n-gram families of earlier work, the statistics S are local: the prediction in context a depends on the prefix only through the counts in row a, and F acts row by row. The sources of Section 2 break this locality in three distinct ways, and each way has a distinct signature. 

Global statistics: the shared-base source. Under the shared-base prior the prediction in row a

is, by (15), the posterior average of ZL(Ma + eb; q)/Z L(Ma; q) over the base law q (that is, over the exponent α and the rank permutation σ) and the common depth L. The row’s own counts Ma

enter as before, but q and L are inferred from all rows together: the rank assignment mainly from the total token frequencies Nb = P 

> a

Ma(b), which order the labels; the exponent from how steeply those totals fall; and the depth from how concentrated every row looks, i.e. from the count profiles of the rows. So the sufficient statistics contain a global part—the totals and the profiles—that requires attention over all past positions, not only the matching ones, and the estimator has to combine the global part with the local counts. The uniform-base control removes the global part: q is known, and the only shared quantity is the depth, which matters little for prediction. The unseen-context test of Section 4.3 isolates the global part experimentally. When the current token appears for the first time, the local counts are empty and the prediction is the posterior mean of the base law, R q π (dq | xt),which is a function of the global statistics alone. The transformer’s gain over uniform there, 0.793 

bits against Bayes’s 0.799 at L = 1 , t = 64 , and its near-zero gain on the uniform-base control where Bayes predicts none, is direct evidence that it computes the global statistics and uses them where the family says they are informative, and only there. 

A hidden partition: the product source. With a known encoding, the product structure changes selection but not estimation: the head for coordinate u must attend to the positions whose token shares the current token’s u-coordinate, which is a fixed linear function of the token embedding, and the two coordinate predictions are multiplied. Two heads and one layer suffice in principle, and the two-layer model is matched to Bayes at 1024 tokens. With a hidden encoding the partition of the 16 labels 22 Preprint into 2 × 8 coordinates is itself a latent variable of the sequence. Bayes handles it by summing the factorized evidence Qσ (xn) over all 259 459 200 equivalence classes of encodings (Appendix B.4); no network can enumerate them, so a transformer has to infer the partition from statistics of the prefix. Labels that share a coordinate have successor distributions that agree on that coordinate’s marginal, so the partition is readable from the 16 × 16 count matrix, but only after the counts are formed: one layer to accumulate per-label successor statistics, further layers to compare them and form a soft partition, and then the factorized estimate. This is, we suggest, why the hidden-encoding source needed eight layers and 25 .6 million sequences where the known-encoding source needed two layers and 6.4 million, and why the residual gap concentrates at intermediate contexts: at 256 

tokens the gap is 0.022 –0.025 bits while at 1024 it is 0.002 , and 256 tokens is where the posterior over encodings is neither flat nor concentrated, so that the soft sum over encodings—the hardest part of F to imitate—matters most (Figure 4, Table 6). 

Selecting the family: pooled training. Under pooled training the family f is one more latent variable. The Bayesian prediction is the mixture (20)–(21) of Appendix D.7, whose weights are a softmax of the cumulative log-likelihoods of the three family predictors. Family selection is therefore the same computation as parameter selection one level up: each family’s evidence is a sum over positions of that family’s per-position log-probabilities, a quantity attention can accumulate, and the weights are a softmax of those sums. A network trained on the pooled prior has to carry three family-specific predictions and their running evidences at once, which requires the network to combine within-family inference with an additional weighting step. At four observed tokens, increasing the model from two to four layers reduces the gap to the Bayesian mixture from 0.0197 to 

0.0052 bits in run 1 and from 0.0190 to 0.0025 bits in run 2, at the same training budget. C.5 WHY AND HOW THE STRUCTURE IS SELECTED 

Why a trained network uses the shared structure rather than ignoring it follows from Section 5: under the training prior, ignoring the structure costs regret at every context length, and the objective is the expected loss over exactly that prior. The size of the incentive is the difference between the regret of the family and the regret of the larger family the network would otherwise be learning. For the shared-base source at depth zero, the family has the d − 1 parameters of the base law and the tokens are independent; a predictor that treats the 64 rows as independent would have to learn 

64 × 63 parameters from the same tokens, and its regret would be far larger at every tested t. The pooled setting makes the incentive explicit: not knowing the family costs Bayes at most log 2 3 bits cumulatively (Appendix D.7.1), whereas predicting with the wrong family costs R∞ bits on every token (Section 4.1). A network that did not select would pay the second cost; one that selects as Bayes does pays the first. How the structure is selected is the posterior over the discrete latent variables—the family, the depth, the encoding—which is a softmax of accumulated log-evidence, and the posterior over the continuous parameters, which the estimator F represents implicitly. Two consequences follow. First, selection is soft and stays soft: the posterior over families does not always concentrate, because the shared-base and uniform-base priors both place positive mass on every positive transition matrix and remain ambiguous with unlimited data (inequality (23)). A network imitating QB must remain equally undecided, and the pooled comparisons at longer contexts, once the Bayesian references are complete, test exactly this. Second, the preference for the simpler structure that transformers display when the evidence is ambiguous (Deora et al., 2025) is, in our setting, the prior’s preference and not the architecture’s: when one family is nested in another, or a lower depth is nested in a higher one, the evidence of the smaller family integrates over fewer parameters, so its posterior weight is larger at short contexts and yields only as the counts accumulate. This is the Bayesian universality or Occam factor. A network that imitates QB inherits it, and departs from it only where it departs from QB ; the depth posterior of the shared-base source, which selects the lower depth until the row profiles rule it out, is the place to test this directly. Where the network does depart from QB , the architecture’s own preference of Appendix C.2—for predictors of low architecture-based complexity—is the natural candidate for the direction of the departure, and a test that separates the two universality factors would compare the network’s family weights with the Bayesian ones on prefixes where the family evidence is balanced. 23 Preprint C.6 WHAT THIS PREDICTS ABOUT CAPACITY , TRAINING AND CONTEXT 

The decomposition turns the open questions of the introduction into concrete expectations, which the present experiments partly test and future ones can test further. 

Capacity which allows the expressibility of the approximated target is set by the depth of the composition S → F and by the size of S. Local counts need one layer of selection and one map; a hidden partition needs layers to form statistics, compare them and select; global statistics need width to carry d totals and row profiles beside the local counts. The sources here order themselves accordingly: coins and the known-encoding product are already well approximated by two layers; four layers improve both the known-encoding and uniform-base comparisons; and eight layers bring the hidden-encoding and shared-base models close to informed Bayes. Four layers also improve the pooled model, which must carry three family-specific predictions. 

Training is set by how many distinct count configurations the estimator has to be learned on. Configu-rations that the prior produces rarely are learned last; for the shared-base source these are the deep, sparse rows at long contexts, and it is at t = 1024 that doubling the eight-layer training still helps at every depth (Figure 7). 

Context enters through the nonlinearity of F . At short contexts the posterior is close to the prior and F is nearly constant; at long contexts the posterior is concentrated and F is nearly the empirical frequency; in between, F is most curved and the network’s approximation is weakest. This predicts that the residual gap peaks at intermediate t, and this occurs in several comparisons: at t = 32 for the two-layer depth-(1 , 1) product model (Figure 3), at t = 128 for the two-layer uniform-base L = 0 

control, and at t = 256 for the hidden encoding (Section 3.3). The Bayesian curve says where the hard part of each source lies, and the transformer’s gap sits there. These expectations are consistent with the comparisons reported here; they are not established by them. A direct test of the mechanism would read the attention patterns and the count-like activations against the statistics that QB needs, source by source.

The transformer’s weights are fixed before evaluation. Informed Bayes continues to use the family and prior represented in the transformer’s training sequences. Paired conditions share histories, source parameters and the random numbers that drive the source wherever their test laws permit. All losses and regrets are in bits. Means compare predictions on the same histories, and uncertainty is computed across whole sequences, preserving all tested times and paired conditions. Calculations with continuous or latent variables are repeated with independent integration rules or chains, and, except for explicitly marked preliminary estimates, test-set means are reported only after every required reference passes the criteria in Appendix D.
