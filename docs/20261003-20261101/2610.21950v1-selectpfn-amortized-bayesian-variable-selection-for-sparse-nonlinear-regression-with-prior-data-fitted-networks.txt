Abstract 

Tabular foundation models based on prior-data fitted networks (PFNs) predict on a new data set in a single forward pass, but they cannot report which inputs their predictions depend on. We propose SelectPFN, a PFN that performs Bayesian variable selection and prediction jointly for sparse nonlinear regression Y = f (XS ) + ε, in which the response depends on a small active set S of the p inputs, |S| ≪ p. Given a training set, SelectPFN returns posterior inclusion probabilities for every variable and predictive draws, each computed from a sampled subset only, so that excluded variables provably cannot affect the prediction. We show that this selection-restricted prediction is exactly what the Bayesian model prescribes. The network is pretrained once on synthetic tasks from a meta-prior built around a group spike-and-slab sparse-input neural network and needs no data-set-specific fitting. The column-permutation equivariance of the architecture, combined with a random interleaving of original and knockoff columns, yields knockoff statistics with exact finite-sample false discovery rate control even when the meta-prior is wrong. We also develop theory for what amortization can and cannot guarantee: aBayes-regret bound for the amortized median probability model; a two-sided characterization showing that the information index nκ 2

On out-of-prior nonlinear designs with p up to 500, SelectPFN improves the area under the precision–recall curve by 0.23–0.28 over the lasso, BART, DART, LassoNet and a group-penalized sparse neural network, and by 0.16 over TabPFN with permutation importance (all Holm-adjusted p < 10 −100 ), at a false discovery proportion of about 0.07, in 0.04 seconds per data set (more than 300 times faster than DART). Identifying which of many candidate variables drive a response is a basic task of modern data analysis. It arises whenever the number of candidate predictors p is comparable to or exceeds the sample size n: in genomics, where thousands of gene-expression or genotype measurements are screened for association with a disease (Golub et al., 1999; Wu et al., 2009; Guan and Stephens, 2011); in medical imaging, where voxel-level or radiomic features are related to clinical outcomes (Grosenick et al., 2013; Lambin et al., 2017); in biomedical research more broadly, where high-dimensional covariates enter survival and risk models (Tibshirani, 1997); and in economics, where a few relevant controls must be chosen from many (Belloni et al., 2014). Given D = {(xi, y i)}ni=1 

with xi ∈ Rp, the goal is both accurate prediction and the identification of a small subset of variables that carries the information about the response. The statistical theory of this regime is by now mature for linear models (Fan and Lv, 2008, 2010; B¨ uhlmann and van de Geer, 2011; Hastie et al., 2015; Wainwright, 2019), and it has more recently been extended to nonlinear models: sparse additive models (Ravikumar et al., 2009; Meier et al., 2009), nonparametric regression with variable selection (Lafferty and Wasserman, 2008; Yang and Tokdar, 2015; Jiang and Tokdar, 2021), and sparse-input neural networks (Liang et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2022; Luo and Halabi, 2025). In applications, a selection method has to meet three needs at once. It must be flexible enough to capture nonlinear and interaction effects, because a variable that acts only through an interaction carries no linear signal. It must be fast and free of expert tuning, so that it can be applied routinely and to many data sets. And it must come with guarantees on false selections, so that the selected variables can be trusted. Existing methods meet these needs only in part (Section 2). Penalized and Bayesian selection for linear models (Tibshirani, 1996; Fan and Li, 2001; Zhang, 2010; George and McCulloch, 1993; Roˇ ckov´ a and George, 2018) is fast and well understood, but a variable that acts only through a nonlinear or interaction effect carries no linear signal and is missed. Sparse additive models (Lin and Zhang, 2006; Ravikumar et al., 2009), Bayesian regression trees (Chipman et al., 2010; Linero, 2018) and sparse neural networks (Lemhadri et al., 2021; Luo and Halabi, 2025; Sun et al., 2022) capture such effects, but each solves a new, tuning-sensitive optimization or sampling problem for every data set, which is slow when p is large and gives no finite-sample error control. Knockoffs (Barber and Cand` es, 2015; Cand` es et al., 2018) supply such control, but their power rests on a feature statistic that must itself be fitted to each data set. Tabular foundation models built on prior-data fitted networks (PFNs; M¨ uller et al., 2022), such as TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2023, 2025), remove per-data-set fitting altogether and predict in a single forward pass, but they use every column and cannot say which variables a prediction depends on, and the first amortized selectors (Alrawkan et al., 2026; Schiekiera et al., 2026) are restricted to linear models, and LinearPFN to few variables. What is missing is a selector for sparse nonlinear regression that is amortized, integrated with prediction, and equipped with guarantees that do not depend on how well the network was trained. We propose SelectPFN, a PFN that performs Bayesian variable selection and prediction jointly. Selection in SelectPFN is integrated rather than post hoc: the predictor has the form ˆ y∗ =

gθ(x∗, ˆS , D ˆS ) for a test point x∗, where D ˆS keeps only the columns in ˆS, so a variable outside the selected set ˆS cannot influence the prediction. The network is pretrained once on synthetic regression tasks drawn from a sparse nonlinear meta-prior, built around a group spike-and-slab sparse-input neural network. For a new data set, one forward pass returns posterior inclusion prob-abilities (PIPs) for all variables. Subsets of variables are then drawn from these PIPs, a second pass predicts from the columns of each subset only, and the predictions are averaged over the subsets, 2which is Bayesian model averaging (BMA). This restriction is exactly what the Bayesian model prescribes, because given the active set the posterior predictive depends on the data only through the active columns. A knockoff mode adds exact finite-sample control of the false discovery rate (FDR) that holds whatever the network has learned. Because a trained network is a fixed map from data sets to PIPs, its analysis becomes a question about amortization: what a small training loss guarantees for selection, which variables can be found at all, and what cannot be guaranteed for a fixed truth. We answer these questions and evaluate the method in extensive simulations and on real data. 

Contributions. 

1. Method. We formulate amortized Bayesian variable selection for sparse nonlinear regression. SelectPFN is a two-pass PFN: a selection pass returns PIPs, and a prediction pass sees only the selected columns, which, given the active set, is exactly the Bayesian predictive (Lemma 1). Both heads are trained with strictly proper scoring rules whose population minimizers are the PIPs and the subset-conditional posterior predictive (Proposition 1). Sparsity enters through the meta-prior, a group spike-and-slab sparse-input network mixed with additive, interaction, tree, Gaussian-process and linear families, not through a penalty on network weights. 2. Exact error control. With random interleaving of original and knockoff columns, the knockoff statistic Wj = qj − ˜qj , where qj and ˜ qj are the network’s inclusion probabilities for xj and its knockoff, yields exact finite-sample FDR control for any network weights and any meta-prior (Theorem 1). 3. Theory of amortized selection. The selection loss decomposes into a Bayes floor plus an amortization gap, which bounds the Bayes regret of the amortized median probability model. The information needed to detect a variable grows like (2 /λ j ) log(1 /ω ), where ω is the prior weight of structures that can use the variable as the truth does and λj ≤ 1 is an efficiency, which explains why the meta-prior must give explicit weight to interaction-only structures, as an ablation confirms. Average-case training cannot by itself deliver frequentist selection consistency (Section 4). 4. Numerical evidence. In simulations from nonlinear designs that are not part of the meta-prior, SelectPFN selects variables more accurately than fifteen penalized, Bayesian, sparse-neural-network and foundation-model methods, at a fraction of their cost; its inclusion probabilities are close to an exactly enumerable posterior; and a controlled study confirms that the prior weight governs detection as the theory predicts (Section 5). The advantage carries over to real covariate matrices and real responses (Section 6). Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 describes the method, Section 4 develops the theory, Section 5 reports simulation studies, Section 6 analyzes real data, and Section 7 discusses extensions. Implementation details, proofs and additional results are in the appendices. 

# 2 Related work 

2.1 Penalized and Bayesian selection in linear models 

Variable selection in linear models has been studied for three decades, along two complementary lines: penalized regression, which selects variables by shrinking some coefficients exactly to zero, and Bayesian variable selection, which places a prior on which variables are active and reports 3posterior probabilities. In the first line, the lasso (Tibshirani, 1996) estimates and selects simul-taneously through an ℓ1 penalty, but it shrinks large coefficients and is selection-consistent only under restrictive irrepresentability conditions (Zhao and Yu, 2006). The adaptive lasso (Zou, 2006) reweights the penalty to recover the oracle property, and the nonconvex SCAD (Fan and Li, 2001) and MCP (Zhang, 2010) penalties remove the bias directly; the elastic net (Zou and Hastie, 2005) stabilizes selection among correlated predictors, and the group lasso (Yuan and Lin, 2006) selects predefined groups of variables. When p ≫ n, sure independence screening (Fan and Lv, 2008) reduces the dimension before any of these is applied. The second line, Bayesian variable selec-tion, places a prior on inclusion indicators γ ∈ { 0, 1}p (Mitchell and Beauchamp, 1988; George and McCulloch, 1993) and reports posterior inclusion probabilities P(γj = 1 | D), which quantify uncertainty about each variable and lead to the median probability model (Barbieri and Berger, 2004). Stochastic search over the 2 p models is slow and mixes poorly, and the spike-and-slab lasso (Roˇ ckov´ a and George, 2018) recovers fast posterior modes by optimization, at the price of the full posterior. All of these methods assume that the response is linear in the covariates: a variable that affects the response only through a nonlinear function or through an interaction with another vari-able can have no linear association with the response at all, and is then missed, and each analysis must be refitted and retuned for every new data set. 

2.2 Selection in nonlinear models 

To capture nonlinear and interaction effects, selection has been extended beyond linear models along three lines of increasing flexibility: sparse additive models, which allow each variable its own nonlinear effect; tree ensembles, which also capture interactions; and sparse neural networks, which can represent general nonlinear functions. Each line has penalized and Bayesian versions. In the first line, COSSO (Lin and Zhang, 2006) and sparse additive models (Ravikumar et al., 2009) fit a smooth function for each variable and set whole functions to zero, but by construction they cannot detect a variable that matters only through an interaction with another variable. Model-free screening by distance correlation (Li et al., 2012) or HSIC (Yamada et al., 2014) relaxes the additive assumption and detects general dependence, but it only ranks variables and provides no fitted model or selected set. The second line, tree ensembles, captures interactions automatically. Random forests (Breiman, 2001) rank variables by importance scores computed after fitting, which have no probabilistic meaning; BART (Chipman et al., 2010) places a prior on a sum of trees, and DART (Linero, 2018) adds a sparsity-inducing prior on how often each variable is used for splitting, so that the posterior indicates which variables are included. The third line makes neural networks sparse in their inputs. On the penalized side, a group penalty on all outgoing weights of an input removes that input from the network: LassoNet (Lemhadri et al., 2021) does so through a hierarchical skip connection, and concave group penalties (Luo and Halabi, 2025) avoid the over-shrinkage that leads the group lasso to retain unimportant variables. On the Bayesian side, spike-and-slab or horseshoe priors on the weights or hidden units give sparse Bayesian neural networks (Ghosh et al., 2019; Bai et al., 2020), with selection-consistency guarantees for spike-and-slab priors on the input layer (Liang et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2022). All three lines are flexible, but every method in them solves a new nonconvex optimization or Markov chain Monte Carlo problem for each data set, with tuning parameters chosen by cross-validation; the cost grows quickly with p, samplers over inclusion indicators mix poorly when p ≫ n, and none of these methods controls false selections in finite samples. 42.3 Finite-sample error control with knockoffs 

Finite-sample control of false selections is provided by the knockoff framework. Fixed-design knock-offs (Barber and Cand` es, 2015) control the FDR in linear Gaussian models with n ≥ p; model-X knockoffs (Cand` es et al., 2018) remove the model assumption by requiring knowledge of the covari-ate distribution instead, and then accept any feature statistic that is antisymmetric in swapping a variable with its knockoff. Deep knockoffs (Romano et al., 2020) construct knockoffs for non-Gaussian covariates, DeepPINK (Lu et al., 2018) uses a neural network as the feature statistic, and derandomization (Ren et al., 2023) reduces the variability that comes from a single knockoff draw. Validity in this framework does not depend on the model, but power depends entirely on the feature statistic. Flexible statistics such as DeepPINK must be trained for every data set, and no statistic computed in a single pass of a pretrained network has been available. 

2.4 Amortized inference and prior-data fitted networks 

Amortized inference replaces per-data-set computation by a network trained once on simulated data. In simulation-based inference (Cranmer et al., 2020), networks such as BayesFlow (Radev et al., 2022) learn the posterior of the parameters of a fixed simulator. Prior-data fitted networks (M¨ uller et al., 2022) go further and train a transformer across data sets drawn from a meta-prior, so that one forward pass approximates the posterior predictive p(y∗ | x∗, D ) for a new data set D

and test point x∗. TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2023, 2025) showed that this approach is competitive with tuned gradient boosting on small and medium tabular problems. On the theory side, Nagler (2023) analyzed the bias and variance of PFN predictions, and Melnychuk et al. (2026) showed that the prior need not be washed out by the data. Relevance can be extracted from a PFN only after the fact, by Shapley values (Lundberg and Lee, 2017) or by a second network trained to predict attributions zero-shot (Fonseca and Stoyanovich, 2026), and Zhao et al. (2026) studied how transformers trained on sparse linear priors suppress irrelevant features internally. A PFN nevertheless attends to every column: post-hoc relevance does not restrict the prediction to the variables it reports, and it comes with neither inclusion probabilities nor error guarantees. 

2.5 Amortized variable selection 

Only very recently has selection itself been amortized. Alrawkan et al. (2026) train a classifier on simulated linear models to label predictors as active from least-squares summaries, and Maceda et al. (2026) estimate PIPs for sparse linear regression with an amortized network. LinearPFN (Schiekiera et al., 2026) is pretrained on a spike-and-slab prior over main effects and pairwise interactions and returns PIPs, coefficients and predictions in one pass, for up to p = 30 variables. PACE (Qin et al., 2026) places a frozen tabular-foundation-model column encoder in front of an existing screening statistic, so the selection rule itself is not learned. Beyond regression, amortized causal discovery (Lorch et al., 2022) learns edge probabilities for nonlinear structural models with few variables, and amortized model comparison (Radev et al., 2023) returns posterior probabilities over a small, fixed set of models. These methods are linear, limited to few variables or to a fixed set of models, or do not couple selection to prediction, and none comes with theory for the amortized posterior. SelectPFN is designed to close this gap for sparse nonlinear regression. 53 Method

Meaning of the inclusion indicators. For γj to mean “ xj is used by f ”, each active variable should have a non-negligible effect on the response. The generator therefore estimates the total effect of every active variable by Monte Carlo and redraws f when one falls below a small threshold; if this repeatedly fails, the weak variables are set to zero and labelled inactive (Table 14). Even so, the data cannot always tell which variables are active. When several covariates are strongly correlated, they carry nearly the same information about the response, and the posterior naturally spreads its inclusion probability among them rather than committing to one. Because SelectPFN approximates this posterior, a group of moderate PIPs on correlated variables is best read as evidence that at least one variable of the group matters.

3.5 Model 

SelectPFN uses the table transformer of Section 3.2, denoted Fθ, with alternating row and column attention and no embedding of column identity. Because it is equivariant to column permutations, it has no notion of “variable 3” and must judge each variable from its values and their relation to the response. The same network is used in two passes. 

Selection pass. The training set (with responses) is encoded; each feature’s representation is pooled across rows (mean and attention pooling, concatenated with the pooled response represen-tation and a global token that carries log n and log p), and a head outputs qj (D) = logit −1(sθ(hj )), where hj is the pooled representation of column j. The test covariate is not used, so q is a function of D alone, matching πj (D). 

Prediction pass. Given a subset S, the excluded columns are removed from the training data and the test covariate, and the network is applied to ( DS , x ∗,S ) to produce a discretized predictive distribution over standardized y (a “bar distribution”; M¨ uller et al., 2022). Because excluded columns never enter the computation,

3.7 Inference 

After pretraining, a new data set needs no optimization (Algorithm 2). The outputs are the PIPs; paired draws, each attached to the subset that produced it, whose mixture approximates Bayesian model averaging (BMA); and a single reported model, the median probability model (MPM) ˆS = {j : qj > 1/2} (Barbieri and Berger, 2004). The B subsets are drawn once per data set and shared by all test points, each prediction pass processes all test points at once, and it is cached per distinct subset; distinct subsets are few when the posterior is concentrated. The BMA samples subsets from the product of marginals; capturing posterior dependence between inclusions (“ x3 or x4, but rarely both”) would require an autoregressive selection head (Section 7). 

3.8 Knockoff mode: exact finite-sample FDR control 

PIPs are calibrated only to the extent that the meta-prior resembles the data. For a guarantee that does not depend on the prior, we combine SelectPFN with model-X knockoffs (Cand` es et al., 2018). The FDR guarantee itself is the standard one; what is new is a construction that makes a pretrained 

network a valid knockoff statistic without per-data-set training, in contrast to neural statistics such as DeepPINK (Lu et al., 2018), which are fitted to each data set. Let y = ( y1, . . . , y n)⊤ and let ˜X be a knockoff copy of X: ˜X ⊥⊥ y | X, and swapping any subset of columns between X and ˜X

leaves the law of [ X, ˜X] unchanged. 

Training. Exact knockoffs are cheap for synthetic tasks, whose PX is known (Gaussian knockoffs for the latent Gaussian copula). The network receives the 2 p columns [ X, ˜X] in a random order, with responses, and the selection head is supervised with γj for original columns and 0 for knockoff columns. No input marks which columns are knockoffs. 

Inference. Draw a uniformly random permutation of the 2 p columns independently of the data ,apply the network, map the outputs back, and set Wj = qj − ˜qj , where ˜ qj is the output for the knockoff copy of xj ; then apply the knockoff+ threshold at level αFDR (Barber and Cand` es, 2015). Theorem 1 shows that the false discovery rate is at most αFDR in finite samples, for any network weights and whether or not the meta-prior is correct, provided the knockoffs are valid. Exact column equivariance in finite-precision arithmetic is not required: the random interleaving makes the flip-sign property hold in distribution. On real data, knockoffs are constructed approximately (second-order or deep knockoffs; Romano et al., 2020), and the guarantee holds to the extent that the approximation is accurate. 

3.9 Scaling to large p

Training cost grows with the number of cells, and feature attention is quadratic in p. We therefore pretrain at p ≤ pmax and, for larger p, (1) screen: randomly partition the variables into blocks of 11 size at most pmax , compute q within each block, and average over R random partitions; (2) refine: run one joint pass on the top-k variables. Column equivariance means block assignment introduces no ordering artifacts. A variable that acts only through an interaction with a variable placed in another block can be missed in a single partition; repeated partitions mitigate this. This scheme is implemented but not yet validated beyond p = 500 (Section 7). 

# 4 Theory 

A trained PFN is a fixed function q that maps a data set to a vector of inclusion probabilities. Training controls only an average over the meta-prior of how far q is from the exact posterior π.This section asks what that average buys, and what it cannot buy. Four results answer the question. 

• Section 4.1: the training loss controls the Bayes risk of the reported model. 

• Section 4.2: knockoff mode gives exact finite-sample FDR control whatever the network has learned. 

• Section 4.3: the meta-prior’s weight on the right function class sets the sample size at which a variable is found. This explains why the meta-prior must contain interaction-only structures, as the ablation in Section 5.4.4 confirms. 

• Section 4.4: frequentist selection consistency for a fixed truth requires a uniform amortization assumption that the training loss cannot certify. Every numbered result is proved analytically in Appendix B. A few published results are used as tools and are marked cited where they enter. Numerical computations appear only as illustrations, in paragraphs labelled as such, and are never a step of a proof. Results that rest on assumptions we do not verify state those assumptions explicitly. 

Status of the assumptions. 

• Knockoff mode (Theorem 1) assumes only exact model-X knockoffs, the standard requirement of Cand` es et al. (2018); it assumes nothing about the network, the meta-prior or training. 

• The regret bound (Proposition 2) needs no assumption for (i)–(ii); (iii) adds a standard margin condition. 

• The detection theory uses Gaussian noise with known variance and a fixed covariate law (Assumption 2), the usual setting of posterior-contraction theory; the likelihood factorizes, so the covariate law cancels from the posterior of the regression function (it enters only through the definitions of the effect of a variable and of the norm); for a random covariate law this requires the regression function to be a priori independent of it. The conditions linking labels to functions hold by construction of the meta-prior, and the sieve condition is verified for most of its families. The one high-level condition is Assumption 3, a Bayes-factor bound on spurious structures. It is used only for the lower bound in terms of the informative weight: the upper bound (Theorem 2(i)) does not use it, and with the trivial partition, which counts every structure that uses a variable as informative, Assumption 3 holds trivially, so the lower bound of Corollary 1(i) holds in terms of the total prior weight of structures that use the variable; the sharper constant of Theorem 2(ii) additionally needs a clipped-gap hypothesis for that class. 12 • Consistency composes Bayesian selection consistency (Assumption 4), known for several pri-ors, with uniform amortization (Assumption 5). The latter is not a convenience: Proposition 5 shows that it cannot be derived from the training loss, and identifying it is a result of this section.

Related theory. Nagler (2023) shows that the bias of a PFN need not vanish without locality. Melnychuk et al. (2026) show that PFN treatment-effect posteriors can be frequentist-inconsistent. # 5 Simulation studies 

We evaluate SelectPFN in simulations that address four questions: how accurately it selects vari-ables in nonlinear models that it never saw during training, how closely its amortized inclusion probabilities track the exact Bayesian posterior, whether restricting prediction to the selected vari-ables costs accuracy, and whether knockoff mode controls the false discovery rate. Section 6 then turns to real covariate matrices and real responses. 

5.1 Simulation setup 

SelectPFN models. SelectPFN is trained on the meta-prior of Section 3.3 (Table 14) at two sizes: SelectPFN , with d = 128, 6 layers and 1 .9M parameters, trained for 80k steps (4.9 GPU-hours on one RTX 4090), and SelectPFN-L , with d = 256, 8 layers and 9 .5M parameters, trained for 120k steps (17.8 GPU-hours). 5.2 Evaluation metrics 

• Ranking accuracy: the area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC) of the variable scores against S0, our primary metric because active variables are few, and the area under the ROC curve (AUROC). 

• Selected set: the true positive rate (TPR), the false discovery proportion (FDP), and exact recovery, 1{ ˆS = S0}. The selected set of SelectPFN is the median probability model (MPM), ˆS = {j : qj > 12 }; the other methods use their default or cross-validated rule. 

• Posterior accuracy: the mean absolute difference |qj − πj | between amortized and exact PIPs, the agreement of their MPMs, and the realized Bayes regret of Proposition 2; for calibration, the expected calibration error (ECE) and the largest deviation of the reliability curve. 

• Prediction: the held-out RMSE, reported as a ratio to TabPFN fitted on all columns. 

• Knockoff mode: the empirical FDR and power of knockoff+ at levels αFDR ∈ { 0.1, 0.2}.

• Computation: wall-clock time per data set. All intervals are 95% percentile bootstrap intervals over data sets, with 2,000 bootstrap resamples. 5.4.4 Ablation: what the meta-prior must contain 

Theorem 2 predicts that a variable can be detected only if the meta-prior gives weight to the kind of structure through which it acts. We test this prediction with two ablations at d = 128. 

Removing the interaction-only family. We trained SelectPFN on a meta-prior without the interaction-only family and the weak-effect regime, in which every interacting variable of the pair-wise family also has a main effect and the remaining families are reweighted (Table 14). 5.4.6 Prediction from the selected variables

5.4.8 Computation time and robustness to training randomness 

SelectPFN needs no fitting, tuning or convergence diagnostics for a new data set. # 7 Discussion 

SelectPFN shows that variable selection can be amortized for a broad class of sparse nonlinear regression models. It does so without giving up the two properties that distinguish integrated selection from post-hoc importance: predictions that provably use only the selected variables, and, in knockoff mode, exact finite-sample FDR control that does not depend on the meta-prior. On out-of-prior designs it recovers active sets markedly better than penalized, Bayesian-tree and sparse-neural-network methods, at a small fraction of their cost. 

What the theory says about the prior. Theorem 2 turns a modelling intuition into a quanti-tative statement. A variable is detected once its information index exceeds about (2 /λ j ) log(1 /ω ), where ω is the prior weight of structures that can use the variable as the truth does. Breadth of the meta-prior therefore matters, and it matters logarithmically: a structure the prior never generates (ω = 0) is essentially never found, whatever the sample size. Raising its weight from negligible to moderate shifts the detection threshold by a computable amount. Our ablations confirm this mechanism: without interaction-only structures in the meta-prior, variables that act only through an interaction are essentially never found, and in a controlled sweep of their weight detection rises monotonically with the weight, at no cost in false selections (Section 5.4.4). For a trained network the effect is even stronger than for the exact posterior, because rarely generated structures are also learned less well. It also gives a principled way to decide which families a foundation prior for selection must contain. 32 What amortization cannot give for free. Training minimizes an average over the prior. Proposition 5 shows that such average-case guarantees do not transfer to a fixed truth, so frequentist selection consistency of an amortized posterior requires a uniform-amortization assumption that training does not certify. Knockoff mode sidesteps this for error control. For power and calibration at a fixed truth, the assumption remains the main open problem. 

Limitations. 

• Exactness. The median probability model agrees exactly with that of an enumerable poste-rior on 0 .846 of the tasks for the larger model; the remaining disagreements concentrate at near-ties, which cost little Bayes risk (Proposition 2), and agreement improves steadily with training compute (Section 5.4.5). 

• Factorized selection head. Model averaging samples subsets from the product of the marginal PIPs and ignores posterior dependence between inclusions. An autoregressive selection head would remove this. 

• Scale. All experiments use p ≤ 500. The block-wise scheme of Section 3.9 is implemented but not validated beyond that. 

• Prior coverage. Like any pretrained model, SelectPFN reflects what its meta-prior contains. Dense signals, such as spectra, are not generated by the current prior (Section 6); by Theo-rem 2, adding dense and low-rank families is the way to cover them. 

• Baselines. PACE has no public code. LinearPFN supports only p ≤ 30 and is compared on a p = 20 slice. The spike-and-slab sparse neural network is our own implementation of Sun et al. (2022); with n ≤ 800 it selects conservatively, and a tuned implementation by its authors could do better. 

• Prediction at the larger size. The larger model (width d = 256 with 8 layers) improves selection but regresses in prediction on one design. About half of the regression comes from extrapolation to extreme responses, and the rest is unexplained (Section 5.4.6). We use the smaller model for prediction. 

Extensions. Binary and time-to-event responses require only a different likelihood in the meta-prior and a different prediction head. Test-point-specific selection and group-level inclusion for correlated blocks are natural next targets. On the theory side, the remaining open problems are spurious-inclusion control (assumption Assumption 3) for nonparametric priors, an explicit second-order term in the detection threshold, and conditions under which training provably achieves uniform amortization.

Relation to Assumption 1. The generator departs from Assumption 1 in minor ways. The function f is rescaled to unit variance and its effect sizes are checked on reference rows drawn from the same task distribution PX , so f depends on the task-level parameters of PX (but not on the observed rows), and columns are standardized jointly over the observed and reference rows. The noise standard deviation is set from the sample variance of f over the rows of the task and the drawn SNR, so the rows are not exactly conditionally independent given the covariates; since this variance depends on the covariates only through the active columns, the likelihood still depends on the data only through DS and x∗,S , and the conclusion of Lemma 1 is unaffected. In addition, with label relabelling after freezing switched off (the default), heteroscedastic noise can depend on a variable that was frozen and labelled inactive, and a variable whose only interaction partner was frozen remains labelled active. They affect only the training targets, not the knockoff guarantee of Section 3.8, which holds for any network weights. 

Exactness tests. Unit tests verify eq. (4) (the prediction is bit-identical when excluded columns are changed), invariance to padding, and column-permutation equivariance of q.

Reproducibility. Every number in Section 5 and this appendix is regenerated from the stored per-data-set results: 55 • manuscript/make tables.py regenerates the tables and inline numbers; 

• manuscript/make figures.py regenerates the figures; 

• experiments/report 1c.py produces the summary report. Data sets are generated deterministically: each one is seeded by a hash of its design, n, p, ρ, SNR and replicate index. Paired comparisons therefore use identical data for every method. 

Implementation choices that affect the comparisons. 

• TabPFN. TabPFN is ranked by permutation importance on a 30% split of the training data, and only on 10 replicates per setting, because KernelSHAP at p = 500 is infeasible for thousands of data sets. TabPFN+SHAP (permutation SHAP with 2 p + 1 evaluations per explained row, 20 background rows and 30 explained rows) is run only on the p = 20 slice. 

• DART. DART is BART::wbart with sparse=TRUE . Its PIP is the fraction of posterior draws in which the variable is used; variables are ranked by this PIP, with ties broken by the posterior splitting probability. 

• GP-ARD. GP-ARD ranks variables by inverse length-scale. 

• HSIC-Lasso. HSIC-Lasso uses 30,000 subsampled kernel pairs and ranks variables only. 

• LassoNet. LassoNet ranks variables by the penalty level at which each one leaves the regular-ization path. Variables that never leave the path before it stops are ranked by the magnitude of their skip-connection weights. An earlier version left them tied at + ∞; those results were discarded. 

• Sparse BNN. The sparse Bayesian network † is fitted by MAP with an annealed spike variance and pruned at a weight inclusion probability of 12 . The prior inclusion probability is chosen from {0.5, 10 −1, 10 −2, 10 −4, 10 −6, 10 −8} on a 20% validation split. 

• Group-MCP network. The group-MCP network † is a simplified version of GCRNet (Luo and Halabi, 2025) (proximal gradient over a 10-value penalty path, no ridge tuning); it selects its penalty on a 20% validation split. 

• BART. BART is BART::wbart with sparse=FALSE , which fixes the splitting probabilities at 1/p . Variables are ranked by their average share of splits (Bleich et al., 2014), and the selected set is {j : PIP j > 12 }. An earlier version ranked by the fixed splitting probabilities, which tie across variables; those results were discarded.
