# ABSTRACT 

Prior-data fitted networks (PFNs) are transformers pretrained on simulated datasets to approximate Bayesian posterior predictive distributions. Standard PFN theory assumes a separable task prior, under which the feature distribution and the mechanism generating labels from features are independent. We show that com-mon PFN priors are non-separable, even in simple settings. Consequently, stan-dard pretraining targets a predictive distribution based on a task posterior updated using both the labeled context and the query features. We characterize its differ-ence from the predictive based on the context posterior alone and show that this difference vanishes under posterior concentration. Under misspecification (i.e., when the true joint distribution lies outside the prior’s support), however, prior non-separability can favor better feature fit at the expense of conditional label fit. We illustrate the consequences for feature attribution, Bayesian optimization, and martingale checks of Bayesian behavior. The same dependence also enables PFNs to learn from additional unlabeled observations. We demonstrate this with SemiTabICL, a PFN explicitly pretrained to use these observations, which im-proves accuracy over a matched supervised baseline. Our findings highlight the need for explicit rules on unlabeled data use in PFN evaluation and establish the dependence between feature and label distributions as a consequential prior design choice. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Tabular foundation models such as TabPFN and TabICL are prior-data fitted networks (PFNs; Grin-sztajn et al., 2025; 2026; Hollmann et al., 2023; 2025; Qu et al., 2025; 2026). A PFN is an approx-imator to a family of posterior predictive distributions (PPDs; Nagler, 2023), usually implemented as a transformer with parameters θ and pretrained on simulated datasets drawn from a prior Π over tasks. A common data prior choice are structural causal models (SCMs). We use uppercase letters for random data and lowercase letters for their realizations. At inference, the PFN receives an ob-served context dn = {yi, xi}ni=1 and a query feature vector x and returns a predictive distribution 

qθ (y | x, d n) in its forward pass. For classification, PFNs are pretrained by cross-entropy on sim-ulated contexts and query pairs drawn from the same task. The target is therefore the conditional distribution of the query label given its feature vector and the context (Nagler, 2023, Theorem 2.2). A trained PFN approximates this target with finite capacity and a finite pretraining budget. While previous research on PFNs has asked how accurately a trained network approximates its target PPD, we ask a prior question: Which predictive distribution does pretraining actually target? As outlined below, our motivation stems from the (often implicitly made) assumption of separability of the data prior, which has so far received too little attention.

Our contributions By studying the difference of predictives in Eq. (1) through various lenses, we make the following contributions: • We prove that prior non-separability already arises in simple SCMs and that mixing (separable) priors, as in TabPFN, TabICL, and Mitra, preserves separability if and only if the regimes share the same prior over feature distributions or the same prior over conditional label distributions. • We characterize a predictive gap between π and ¯π at finite context sizes and show that it vanishes for typical queries under posterior concentration. Under misspecification, their common limit can nevertheless have an arbitrarily large optimality gap relative to the best supported conditional label law. • We study the practical consequences for downstream use and evaluation of PFNs, including interpretability, Bayesian optimization, semi-supervised prediction, and martingale checks. pX (x) of the query. 2.1 SCM PRIORS ARE NON -SEPARABLE 

In an SCM prior, a random graph, random node functions, and a random choice of the response node generate the features and the label jointly. The feature distribution and the conditional label distribution are then two functionals of the same random mechanism, and thus we should not expect separability to hold for these priors. In fact, it already fails in simple cases like a linear Gaussian SCM with a random label node (see Proposition A.2). Mixing multiple generator regimes provides another potential source of non-separability. The origi-nal TabPFN prior mixes SCM and Bayesian neural network datasets equally (Hollmann et al., 2023). TabICL mixes neural and tree-based SCM datasets in a 70 /30 ratio (Qu et al., 2025). Mitra mixes SCM and tree-based priors (Zhang et al., 2025b), and Mitra-v2 adds a hybrid SCM to this outer mix-ture (Tao et al., 2026). TabICLv2 also combines neural, tree, Gaussian-process, and other functions within individual SCMs (Qu et al., 2026). Even if the feature and label laws of these priors were separable within each regime, their mixture is non-separable when both induced laws differ across regimes, as the following result shows (proof in Appendix A.2.1). 

Proposition 1. Let ΠX,s and ΠY |X,s be priors on the induced feature and conditional label laws for s ∈ { 0, 1}. For λ ∈ (0 , 1) , set 

Π = (1 − λ) Π X, 0 ⊗ ΠY |X, 0 + λ ΠX, 1 ⊗ ΠY |X, 1.

Then Π is separable if and only if ΠX, 0 = Π X, 1 or ΠY |X, 0 = Π Y |X, 1.

Practically, at finite context sizes, the query can therefore update beliefs about the labeling mechanism, producing a predictive gap between what the PFN commu-nity reports to target and the query-updated predictive shown to be the actual target (Proposition A.1 and Eq. (2)). Under posterior concentration, this correction vanishes, and both predictives approach the same conditional distribution (Proposition 2). Both gaps can be seen as a separability gap , causing (potentially arbitrarily large) differences between the predictives under non-separable priors. For PFN design, the prior’s feature generator therefore affects the predictions themselves. A PFN pretrained on an SCM prior infers the labeling mechanism partly from the query’s feature values, and it can use additional unlabeled rows the same way (as demonstrated in the following Section 3). Whether this improves or degrades predictive performance on real data depends on whether the prior couples feature and label laws as the data do. 

# 3 CONSEQUENCES OF NON -SEPARABLE PRIORS 

We have shown that under a prior with coupled feature and label laws, the query can update the posterior over the labeling mechanism. This has many important consequences. 

Interpretability methods Feature effect methods for model interpretability are also applicable to PFNs, where batched inference can bring down the cost of repeated model evaluations (Rundel et al., 2024). Many interpretability methods, such as partial dependence (PD) and individual conditional expectation (ICE) plots vary selected features while averaging over, or fixing, the remaining coordi-nates (Goldstein et al., 2015). For a PFN approximating π, varying x changes both p(y | x) and the posterior Π( · | x, d n) over tasks (Eq. (2)). The resulting curve therefore combines variation within labeling mechanisms with evidence about which mechanism generated the query. For example, if every task has a constant class probability cp := p(1 | x), but cp is associated with the task’s feature distribution, then π(1 | x, d n) can vary with x even though ¯π(1 | x, d n) is constant, as illustrated in Figure 3. This effect can arise within the data support, in addition to the familiar extrapolation problem of PD plots (Apley & Zhu, 2020). The marginalization convention of SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP; Lundberg & Lee, 2017) does not remove it either. Conditional SHAP aver-ages missing coordinates under their distribution given the retained coordinates, whereas marginal 6Preprint SHAP averages under their unconditional distribution (Molnar, 2025). Both still evaluate the same query-updated predictor at each completed input. Thus, these methods can faithfully explain the PFN’s predictions while attributing importance to features that identify the task rather than change its conditional label distribution.

In either case, a PFN trained on a non-separable prior does not provide the predictive needed for BO.

PFNs as semi-supervised predictors By Corollary A.1, PFNs pretrained on non-separable pri-ors are already semi-supervised predictors: π uses the unlabeled query x as evidence about the labeling mechanism. While they are currently limited to only use the additional evidence in the single query row, there is no structural reason not to integrate further unlabeled rows V into the prediction, turning PFNs into full semi-supervised predictors. Sch¨ olkopf et al. (2012) argue that this addition of unlabeled data should help especially in anti-causal and confounded tasks, which SCM priors already generate. Hence, integrating such unlabeled rows V into existing training pipelines and architectures should prove beneficial, which we show for both the ordered-cluster prior (see Appendix C.1) and the full-sized production prior of TabICLv2. As such, we pretrained a novel 8.5M-parameter TabICL variant, SemiTabICL , to not just use the unlabeled information in the query x, but instead also explicitly allow for the incorporation of an additional set of unlabeled context rows V which are processed exactly as regular context rows, only with an additional learned embedding vector instead of the label embedding (see Appendix C.3). Following Guo et al. (2010), we evaluate SemiTabICL in the standard setting of semi-supervised learning with a low number of labeled training points relative to a large number of unlabeled training points. To this end, we reuse the TALENT benchmark (Liu et al., 2025) and convert it into a benchmark for semi-supervised classification, that we call semi-TALENT , by splitting the official training fold of the classification datasets into labeled partitions of n ∈ { 16 , 32 , 64 , 96 } rows and unlabeled partitions across multiple repetitions. We compare SemiTabICL to a baseline TabICL model pretrained without additional unlabeled context rows, as well as a self-training PFN variant where unlabeled rows predicted with high confidence are iteratively appended with their predicted labels before the prediction on the test set is made (Yarowsky, 1995). Figure 5 shows the differences in accuracy for these two settings across a range of labeled training rows n and the multiples thereof added as additional unlabeled rows k. SemiTabICL consistently improves upon the baseline by 0.4–1.1 percentage points, while the self-training hurts performance in almost every setting, increasingly so with more labeled rows. # 4 DISCUSSION 

This article has highlighted a subtle but important discrepancy between the typical formulation of the posterior predictive distribution in the PFN literature and the actual target of PFN pretraining under non-separable priors. We further showed that this discrepancy has several practical implica-tions for interpretability, Bayesian optimization, martingale checks, and semi-supervised learning. Depending on the application, these effects can be unwanted or beneficial. We argue that the distinc-tion between the query-updated predictive π and the context-updated predictive ¯π is important when designing PFN pretraining and evaluation protocols, especially in the presence of non-separable pri-ors. Although this difference vanishes under the stated concentration conditions, coupling feature and label distributions can still sacrifice label fit for feature fit under misspecification. Our experi-ments confirm recovery of the query-updated target and show that explicitly incorporating additional unlabeled rows yields predictive gains with SemiTabICL on real tabular data. Separable priors avoid the issues discussed in this work, but also forgo potential gains from using un-labeled features. Our analysis already points to useful design principles for such priors. SCM priors can remain the basis for generative modeling of tabular data, but two independent SCM mechanisms should be drawn, one for generating the features and one for generating the label. One could also pretrain on a range of coupling strengths between the mechanisms. These findings make the depen-dence between feature and label laws a concrete design choice for PFNs and a promising direction for future work. 

Limitations The main limitation of this work is that we mainly establish that non-separability 

can have the issues discussed in this work. We do not yet know whether production-grade PFNs benefit or suffer from it in practice, and to what extent. Studying this is challenging, since available PFNs use diverse pretraining priors and many are not open-source. Further, we can only compare to meaningful comparator models after full-scale retraining of the model under a separable sibling of its prior. so the predictive gap ∆π (Corollary 1) is added on top of the approximation error, and any divergence or loss difference computed against ¯π mixes the two. On real data, labels do not follow π, so even with the correct reference the loss difference is no longer a divergence and can be negative (cf. H¨ ulskamp et al., 2026). 

Counterexample for martingale zero-drift under non-separable priors Consider two equally likely tasks on X ∈ { 0, 1, 2} with feature probabilities (0 .5, 0.4, 0.1) and (0 .5, 0.1, 0.4) , and class 1 probabilities at X = 0 of 0.2 and 0.8, respectively. At X = 1 , 2, let both tasks assign class 1 probability 0.5. This prior is non-separable, but fixing the query and all future features at zero yields a martingale: their common feature likelihood 0.5 cancels from every update, leaving ordinary Bayesian label updates. Non-separability therefore permits drift without requiring it at every query. The two terms can also cancel while both are nonzero. With no initial context, take two equally likely tasks on X ∈ { 0, 1} with pX (0) equal to 0.8 and 0.2, and deterministic labels Y = 1 and 

Y = 0 , respectively. At query and future feature x = 0 , the initial class 1 probability is 4/5

and conditioning on both feature rows gives 16 /17 . The sampled label identifies the task, so the feature-update and sampling terms are 12 /85 and −12 /85 : the net drift is zero. 

Transductive channels in current benchmarks and models Currently, the handling of test data differs substantially between different benchmark suites as well as between different models. Below we summarize important aspects with regard to possible transductive channels in some of the most popular benchmarks and models contained in them: • TabArena (Erickson et al., 2026) passes the full test fold to the model. It has no written rule on transductive use. Its only check is a smoke test that compares the prediction for a single row alone and within the batch, and methods can be exempted from it. • TALENT (Liu et al., 2025) processes test minibatches in its neural-model evaluation rou-tine, so a prediction can depend on the composition of the batch. • ScoringBench (Landsgesell et al., 2026) passes all test features jointly to its predictive-distribution interface. • BeyondArena (Purucker et al., 2026) explicitly documents transductive preprocessing for label-per-group tasks. It computes feature summaries within each test group and attaches them to that group’s rows (Purucker et al., 2026, Appendix C.2). This reflects tasks whose prediction unit is naturally a group and does not by itself make comparisons unfair. 19 Preprint On the model side, the commonly used attention masks, for example in TabPFN and TabICL, prevent direct attention between test rows. TabICL also excludes test rows from the shared context of its column embedding by default and includes them only with embed with test=True . While these restrictions blocks large interactions, they do not make the complete pipeline independent of the other test rows: • The standard forward path of TabPFN-v2.5 computes constant-feature masks and feature-group normalization parameters from the combined training and test input. • TALENT’s bundled LimiX implementation (Zhang et al., 2025a) performs feature filtering, categorical-type inference, and ordinal encoding on the combined training and test features. • TALENT’s default SAINT configuration enables intersample attention, so its predictions explicitly depend on the other rows in the test minibatch (Somepalli et al., 2021). • Proprietary models served through an API, such as TabPFN-Thinking, receive the whole test fold server-side and can only be probed as a black box for batch invariance. In total, an evaluation protocol should specify the prediction unit, which unlabeled observations are available and when, and how each method uses them, including any dependence on the composition of the test batch. Per-row prediction calls enforce invariance only if preprocessing and retained state have not already used other test rows. When transductive inference is permitted, all methods should receive the same information, although they need not exploit it identically.

Prior We introduce the ordered-cluster prior that lets us study our theoretical claims empirically, where we pretrain TabICL models from scratch on this custom prior. It is a finite non-separable classification prior over task distributions pa,b , where the centers a < b are chosen uniformly from the 36 ordered pairs on the grid {− 4, . . . , 4}. For each row, the class is drawn uniformly, with class 0 generating features from N (a, 0.52) and class 1 from N (b, 0.52). Because the same cluster centers determine both the feature distribution and the labeling mechanism, an unlabeled query feature can provide information about its label. More precisely, conditioning on a query x changes the label prediction exactly when its likelihood under the posterior tasks is correlated with their class probability at x (see Eq. (2)). This effect is particularly pronounced when the context contains only one class. In this case, the corresponding center is observed, while the other remains uncertain, so the query location helps identify the missing center. This is also the effect illustrated in Fig. 1. The finite task space makes both the query-updated predictive π and the context-updated predictive ¯π

exactly enumerable, providing a controlled setting in which to determine which predictive standard pretraining recovers. Architecture SemiTabICL uses the three-stage TabICL architecture with a column embedding (2 blocks, embedding dimension 128, 128 inducing points), a row interaction (2 blocks, 3 CLS tokens) and an in-context transformer (6 blocks, width 3 · 128 = 384 ), each with 8 attention heads, for 8.5M parameters in total. Every dataset is laid out as [ dn | v | queries ] with n labeled, k unlabeled and m

query rows. Wherever TabICL attends to the training rows, i.e. in the inducing-point attention of the column embedding and in the in-context transformer, the attention context consists of the first n + k

rows, so labeled and unlabeled rows attend to each other and queries attend to both. Unlabeled rows carry a single learned vector in place of the label embedding. The loss is the cross-entropy on the query rows only. The baseline TabICL is identical except for this vector and never sees unlabeled rows. 

Prior Both models are trained on the unchanged graph-SCM prior of TabICLv2 (Qu et al., 2026) (random DAGs with 2–32 nodes, 1–100 features, up to 10 classes, predictability filters). The unla-beled rows are further samples of the same SCM whose labels are dropped. For SemiTabICL, every dataset has such a pool: the labeled rows make up 30% of a sequence length drawn log-uniformly from [14 , 334] , so n ∈ [4 , 100] and m ≈ 2.3 n, and the pool has k = round( r n ) rows with r

log-uniform on [1 , 9] . The number of classes is capped at max(2 , ⌊min( n, m )/2⌋). The median synthetic dataset has n = 20 , k = 59 and m = 47 rows. The baseline is trained on the same distribution of (n, m ) without a pool. 

Training We run pretraining for 30 000 optimizer steps, 1% warmup, with bfloat16 precision, and batches of 64 datasets in groups of 16 that share their sequence length and pool size. Each model trains in about two hours on four NVIDIA H200 GPUs.

# D RELATED WORK 

Semi-supervised learning (in ICL) Sch¨ olkopf et al. (2012) point out the importance of analyzing the causal mechanisms underlying datasets for supervised learning and highlight under what con-ditions additional unlabeled data should prove useful with semi-supervised approaches. Li et al. (2026) show, from an expressivity perspective, that linear attention needs depth to exploit unlabeled in-context rows under a binary Gaussian-mixture prior, which is a special case of what we call a non-separable prior. Though they mention TabPFN as an application, the question studied is or-thogonal to ours: we ask whether a network pretrained on a non-separable prior natively exploits additional unlabeled rows, as its objective says it should, whereas they wrap an off-the-shelf TabPFN in an external pseudo-labeling loop and show that self-training can improve predictive performance. Swelam et al. (2025) study whether the internal representations of TabPFN contain causal informa-tion and how well these can be repurposed for causal discovery by training an additional decoder. Their positive findings provide adjacent evidence that predictive pretraining yields feature repre-sentations that not only identify the latent mechanism p(y | x) but also p(x) as required for the query-updated predictive of Eq. (4). 

Bayesian targets and direct reference comparisons. M¨ uller et al. (2022) introduce PFNs and show that prior-data NLL equals the expected KL to the PPD up to the Bayes-optimal loss, so held-out NLL measures the amortization gap whenever that loss is known, which is measured for a GP with fixed hyperparameters and a Gaussian target whose mean is linear in the context labels. In these settings the PFN approaches the exact NLL with more pretraining. Furthermore, M¨ uller et al. (2024) extends the analysis by enumerating finite libraries of one-dimensional step, sine, and linear 23 Preprint functions and show on selected datasets that predictive means follow the exact posterior, including non-obvious generalizations. This setup also separates failures of the posterior from failures of the network, such as an inability to count repeated observations. For intractable priors, the existing com-parisons rank a PFN against NUTS, variational inference, or MLE-II (M¨ uller et al., 2022), primarily designed as speed comparisons and are very informative as such, but a Monte Carlo reference whose NLL never falls below the PFN’s cannot locate the Bayes-optimal loss, so the gap under a general prior has not been measured. The later literature has largely turned to predictive performance on real benchmarks (Grinsztajn et al., 2025; Hollmann et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2025; 2026), which establishes that PFNs predict well but not what they compute. 

Neural processes Neural processes (NPs) share the PFN goal of learning to predict from a con-text set across a distribution of tasks. In particular, PFNs are closely related to transformer NPs, a transformer-based extension of conditional NPs (Nagler, 2023; Nguyen & Grover, 2022). Con-ditional NPs directly parameterize predictive distributions (Garnelo et al., 2018a), while latent NPs introduce a shared latent variable to model dependencies between predictions (Garnelo et al., 2018b). Attentive neural processes use query-specific context representations (Kim et al., 2019), and trans-former neural processes formulate prediction through likelihood-based sequence modeling (Nguyen & Grover, 2022). Closely related to our target-identification question, Foong et al. (2020) show, un-der their stated conditions, that the population maximum-likelihood objective recovers the ground-truth stochastic process’s prediction map. This supports interpreting learned predictions as approx-imations to conditional distributions, without guaranteeing exact Bayesian inference for a finite trained network. 24
