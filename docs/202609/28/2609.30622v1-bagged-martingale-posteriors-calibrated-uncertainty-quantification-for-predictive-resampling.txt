# Bagged Martingale Posteriors: Calibrated Uncertainty Quantification for Predictive Resampling ∗

## Hui Wang 1, Edwin Fong 2, and David T. Frazier 11Department of Econometrics and Business Statistics, Monash University

Abstract 

Martingale posteriors and related predictive resampling methods replace the likeli-hood–prior pair used within Bayesian inference with a predictive model for future observations. These methods are simple to implement and increasingly popular due to their computational efficiency, but little is known about their ability to accurately quantify uncertainty. In this work, we study the concentration and calibration prop-erties of the martingale posterior for general functionals, and show that credible sets can systematically undercover if the predictive algorithms are not carefully tuned. We propose a simple remedy: the bagged martingale posterior. Rather than starting every predictive path from the observed sample, we start the paths from random boot-strap resamples of the data and then amalgamate the resulting draws. Critically, this approach incurs no additional simulation cost compared to standard predictive re-sampling, and delivers conservatively calibrated credible sets. This scalability enables application to a range of challenging examples, including sparse high-dimensional re-gression and nonparametric conditional quantile models. 

## Keywords : Bagging; bootstrap; calibration; martingale posterior; model misspecification; predictive inference

In complex problems, specifying such a likelihood–prior pair may be difficult or unnatural. Predictive Bayesian inference (PBI; see, e.g., Berti et al., 2021; Fortini and Petrone, 2023, 2025) and martingale posteriors (MGPs; Fong et al., 2023; Holmes and Walker, 2023) dispense with this requirement: they replace the likelihood–prior pair by a predictive model, or algorithm, for future observations given past ones, from which draws can be cheaply simulated. Given a predictive model F(Xn+1 ∈ · | x1: n) and a link between future data and the parameter of interest, draws from the MGP for θ | x1: n are obtained directly by predictive resampling (Section 2). These draws require no Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), and are therefore available in settings where MCMC samplers fail without considerable care and expertise and usually require a fraction of the computational cost. This ease of implementa-tion has produced a rapidly growing body of applications of PBI, from quantile estimation (Fong and Yiu, 2026b) to neural processes (Lee et al., 2023) and tabular foundation models (Ng et al., 2025). This growth in popularity demands a stronger theoretical understand-ing of concentration and calibration properties of general PBI methodology. Critically, we show in Section 2.2.2 that the MGP’s ability to correctly quantify uncertainty is strongly 2susceptible to predictive misspecification: if the model does not faithfully reproduce future observed data, measures of uncertainty obtained from the MGP need not be calibrated in the frequentist sense. To illustrate this fact, Figure 1 (top panel) plots the parametric MGP of Fong and Yiu (2026a) for an unknown regression parameter of interest under various degrees of model misspecification, represented on the x-axis; complete details of this example are given in Section 4.1. Even under mild levels of misspecification, the MGP is highly susceptible to mismatches between the assumed predictive model and the observed data and exhibits poor uncertainty quantification. We confirm this finding theoretically in Section 2.2.2, and show in Section 4.1 that the actual coverage of the 95% MGP credible set in this example is 78.5%. Motivated by this issue, we propose a modification of predictive resampling that re-pairs this failure at no additional simulation cost. Like a number of recent proposals that loosen the likelihood–prior paradigm (see, e.g., Bissiri et al., 2016), this approach com-bines frequentist and Bayesian devices: instead of running every predictive path on the same data set, we run paths based on bootstrap resamples (Efron, 1979) of the data, and amalgamate the resulting posterior draws. (2025). ## 2.2 Concentration and Calibration of the MGP 

Several authors have analyzed conditions to ensure that Π ∞ exists, and to ensure that ΠN is an accurate approximation of Π ∞, see, e.g., Fong et al. (2023) and Battiston and Cappello (2025). Verifying such conditions requires example-specific analysis that depends on the chosen predictive model class Fn:n+1 and on the behavior of T (·). We do not pursue that analysis here, and throughout the remainder assume that Π ∞ exists directly. 7While it is almost trivial to draw samples from Π N , little is known in general about the ability of Π N to accurately produce inferences on the true population functional of interest 

ϑ0 := T (P0). In this section, we answer this question in the negative: in general ϑ ∼ ΠN

may not necessarily concentrate around ϑ0 := T (P0). More generally, even when the MGP concentrates on ϑ0, using Π N to quantify uncertainty about ϑ0 may not deliver calibrated inference in general. 2

Before showing that Π N may not deliver calibrated inferences on ϑ0, we first give a gen-eral posterior concentration result for Π N that is of independent interest. We are unaware of any such general concentration result for PBI methods. This concentration result then allows us to establish the inability of Π N to deliver calibrated inferences in general. The posterior Π N (· | x1: n) averages over the simulated path, so it is a function of the observed data alone, and the result below is a statement about x1: n in the usual sense of posterior contraction. A property holds with P0-probability converging to one if the P0-probability of the set of data sequences on which it holds tends to one.

# 3 Bagged MGPs 

For the MGP, uncertainty quantification for the population value ϑ0 = T (P0) is induced by the imputation of future observations according to a predictive engine. While the predictive engine may be estimated from observed data, it is generally not the case that the predictive engine will learn a sufficiently accurate estimate of the data-generating process from a finite sample, for example if the predictive model is misspecified. Furthermore, we have illustrated in the previous section that the calibration of the MGP depends delicately on this predictive engine. In this section, we propose a direct remedy to this issue.

The additional condition in Corollary 1 is required since, in contrast to the classical Bernstein–von Mises theorem, see, e.g., Kleijn and van der Vaart (2012), there is no uni-versally acceptable centering sequence for MGPs: nothing restricts the form of ¯ξn other 18 than the choice of predictive engine. That is, while our maintained assumptions control the fluctuation of the simulated path about this center, which is what Σ ⋆ measures, they do not determine the sampling law of ¯ξn. Theorem 3 adds a condition on the bootstrap law of the center, Assumption 7; Assumption 8 is its sampling counterpart, and Appendix C.3 shows that the two hold together whenever the center is a smooth functional of the empirical measure. Indeed, as discussed in Fong and Yiu (2026a), the choice of the cen-tering sequence need not agree with the limiting form of the MGP, so that the centering sequence and the algorithm must generally be based on different assumptions; see Sections 4.3 and 4.4 of Fong and Yiu (2026a) for additional discussion on this point. Theorem 3 demonstrates that the bMGP delivers conservatively calibrated inferences on ϑ⋆, in the sense of Corollary 1, and at the same forward-simulation budget as the original MGP, at least for the choices of B and MB that equate the two simulation budgets. # 5 Discussion 

Predictive Bayesian inference methods, such as the martingale posterior (MGP), replace the likelihood and prior with a model for future data. The key question asked in this paper is whether the posterior that results from this substitution produces inferences that reliably quantify uncertainty. We show that the MGP concentrates onto an appropriately defined pseudo-true value but that the resulting credible sets can be severely over-confident. To solve this issue we propose the bagged MGP (bMGP), which has credible sets that are guaranteed to deliver conservatively calibrated inferences. The bMGP belongs to a family of procedures that merge frequentist and Bayesian ideas 31 in order to deliver more reliable uncertainty quantification. Critically, unlike related meth-ods such as BayesBag (Huggins and Miller, 2024), and the variational bagging approach of Fan et al. (2025), the bMGP carries the same forward-simulation budget as the MGP. Its additional costs are the B initializations and the overhead of running the recursion separately for each bagged data set, both quantified in Appendix A.3.1. This unique fea-ture of the bMGP ensures that it can be applied to deliver reliable Bayesian inference in high-dimensional and highly nonlinear problems where other Bayesian methods would be too computationally onerous. The bMGP delivers conservatively calibrated inferences. As such, the bMGP credible sets are larger than necessary when the predictive engine is correctly specified. In addition, we note that the results obtained herein require technical conditions that may be difficult to verify outside of simple predictive engines, which of course does not invalidate the practical usefulness of the bMGP.

A.3.1 Computational Cost of Bagging 

Table 9 reports the wall-clock cost of one replication of the spike-and-slab experiment of Section 4.1.1 at n = 250 and p = 200, for the four procedures compared in Table 2, all based on spike-and-slab initialization. The Bayesian posterior is fitted by the Gibbs sampler of George and McCulloch (1993) for the continuous spike-and-slab prior that the working model already uses, with the chain length of Sun and Fong (2026): 10 ,000 burn-in iterations followed by 25 ,000 kept iterations, thinned to 5000. Over the 25 ,000 kept iterations the smallest effective sample size among the active coefficients is 251 under the homoskedastic DGP and 118 under the heteroskedastic one, with medians over the active coefficients of 9205 and 847. BayesBag applied to this working model needs one such fit for each of the 

B = 50 bagged data sets, the same bagged data sets the bMGP uses, and the 50 fits were run rather than extrapolated. They take 23 .2 minutes, about 50 times the 28 .0 seconds of one bMGP replication. Scaled to the 160 data sets of Table 2, the comparison is about 62 hours for BayesBag against 1 .25 hours for the bMGP. Within a bMGP replication the 50 EM initializations account for 3 .6 seconds. The remainder is the predictive recursion, which the current implementation repeats once per bagged data set because each has its 46 own covariance state, so the bMGP takes about 35 times as long as the MGP although the two produce the same number of terminal draws. The Student-t spike-and-slab experiment of Section 4.1.2 gives the same picture: one Gibbs fit takes 43 seconds and the 50 bagged fits 36 minutes on the homoskedastic data set, against 5 .6 seconds for a bMGP replication on the same data set, whose predictive horizon is 300 steps. In the Gaussian ridge case every posterior is available in closed form and the ordering is reversed: one replication takes 0 .01 seconds for Bayes, 0 .09 seconds for BayesBag, 0 .75 seconds for the MGP and 25 seconds for the bMGP. Before rearrangement the update propagates the quantile curve as a martingale, so the un-rearranged terminal curve has conditional mean Qinit ; monotone rearrangement is 51 a contraction in L2 of the u-grid, so in that norm the perturbation is bounded by the path dispersion, which vanishes as the sample on which the engine is initialized grows. On the fixed grid, convergence in this norm implies convergence at every grid point, and the reported levels lie on the grid. What is not verified is that the initialization fitted to an oracle sample converges, as m grows, to a fixed curve; the oracle fit is therefore read as a numerical evaluation of Qq(F⋆), whose stability in m is checked directly below.
