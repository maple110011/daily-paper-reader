Abstract 

Deep neural networks (DNNs) have achieved remarkable empirical success, yet their training dynam-ics are still primarily understood through optimization perspectives rather than statistical principles. Here we establish a statistical framework for DNN training in the over-parameterized regime by show-ing that the prediction from a continuous-time neural tangent kernel (NTK) gradient flow is exactly equivalent to a classical random-effects model. In this framework, training time acts as a variance component, or equivalently an empirical Bayes covariance hyperparameter, that governs the alloca-tion of variance from noise to structured signal. This equivalence reveals an optimization–inference duality: the gradient-flow path is simultaneously an optimization trajectory and an empirical Bayes random-effects inference path. Conditional on this hyperparameter, the trained network output is the posterior mean of the latent signal; estimating it by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) turns early stopping into empirical Bayes inference rather than external tuning. This perspective yields a two-stage inferential procedure for DNN training. First, we propose a variance-component test to de-termine whether DNN training captures statistically significant structure beyond initialization. Second, conditional on training being warranted, we formulate early stopping as a likelihood-based estimation problem via REML. The resulting stopping rule admits a spectral interpretation in the NTK eigenba-sis, where training proceeds until spectral loss decorrelation is achieved. We further establish that the REML-guided stopping time achieves asymptotically optimal prediction error, both for fixed-design in-sample prediction and, under additional random-design regularity conditions, for out-of-sample pre-diction. This work reframes DNN training as a statistical inference problem and provides a principled foundation for deciding whether and how long to train deep neural networks. 

Keywords : Statistics for AI; deep neural networks; neural tangent kernel; random-effects models; empirical Bayes; REML; early stopping; prediction optimality. 1

# 1 Introduction 

Deep neural networks (DNNs) have achieved remarkable success across a wide range of scientific ap-plications (LeCun et al., 2015), including protein structure prediction (Jumper et al., 2021; Abramson et al., 2024), molecular design (Dauparas et al., 2022), and mathematical discovery (Alon et al., 2026; Castelvecchi, 2026). Despite this success, a principled statistical understanding of DNN training remains incomplete (Samek et al., 2021; Bartlett et al., 2021). Unlike classical statistical models, DNNs are highly nonlinear and trained through iterative optimization procedures, making it difficult to characterize how training extracts structured signal from data, how model complexity evolves during training, and how one should decide whether and how long to train (Goodfellow et al., 2016). As a result, DNNs are often regarded as black-box models with limited interpretability (Rudin, 2019). For broader statistical perspec-tives on deep learning, including approximation, optimization, over-parameterization, and generalization, see Fan et al. (2021). A substantial statistical literature has studied DNNs as a nonparametric regression and established guarantees for the final trained model (Schmidt-Hieber, 2020; Bauer and Kohler, 2019; Kohler and Langer, 2021; Farrell et al., 2021; Jiao et al., 2023). Our focus is different: we study the training trajectory itself and ask whether gradient-flow training admits a statistical inference interpretation. Existing theoretical advances have made it possible to analyze over-parameterized DNN training through function-space descriptions, most notably the neural tangent kernel (NTK) (Jacot et al., 2018). In the infinite-width regime, NTK theory shows that gradient-based training follows kernel gradient dy-namics and converges to kernel regression with respect to a deterministic kernel fixed at initialization (Jacot et al., 2018; Du et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2019; Arora et al., 2019). High-dimensional analyses of the lazy-training regime have further characterized interpolation thresholds, empirical NTK spectra, and the generalization error of neural tangent regression (Montanari and Zhong, 2022). These insights extend beyond fully connected networks, as NTK dynamics have been shown to exhibit architectural universality across modern deep learning models, including convolutional, recurrent, and attention-based architectures 2(Yang and Littwin, 2021). Although the NTK was originally introduced as an infinite-width limiting object (Jacot et al., 2018), a growing body of recent work suggests that NTK-based quantities can also serve as useful empirical diagnostics and modeling tools in modern AI systems beyond the idealized infinite-width regime. For example, Malladi et al. (2023) showed that prompt-based fine-tuning of pretrained transformer language models such as RoBERTa can exhibit kernel-like dynamics and admit accurate NTK approximations during training. Similarly, Afzal et al. (2025) demonstrated that regularized fine-tuning can remain close to its linearized NTK regime, allowing fine-tuning dynamics to be interpreted through kernel regression and spectral properties of the NTK. Recent work has also applied NTK-inspired quantities operationally in large-scale adaptation systems; for instance, Wang et al. (2025) employed NTK-based similarity to select auxiliary data for low-resource adaptation of models such as Llama3-8B-Instruct and Qwen3-8B, while Li et al. (2025) used first-order and NTK-inspired approximations to estimate fine-tuning behavior and task affinity for adapter-based ensembles of Llama and CodeLlama models. Collectively, these results suggest that kernel-based descriptions of training dynamics can remain informative well beyond idealized infinite-width models. However, existing NTK theory does not by itself provide an inferential framework for DNN training. In particular, it leaves open two fundamental statistical questions: whether training extracts statistically significant structure beyond initialization, and how to estimate the stopping time as a statistical parameter controlling predictive performance. This is the gap addressed in the present paper. To fill this gap, we establish a statistical framework for the training trajectory of over-parameterized DNNs in the NTK regime. We show that continuous-time NTK gradient flow is exactly equivalent to best linear unbiased prediction (BLUP) in a classical random-effects model. At every training time, the DNN predictor coincides with the BLUP of a latent random effect whose covariance structure is determined by the NTK and the training time. Under this equivalence, training time acts as a variance component, or equivalently an empirical Bayes covariance hyperparameter, governing the allocation of variance from 3residual noise to structured signal. This representation provides a statistical interpretation of how learning evolves along the training path and connects DNN training to classical tools from random-effects models, variance-component inference, empirical Bayes inference, and likelihood-based model selection. The fixed-kernel regime studied here is not merely formal. Empirically, large-scale comparisons be-tween finite-width networks and their infinite-width kernel limits show that NTK predictors can match or outperform finite-width networks in certain regimes, while also clarifying when finite-width networks deviate from their kernel limits (Lee et al., 2020). Computationally, the Neural Tangents package provides practical tools for constructing NTK kernels from neural-network architectures and evaluating the corresponding continuous-time gradient-flow predictors (Novak et al., 2020). Thus, the NTK-induced covariance matrix in our random-effects formulation is a meaningful large-width object and, for many architectures, a computable quantity. This perspective yields a two-stage inferential framework for DNN training. First, it enables formal hypothesis testing of whether training captures statistically significant structure beyond initialization. We formulate this question as a variance-component score test (Lin, 1997) for the null hypothesis that the training-induced random effect is absent. Rejecting this null provides statistical evidence that training is beneficial. Second, conditional on training being warranted, the framework provides a principled method for selecting the stopping time via restricted maximum likelihood (REML) (Henderson, 1975; Corbeil and Searle, 1976). In this formulation, early stopping is recast as likelihood-based estimation of a variance com-ponent controlling model complexity, rather than as a heuristic or validation-based tuning rule (Prechelt, 1998; Yao et al., 2007; Bengio, 2012; Goodfellow et al., 2016). Unlike validation-based early stopping, the proposed REML criterion does not require repeatedly training and evaluating the network across many epochs on a held-out validation set. Instead, the stopping time can be estimated directly from the NTK-induced random-effects structure before fully traversing the DNN training trajectory, often substantially reducing computational cost while preserving the full training sample size for model fitting. The resulting 4REML-guided stopping rule admits a spectral interpretation in the NTK eigenbasis: training proceeds until the optimized spectral losses become empirically decorrelated from the NTK eigenvalues. We further show that the REML-guided stopping time achieves asymptotically optimal prediction error, matching the oracle stopping time for fixed-design in-sample prediction and, under additional random-design regularity conditions, for out-of-sample prediction. Conceptually, our results reveal an optimization–inference duality in DNN training. Algorithmically, NTK gradient flow is an optimization trajectory that progressively reduces empirical loss. Statistically, the same trajectory is a random-effects inference path: training time acts as a variance component, the trained network is a BLUP or empirical Bayes posterior mean, and early stopping corresponds to REML estimation. Thus, optimization and statistical inference are two sides of the same coin in the NTK description of DNN training. Our main contributions are threefold. First, we establish the exact equivalence between continuous-time NTK gradient flow and BLUP in a random-effects model. Second, this equivalence leads to a variance-component score test for determining whether DNN training captures signal beyond initialization. Third, it yields a REML-based early-stopping rule with a spectral decorrelation interpretation and asymptotic prediction guarantees, both for fixed-design in-sample risk and, under random-design regularity conditions, for out-of-sample risk. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the data model, the over-parameterized DNN setting, and the NTK gradient-flow formulation. Section 3 develops the random-effects representation of NTK training, the variance-component test for training necessity, the REML-based early-stopping rule, and its spectral interpretation and prediction optimality. Section 4 presents numerical experiments evaluating the proposed testing and early-stopping procedures. Section 5 applies the method to UK Biobank proteomics data. Section 6 discusses implications, limitations, and future directions. 52 Problem Setup and NTK Gradient Flow

The central message of this work is that DNN training admits two complementary interpretations. Al-gorithmically, gradient flow is an optimization trajectory that minimizes empirical loss. Statistically, the same path performs random-effects inference, with training time acting as a variance component that con-trols signal extraction and model complexity. This optimization–inference duality provides a statistical interpretation of the NTK training dynamics. Deep neural network training is most commonly formulated as an optimization problem, in which training time is treated as a tuning parameter chosen by validation or heuristic early-stopping rules (Prechelt, 1998; Yao et al., 2007; Goodfellow et al., 2016). In contrast, we provide a statistical formulation of DNN training in the neural tangent kernel (NTK) regime. We show that continuous-time NTK gradient flow admits an exact representation as best linear unbiased prediction in a random-effects model. Under this representation, training time reallocates variance from the residual noise component to a structured random-effect component governed by the NTK. This equiv-alence connects over-parameterized neural network training to classical tools from random-effects models, variance-component inference, empirical Bayes inference, and likelihood-based model selection. From an empirical Bayes viewpoint, the proposed procedure estimates the prior covariance of the latent signal from the data and then uses the corresponding posterior mean as the trained predictor. This inter-pretation highlights several advantages of REML-based early stopping over conventional validation-based procedures. First, the stopping time can be estimated directly from the NTK-induced random-effects structure before exhaustively traversing the optimization trajectory, rather than being selected retrospec-tively through repeated checkpoint evaluation. Second, REML preserves the full sample for model fitting instead of reserving part of the data for validation, which can be particularly important in moderate-sample or low-signal settings. Third, because the stopping time is inferred as a variance-component parameter within a unified statistical model, the resulting procedure avoids the instability associated with validation-split selection and repeated evaluation across epochs. Finally, the REML criterion pro-vides an interpretable statistical characterization of training duration through variance allocation, spectral 28 regularization, and effective degrees of freedom, rather than treating early stopping as a purely heuristic tuning device. A key implication of this perspective is that the decision to train and the decision of how long to train can be separated into two inferential questions. First, before treating nonlinear training as useful, one can formally test whether the NTK-induced random effect captures statistically meaningful structure beyond initialization. The proposed variance-component score test addresses this question directly and provides a principled alternative to assuming a priori that additional nonlinear training is warranted. Second, conditional on evidence that training is beneficial, the stopping time can be estimated by restricted maximum likelihood. In this sense, early stopping is no longer only a computational or algorithmic device; it becomes a likelihood-based estimate of a variance component controlling model complexity. The resulting REML stopping rule also clarifies the spectral mechanism of early stopping. Along the NTK gradient-flow path, eigendirections associated with larger NTK eigenvalues are activated ear-lier, whereas eigendirections associated with smaller eigenvalues remain more strongly regularized. The REML estimating equation selects the training time at which the optimized spectral losses become em-pirically decorrelated from the NTK eigenvalues. This spectral loss decorrelation condition provides an interpretable stopping principle: before this point, dominant eigendirections remain underfit; after this point, further training increasingly targets lower-eigenvalue directions that are more likely to contain noise. Thus, REML-guided stopping identifies a data-adaptive balance between signal extraction and noise fitting. Although our primary focus is deep neural network training in the NTK regime, the exponential covariance structure reflects a more general principle of squared-error gradient-flow training. Whenever the fitted values evolve under a fixed kernel or tangent operator, continuous-time gradient flow makes the residual decay exponentially along the eigendirections of that operator. Finite-time training therefore acts as a form of dynamic spectral regularization: directions associated with larger eigenvalues are learned earlier, whereas directions associated with smaller eigenvalues remain more strongly suppressed until 29 later in training. The exponential covariance structure in our random-effects formulation is precisely the covariance structure needed for the BLUP to reproduce this gradient-flow-induced shrinkage. Thus, this covariance is not an arbitrary modeling choice, but a consequence of the training dynamics. What is special in the DNN setting is that the infinite-width NTK regime places nonlinear neural-network training within this fixed-kernel gradient-flow class. This observation also distinguishes our formulation from traditional kernel ridge regression and kernel-machine mixed models (Liu et al., 2007). In those settings, regularization is typically imposed statically through an explicit ridge or smoothing penalty. By contrast, early-stopped gradient flow induces regu-larization dynamically through the training trajectory itself. This difference explains why the covariance structure arising from gradient-flow training takes an exponential form rather than the covariance struc-ture commonly used in standard kernel-machine mixed models. Although the REML criterion is motivated by a Gaussian random-effects model, the prediction opti-mality results do not require Gaussian errors. Under regularity conditions on learnability, spectral struc-ture, initialization bias, and error concentration, the REML-guided stopping time achieves asymptotically optimal fixed-design in-sample prediction error relative to the oracle stopping time. Under additional random-design regularity conditions, the same REML-guided stopping rule also achieves asymptotically optimal out-of-sample prediction error for an independently sampled test point. These results support the use of the random-effects representation as a working inferential model whose induced stopping rule remains prediction-optimal under a broader data-generating mechanism. The proposed framework also contributes to the interpretation of model complexity in over-parameterized neural networks. Here, the NTK gradient-flow solution yields a spectral degrees-of-freedom measure, with each eigendirection contributing according to its training-time-dependent activation. This connects train-ing duration, spectral regularization, and effective model complexity within a single statistical framework. From this perspective, over-parameterization itself does not imply uncontrolled complexity; rather, the realized complexity depends on how long the training dynamics are allowed to activate NTK eigendirec-30 tions. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the exact equivalence developed here relies on the infinite-width NTK approximation and continuous-time gradient flow. In this regime, the NTK remains fixed during training and the network behaves like its linearization around initialization, a setting often described as the kernel or fixed-kernel regime (Jacot et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2019). Although this regime provides a tractable and widely used theoretical model for over-parameterized neural networks, finite-width networks trained with discrete-time or stochastic optimization may deviate from this idealized setting. In particular, they may exhibit representation learning, whereby internal features and the induced kernel evolve during training rather than remaining fixed (Chizat and Bach, 2018; Woodworth et al., 2020; Yang and Hu, 2021). Extending the present random-effects framework to such evolving-kernel regimes is therefore an important direction for future work. Mean-field analyses provide one possible route, as they describe training through the evolution of the parameter distribution and can view the resulting dynamics as kernel boosting with a time-varying, data-dependent kernel (Mei et al., 2019; Chizat and Bach, 2018). Such an extension would likely require replacing the fixed NTK covariance structure by an evolving, data-dependent covariance process and developing corresponding inference procedures for time-varying random effects. Second, the current theory focuses on squared-error loss. Extending the framework to classification and other generalized response models will require new analysis because the loss geometry, likelihood structure, and induced covariance dynamics differ from the squared-error case. Nevertheless, the random-effects viewpoint suggests a natural path forward through generalized linear mixed-model analogues and variance-component inference for non-Gaussian outcomes. More broadly, this formulation points to a statistical agenda for deep learning. Training time is only one hyperparameter governing the trajectory of a neural network. Other quantities, including learning rate, weight decay, initialization scale, architecture, batch size, and data augmentation, may also regulate variance allocation, spectral shrinkage, or effective model complexity. Developing inferential tools for these 31 quantities could lead to more principled and interpretable model-selection procedures for deep learning. In summary, the proposed random-effects representation provides a bridge between modern over-parameterized neural networks and classical statistical inference. It yields a formal test for training necessity, a likelihood-based rule for early stopping, a spectral interpretation of model complexity, and asymptotic optimality guarantees for prediction.
