# Cost Comparisons for Random and Deterministic Scans in Component-Wise Markov Chains 

Youngwoo Kwon 

School of Statistics, University of Minnesota 

October 2, 2026 

Abstract 

Gibbs samplers, and more generally component-wise Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithms such as Metropolis-within-Gibbs, can be implemented using either random-scan or deterministic-scan updates. How much convergence can depend on this choice of scanning rule has been a longstanding question. We study this problem through L2 spectral gaps, measuring computational cost in units of component updates. For a d-block Gibbs sampler, the cost of random scan is at most twice that of any fixed deterministic scan, while the reverse cost ratio is at most of order d2. We extend these comparisons to general reversible component-wise updates under the global block-wise contraction condition. If Kj denotes the update of block j and Pj its Gibbs counterpart, and ∥Kj − Pj ∥ ≤ λ0 < 1 for 

j = 1 , . . . , d, . then the cost of random scan is at most 2 /(1 − λ0) times that of deterministic scan, while the reverse cost ratio is of order at most d2/(1 − λ0). Examples show that the cost bounds for random scan relative to deterministic scan are asymptotically sharp and that the joint dependence on d and (1 − λ0)−1 in the reverse comparison cannot be improved uniformly. This work was assisted by generative AI, including for formal verification of mathematical results in Lean. The human author reviewed and verified the mathematical content and takes full responsibility for the results. 

# 1 Introduction 

Suppose that we want to generate a sample from a multivariate target distribution Π using a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method [5]. When direct sampling is difficult, we can construct a Markov chain with invariant distribution Π by dividing the coordinates into blocks and updating one block at a time while holding the others fixed. These chains are called component-wise Markov chains. They include Gibbs samplers, which update the selected block by drawing from its full conditional distribution given the remaining blocks [12, 13], and Metropolis-within-Gibbs algorithms, which replace one or more Gibbs updates with conditional Metropolis–Hastings updates [14, 20, 24]. Many MCMC methods, including posterior simulation for Bayesian hierarchical models and data-augmentation algorithms, use such component-wise updates [4, 12, 33]. For a component-wise algorithm with d blocks, we need to choose both the update for each block and how the block updates are performed. The latter choice is called the scanning rule [17]. Among the various scanning rules, two common choices are random scan, which selects a block uniformly and independently at each step, and deterministic scan, which updates all blocks in a fixed order during each sweep. Since a random-scan chain formed from reversible component-wise updates is itself reversible and can therefore be analyzed using standard self-adjoint theory, whereas the corresponding deterministic-scan chain is generally nonreversible and more difficult to analyze, 1arXiv:2610.01121v1 [math.ST] 1 Oct 2026 determining which scanning rule converges faster has long been a natural and challenging question [28]. We address this question in terms of the L2(Π) spectral gap. Let K be a Π-invariant Markov operator on L2(Π) and let ∥ · ∥ denote the operator norm on this space. With Π as an averaging operator f 7 → Π( f ), define the L2(Π) spectral gap of K by ηK = 1 − ∥ K − Π∥. If K has a positive spectral gap ηK > 0, then the chain is geometrically ergodic in L2(Π). Geometric ergodicity also yields central limit theorems under moment conditions [6, 18] and permits asymptotically valid Monte Carlo standard errors under suitable conditions for variance estimation [19, 34]. In general, a larger spectral gap gives a faster geometric bound on convergence. Many earlier studies comparing the convergence of random and deterministic scans have mainly focused on Gibbs samplers. These results show that positivity of the gap for one scanning rule implies positivity for random scan and every deterministic ordering [7, 9]. Extensions to blocked and collapsed Gibbs samplers identify conditions under which gap positivity is inherited when the updates are changed [22]. Quantitative comparisons further bound the deterministic-scan gap below by the random-scan gap divided by a factor of order d. Bounds for the random-scan gap in terms of the deterministic-scan gap are quadratic in the latter and incur additional polynomial and logarithmic losses, with the stronger bound requiring a common gap lower bound over all deterministic orderings [9]. More explicit comparisons are available in particular settings. For Gaussian targets, convergence rates describe how the comparison depends on the covariance structure, blocking and ordering [1, 31]. In the two-component setting, an exact relationship between the asymptotic L2 convergence rates of Gibbs samplers shows that deterministic scan remains at least as fast after accounting for fixed computing times of the component updates [28]. For two reversible updates, deterministic alternation also gives no larger asymptotic variance than their uniform random mixture under covariance summability, when observations are recorded after each update [2]. Examples based on mixing times in total variation favor either scan, exhibit polynomial differences, and show that random scan can lie between the fastest and slowest deterministic orderings [15, 30]. When one or more Gibbs updates are replaced by general component-wise updates, such as Metropolis–Hastings steps, the random-scan and deterministic-scan chains have been analyzed by comparison with the corresponding Gibbs samplers. In the two-component setting, geometric-ergodicity implications connect Gibbs samplers and conditional Metropolis–Hastings variants [28]. Under suitable conditions on the local proposals, uniform ergodicity of a Gibbs sampler implies uniform ergodicity of the corresponding conditional Metropolis–Hastings sampler [20]. Quantitative comparisons for reversible hybrid random-scan chains relate their absolute spectral gaps to the Gibbs gap through uniform conditional absolute spectral-gap bounds [29]. Conditional conductance bounds provide another comparison, including versions localized to sets of high target probability and applications to high-dimensional Bayesian models [4]. For two-component deterministic-scan Metropolis-within-Gibbs chains, weak Poincaré inequalities give explicit subgeometric convergence bounds in terms of the Gibbs sampler and the conditional update kernels [11]. Notably, there exists an earlier result established spectral-gap solidarity for general reversible component-wise updates under a global block-wise contraction condition, together with polynomial quantitative comparisons between random and deterministic scans [21]. In particular, the earlier lower bounds for the random-scan gap are quadratic in the deterministic gap and therefore do not give a cost comparison that is uniform in the size of the deterministic gap. In this paper, we follow this framework to substantially sharpen these comparisons. We show that random scan can be at most a constant factor more costly than its deterministic-scan counterpart, whereas deterministic scan can be more costly than random scan by a factor of order d2, where cost is measured on the reciprocal-gap scale in units of component updates. The constants depend 2on the local contraction parameter. For Gibbs updates, the resulting bounds are consequences of existing projection inequalities, as discussed below. # 6 Discussion 

The comparison results extend the bounds available for Gibbs projections to general reversible component-wise updates and sharpen the quantitative comparisons in the earlier joint paper [21]. In both directions, the lower gap bound is linear in the gap of the other scanning rule and has dimension dependence d−1.For the cost ratio of random scan to deterministic scan, the constant 2 /(1 − λ0) is asymptotically sharp for every fixed λ0 ∈ [0 , 1). The Gaussian overrelaxation example proves this with a fixed local contraction parameter. In the opposite direction, Theorem 1.2 gives a cost bound of order 

d2/(1 − λ0). The Metropolis pyramid attains these joint orders uniformly over the contraction parameter. This establishes sharpness of the parameter dependence. Negative local spectral values explain why the contraction parameter appears in the random-to-deterministic cost bound. Positive semidefinite local updates satisfy the factor-two bound even without Assumption GBC. In the Gaussian example attaining 2 /(1 − λ0), the conditional linear mode has eigenvalue −λ0. On this mode, the local inequality in (31) is an equality. The costs studied here are based on the L2 norm gap. This gap does not determine total-variation mixing times uniformly over starting states. For example, product Gibbs targets have equal costs on the scale considered here, while random scan has a coupon-collector obstruction to updating all coordinates from a point initialization [30, Section 2].
