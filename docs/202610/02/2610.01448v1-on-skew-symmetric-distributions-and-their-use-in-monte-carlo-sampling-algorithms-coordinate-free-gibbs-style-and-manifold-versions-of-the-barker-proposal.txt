Abstract 

Skew-symmetric probability distributions provide a principled mechanism for incorporating gradient information into Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithms. Here we review the (precondi-tioned) Barker proposal, a Metropolis–Hastings algorithm built on skew-symmetric distributions, and motivate its design. We then introduce three natural extensions. First, we propose coordinate-free variants of the Barker algorithm. Second, we introduce a Gibbs-style Barker algorithm that re-evaluates the gradient at each partially updated coordinate. Third, we derive a simplified manifold Barker algorithm, producing a manifold sampler with enhanced robustness compared to natural comparators. Numerical experiments demonstrate that the Gibbs-style variant improves raw sampling efficiency on correlated targets, that the coordinate-free variants offer limited practi-cal advantage over the standard Barker proposal once computational costs are accounted for, and that the simplified manifold Barker algorithm can achieve significant advantages over simplified manifold MALA when the local geometric structure of the target is irregular or unreliable. 

Keywords: Markov chain Monte Carlo; skew-symmetric distributions; manifold methods; Langevin dynamics; locally-balanced proposals 

# 1 Introduction 

Monte Carlo sampling algorithms are a ubiquitous tool in modern probabilistic modeling. The need to compute expectations that are not available analytically is commonplace in the classical settings of Bayesian inference and maximum intractable likelihood estimation, as well as the more contemporary settings of deep generative models and reinforcement learning. Sampling algorithms based on Markov chains are the gold standard for high-dimensional Monte Carlo. The Metropolis–Hastings algorithm provides an umbrella approach from which to design more specific methods, and reduces the question of designing a useful algorithm to that of designing an effective proposal at each iteration of the algorithm, which is then accepted or rejected using the Metropolis–Hastings filter. When sampling from continuous probability distributions with smooth densities, state of the art approaches use the gradient of the log-density to guide proposals into favorable regions (Green et al. 2015; ˇStrumbelj et al. 2024). This strategy has proven highly successful in practice and is well motivated by the study of continuous-time Markov processes and their mixing properties (Green et al. 2015). Skew-symmetric probability distributions were only recently suggested as a choice of proposal in a Metropolis–Hastings algorithm, via the Barker proposal of Livingstone and Zanella (2022). The skewing mechanism provides a principled way to introduce the gradient, leading to an approach that can work well when other gradient-based methods fail. The skew-symmetric proposal can be theoretically

In both cases the process being approximated has limiting distribution equal to that from which samples are desired. In this article we will review skew-symmetric distributions in Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling and motivate their use both theoretically and practically. We then introduce some natural extensions and evaluate them on some benchmark examples. In Section 2 we review the Barker proposal scheme as a Metropolis–Hastings algorithm, paying close attention to the role of skew-symmetric distributions in its design. In Sections 2.1-2.2 we discuss the theoretical motivation behind the algorithm, via its connection to two continuous-time stochastic processes, the overdamped Langevin diffusion and the locally-balanced Markov jump process. In Section 3 we introduce a new version of the Barker proposal with more natural geometric prop-erties, defined as a mixture of (element-wise) skew-symmetric kernels over orthogonal transformations. We consider a version obtained by averaging with respect to the Haar measure over the orthogo-nal group, and another using a more computationally efficient discrete mixture distribution based on Walsh–Hadamard transforms (Ailon and Chazelle 2009; Tropp 2011). In Section 4 we introduce another perturbation of the Barker proposal in which the skew-symmetric kernels are defined using iterative computation of the gradient vector. This algorithm, termed ‘Gibbs-style Barker’, can exploit intermediate decisions to guide the proposal in a more informed way than the standard Barker algo-rithm when the distribution of interest has strong dependencies between coordinates. We compare each of these new algorithms to the standard Barker proposal on some natural benchmark models. In Section 5 we consider skew-symmetric distributions arising from approximation of more general diffusion process dynamics, which naturally leads to an algorithm that exploits not only gradients but second and third derivative information about the distribution of interest when applied to the overdamped Langevin diffusion with position-dependent volatility (Xifara et al. 2014). The class of algorithms based on this object and other related stochastic processes is called manifold methods . We introduce a simplified manifold Barker algorithm, following the approach of the simplified manifold MALA algorithm of Girolami and Calderhead (2011), in which terms involving third derivatives are ignored to reduce computational costs. We then compare simplified manifold Barker and MALA on some numerical examples to illustrate the enhanced robustness properties that the skew-symmetric approach provides in the setting of manifold Markov chain Monte Carlo.

The algorithm inherits an important stability property from skew-symmetric distributions. The size of the proposal |yi − xi| = |zi|, where zi ∼ fσ is a draw from the symmetric base density fσ .Crucially, therefore, the size of the increment is not influenced by the magnitude of the gradient vector. This is in stark contrast to alternative gradient-based approaches, in which the size of the proposed move is essentially proportional to the size of the gradient when the latter is large. Because of this, gradient-based algorithms typically become unstable when the gradient is not a Lipschitz function of 

x or behaves irregularly in some other way. Alternative approaches to combating this phenomenon tend to modify the algorithm so that only the unit vector in the gradient direction is used, but this sacrifices the information about the size of the gradient, which is informative if used correctly. The skew-symmetric construction of the Barker algorithm, by contrast, uses both the magnitude and the direction of the gradient, but does so in a way that does not result in stability issues when the gradient is very large or otherwise irregular. In practical applications, it seems that this robustness is most easily seen when the global scale σ and the preconditioner L are learned adaptively during the sampling (e.g. Andrieu and Thoms (2008)). The optimization schemes used to choose σ and L seem to converge much more quickly in the presence of irregular gradients for the Barker proposal as compared to other gradient-based sampling algorithms, and this can be justified theoretically by studying spectral gaps of the resulting Markov chains (Livingstone and Zanella 2022). The name ‘Barker’ proposal pays homage to an alternative Metropolis–Hastings style algorithm proposed by the physicist AA Barker (Barker 1965). In place of the acceptance rate min(1 , t ), where t is the Hastings ratio π(y)q(x|y)/(π(x)q(y|x)), the function 1 /(1 + t−1), sometimes called ‘Barker’s rule’, is used. ## 2.1 Motivation from Langevin dynamics 

One example of a continuous-time process that has limiting distribution π under mild conditions is the (preconditioned) overdamped Langevin diffusion, the solution to the stochastic differential equation 

dX (t) = 12 A∇ log π(X(t)) dt + √A dW (t), X(0) = x, (3) for some positive-definite matrix A ∈ Rd×d. This process is the inspiration for numerous sampling algorithms, both within the Metropolis–Hastings class and beyond (e.g. Durmus and Moulines (2019) and Welling and Teh (2011)). A natural way to design a Metropolis–Hastings proposal based on (3) is to approximate the intractable transition density ps(·| x) meaning the density of X(t + s)|(X(t) = x), with some tractable surrogate ˜ ps(·| x) over a small time period s > 0. Repeatedly simulating X(n+1) ∼

˜ps(·| X(n)) will produce a valid Markov chain without further modification, but it will not in general have limiting distribution π because ˜ ps is not exactly the same as ps. To correct for this, we can instead set ˜ps(·| x) = q(·| x), the proposal in a Metropolis–Hastings algorithm, and allow the Metropolis–Hastings filter α to ensure that the Metropolized Markov chain will have limiting distribution π.The most conventional way to design Metropolis–Hastings proposals using (3) is via the Euler– Maruyama discretization scheme (Kloeden and Platen 1989). This equates to approximating the transition density ps(·| x) with a Gaussian of the form N (x + sA ∇ log π(x)/2, sA ). ## 3.2 Coordinate-free Metropolis–Hastings proposals 

We will call a Metropolis–Hastings proposal coordinate-invariant or coordinate-free if its proposal transition density qπ,L (which may depend on both π and a preconditioner L ∈ Rd×d) satisfies the relationship 

qπQ,QLQ ⊤ (Qy |Qx ) = qπ,L (y|x), πQ(x) := π(Q⊤x) (5) for all x and y in Rd, and any orthogonal matrix Q ∈ Rd×d. Intuitively this means that changing the coordinate system will not affect where the proposal places its probability mass. The Barker proposal does not satisfy (5). A simple attempt to define a coordinate-free alternative is the skew-symmetric distribution 

q(y|x) ∝ 2 · F (cF · L⊤∇ log π(x)⊤z)fσ (z), z := L−1(y − x).

This is arguably a more geometrically natural choice of multivariate proposal, but was already con-sidered in Livingstone and Zanella (2022) and shown to be inferior to (1). Intuitively the limitation is that the information provided by the gradient is summarized in a single scalar L⊤∇ log π(x)⊤z. This is discussed in some detail in Section 4 of Hird et al. (2020). Another option is to consider a coordi-nate system based on the gradient itself, meaning that ∇ log π(x) is chosen as the first basis vector at each iteration. In fact, however, this results in exactly the same coordinate-invariant proposal as that described above, though some algebra is required to see this.

# 5 A Manifold Barker algorithm 

Distributions of interest that possess varying local geometry, non-uniform scaling, or correlation struc-tures that change across the state space pose a challenge for samplers based on a constant global preconditioner. A natural remedy is to let the preconditioning matrix adapt to the local geometry, resulting in a position-dependent transformation. In this section we devise a variant of the Barker pro-posal that can operate well in such a setting, based on the theory of Riemannian sampling algorithms, which are briefly reviewed below.

The Euler–Maruyama discretization of this stochas-tic differential equation with approximate transition density N (x + sA (x)∇ log π(x)/2 + sΓ( x), sA (x)) for suitably chosen s > 0 is the manifold MALA (MMALA) proposal distribution used by the same authors to define a Metropolis–Hastings algorithm that can navigate irregularly-shaped distributions of interest. The function A : Rd → Rd×d is required to be positive definite at every x, but is otherwise a free design decision that is originally motivated by geometric considerations (Girolami and Calderhead 2011). A natural objective is to choose A(x)−1 to match the local covariance structure of π. An intuitive choice is therefore the Hessian A(x)−1 = −∇ 2 log π(x), though this may not be positive definite everywhere when π is not log-concave. One approach to address this is to use the SoftAbs metric (Betancourt 2013), which applies a smooth transformation to each eigenvalue of the metric via

Other variants and non-Hessian based choices are also possible (Girolami and Calderhead 2011; Livingstone and Girolami 2014). Moreover, it is shown in Girolami and Calderhead (2011) that SMMALA performs comparably to MMALA on a range of targets while avoiding the third-derivative computation, leading to a more efficient algorithm in terms of effective samples produced per unit of time owing to its lower cost per iteration.

# 6 Numerical Study 

We present two sets of experiments. The first compares the Gibbs-style, Hadamard, and Rotated Barker variants against the standard Barker proposal on a high–dimensional Gaussian target distri-bution with known precision matrix and on a Bayesian logistic regression posterior using the Sonar dataset. The second compares SMBarker against MALA, Barker, and SMMALA on two target dis-tributions with non-linear correlation structure, namely the hybrid Rosenbrock distribution and the FitzHugh–Nagumo model. For SMMALA and SMBarker, the position-dependent preconditioner is taken to be SoftAbs metric of Betancourt (2013), meaning the Hessian A(x)−1 = −∇ 2 log π(x) regu-larized by the SoftAbs function as described in Section 5. All other methods use an identity precondi-tioner L = I. All algorithms are implemented in Python to ensure a fair comparison of computational costs. Sampling efficiency is measured using effective sample size (ESS), which approximates the number of independent draws from the target distribution that would give the same Monte Carlo precision as the correlated chain (Roy 2020). For each run, the effective sample size is computed separately for each coordinate and the minimum across coordinates is taken as a summary. We report this minimum ESS, ESS per iteration, and ESS per CPU second. Each metric is averaged across independent runs and computed using post-burn-in iterations only. Step sizes are tuned adaptively during burn-in iterations using Algorithm 4 of Andrieu and Thoms (2008) and held fixed thereafter, with all performance metrics computed on the main iterations only. The target acceptance probability is set to 0 .574 for all algorithms following the asymptotic optimality results for MALA and the Barker proposal (Roberts and Rosenthal 1998; Vogrinc et al. 2023).

# 7 Discussion 

Skew-symmetric distributions have proven useful in many application areas. We have highlighted their effectiveness in designing Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling algorithms. The justification for their use can be understood in two different ways, as detailed in Sections 2.1-2.2. The first is derived from a more general application of skew-symmetric distributions to approximate the transition densities of stochastic differential equations for numerical simulation purposes. The second is via a newly studied class of Markov jump processes for which skew-symmetric distributions are often a good approximation to the jump kernel. The resulting MCMC algorithm has some connection to the work of physicist AA Barker (Barker 1965), and has therefore been given the name ‘Barker proposal’ when used as a proposal kernel within a Metropolis–Hastings algorithm. Here we present a full description of the preconditioned Barker proposal on a general d-dimensional Euclidean space, and review its motivation. We then consider three natural ways to modify the algorithm and assess the relative benefits of each. First we study coordinate-free variants of the algorithm, which rely on the introduction of an orthogonal mixture of skew-symmetric distributions. We then consider a variant in which the degree of skew for the ith component when i > 1 depends on an innovation in the first ( i−1) components, meaning that the sampling of a d-dimensional vector from this so-called Gibbs-style Barker proposal must be done in an iterative manner (similar in spirit to the Gibbs sampler). Finally we construct a version of the Barker proposal that exploits local geometry of the target distribution, leading to a Riemannian manifold sampler in the spirit of Girolami and Calderhead (2011). Some natural conclusions can be drawn from the work offered here. The standard Barker proposal is not coordinate-invariant according to the definition of Section 3, but the degree to which performance depends on the coordinate basis appears to be mild in comparison to some other algorithms (such as the Gibbs sampler). This is evidenced by the numerical results of Sections 6.1 and 6.2, in which the raw ESSs of the standard Barker proposal and coordinate-free alternatives are consistently comparable. When the additional costs associated with generating new bases at each iteration are taken into account, then the coordinate-free approach does not appear to offer any benefits. Iterative computation of the degree of skew in each coordinate as in the Gibbs-style Barker algorithm does, however, appear to be advantageous in terms of raw effective sample sizes, with a factor of around 1.8 increase demonstrated for the logistic regression example using the Sonar dataset in Section 6.2. The numerical challenge posed by iteratively evaluating the gradient seems to limit time-normalized performance, however. In cases such as logistic regression, where each partial derivative requires looping through the entire dataset to re-evaluate quantities, then these numerical difficulties may be unavoidable without some 16 level of approximation. For other examples like the Gaussian, however, there is some evidence offered in the appendix that implementation in a compiled programming language such as C++ can make the sampling costs comparable between the Gibbs-style and standard Barker approaches. The simplified manifold Barker algorithm appears to offer benefits over the simplified manifold MALA approach in terms of ease of tuning and robustness, sometimes at the cost of reduced efficiency. This is in line with typical findings comparing the standard Barker algorithm with Gaussian symmetric noise distribution to MALA. In that case the loss in efficiency can typically be reduced to zero by replacing the Gaussian with a bimodal symmetric density, as shown in Vogrinc et al. (2023). The extra degree of mismatch that this causes within the acceptance rate of simplified manifold Barker, however, seems to limit the degree to which similar performance gains can be made in the manifold case.

We include a short experiment comparing the run time in three different programming languages to compute a 1000-dimensional dense Gaussian gradient vector ∇g(x) at state x, a random sample from the same Gaussian with full precision matrix, and then perform the update x + h∇g(x) for some fixed scalar h > 0 (Full update), compared with the iterative computation of ∂g (x[i−1] )/∂x i and the update yi = xi + h∂g (x[i−1] )/∂x i for i ∈ { 1, ..., 1000 } where x[i−1] := ( y1, ..., y i−1, x i, ..., x 1000 ) (Staged update). The results of Table 5 suggest that in a compiled language such as C++ the run times are comparable, whereas in an interpreted language such as Python or R the staged update, which mimics the Gibbs-style Barker algorithm, takes significantly longer. Similar experiments with g taken as the log-posterior of the logistic regression model using the Sonar dataset did not, however, give the same results, with the Gibbs-style update still taking significantly longer in C++.
