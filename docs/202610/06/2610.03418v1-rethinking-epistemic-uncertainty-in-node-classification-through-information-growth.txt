## ABSTRACT 

Epistemic uncertainty should decrease as additional information about the data-generating process (DGP) becomes available to the predictor. Yet, existing graph evidential deep learning (EDL) methods for node classification typically construct epistemic uncertainty from graph-specific properties and evaluate it on down-stream tasks such as out-of-distribution detection, which do not test its reducibility as information about the DGP increases. To make reducibility directly testable, we introduce a statistical framework for studying epistemic uncertainty under in-formation growth. Our framework specifies an information-growth experimental protocol and a consistency criterion for epistemic predictors, while using projec-tive graph DGPs to ensure that growing graphs, which in general need not provide increasing information about the same DGP, constitute coherent observations of the same underlying process. We show that EDL methods do not explicitly es-timate data uncertainty arising from a single finite graph observation and instead regulate epistemic uncertainty through model hyperparameters, precluding con-sistency, as corroborated by controlled information-growth experiments. As an alternative, we propose graph bootstrap ensembles, capturing both data and pro-cedural uncertainty through graph resampling and randomized training. Under the same experimental protocol, these ensembles exhibit epistemic uncertainty reduc-tion beyond standard deep ensembles. These findings support bootstrap ensembles as candidate consistent epistemic predictors under information growth. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Machine learning models are increasingly expected to provide not only accurate predictions, but also reliable estimates of predictive uncertainty (Abdar et al., 2021; Gawlikowski et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025). Predictive uncertainty is commonly decomposed into aleatoric uncertainty, arising from intrinsic ambiguity in the data-generating process (DGP) and thus irreducible, and 

epistemic uncertainty, arising from limited knowledge of that process and thus reducible with ad-ditional data (H¨ ullermeier & Waegeman, 2021; Sale et al., 2026). In classical supervised learning with independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations, under suitable regularity condi-tions, epistemic uncertainty is operationally characterized to decrease monotonically as information increases and eventually vanish (Bengs et al., 2022; Juergens et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2024). Extending this characterization to node classification on graphs is non-trivial. While increasing sample size provides a natural information-growth mechanism in the i.i.d. setting, no analogous mechanism is readily available for node classification. Intuitively, information growth would involve progressively larger graph observations and training sets becoming available, allowing predictors to be retrained on more informative observations for the same node classification problem. However, nodes are dependent, and observing more of them does not necessarily yield more information about the same underlying DGP (Shalizi & Rinaldo, 2013). Consequently, node classification currently lacks a framework for assessing the reducibility of epistemic uncertainty under information growth. Graph uncertainty quantification has been studied using a variety of approaches (see Wang et al. (2024); Chen et al. (2024) for reviews). Among these, evidential deep learning (EDL) meth-ods (Zhao et al., 2020; Stadler et al., 2021; Hart et al., 2023; Damke & H¨ ullermeier, 2024a;b) are particularly relevant because they make the epistemic interpretation a central modeling objective, 1arXiv:2610.03418v1 [cs.LG] 2 Oct 2026 Preprint prescribing appropriate uncertainty behavior through graph-specific principles, including reducibil-ity through feature support and structural support , and the reducibility or irreducibility due to neigh-borhood conflict . However, these principles are defined with respect to the features and topology of a fixed observed graph rather than under information growth. Empirical evaluations likewise assess epistemic uncertainty through its utility for out-of-distribution (OOD) detection, whose opti-mal predictors need not coincide with predictors of reducible epistemic uncertainty (Paplh´ am et al., 2026). While informative about task-specific downstream utility, such evaluations therefore cannot establish whether an uncertainty score has this epistemic interpretation. Together, these limitations raise fundamental questions about how epistemic uncertainty should be defined and modeled in node classification. We investigate these issues through three research ques-tions: (RQ1) What constitutes a principled statistical framework for epistemic predictors in node classification? (RQ2) Are graph EDL models consistent epistemic predictors, and, if not, what are their fundamental limitations? (RQ3) Which alternative approach can address these limitations? 

Contributions. We address these questions through three main contributions. 

(1) We establish the first statistical framework for epistemic uncertainty under information growth in node classification, comprising an information-growth experimental protocol and a definition of consistent epistemic predictors. The former naturally requires projective graph DGPs to preserve the underlying node-classification problem across nested graphs and learning tasks (Shalizi & Rinaldo, 2013), while the latter requires decreasing and asymptotically vanishing uncertainty. Together, these provide a statistical basis for assessing epistemic reducibility beyond conventional evaluations. 

(2) We unify graph EDL objectives and characterize their population optima, showing how model hyperparameters artificially regulate epistemic uncertainty in the absence of explicit estimation of data uncertainty (Shen et al., 2024), arising from observing a single finite realization of the DGP, ultimately precluding epistemic consistency. Controlled information-growth experiments on projec-tive graphs confirm insufficient epistemic concentration and persistent parameterization dependence, corroborating the structural limitations that undermine their use as consistent epistemic predictors. 

(3) Motivated by evidence that bootstrap-based ensembles capture data and procedural uncer-tainty (Jimenez et al., 2026), thus enabling decreasing epistemic uncertainty in the i.i.d. setting (Jain & Bates, 2025), we extend this approach to graphs through graph bootstrap ensembles . Under the same protocol as in (2), the experiments support these ensembles as a family of candidate consis-tent epistemic predictors, demonstrating the ability to recover epistemic concentration absent from standard deep ensembles. Together, these contributions establish a statistical foundation for understanding epistemic uncer-tainty in node classification, reveal the limitations of existing evidential approaches when assessed through information growth, and introduce graph bootstrap ensembles as a theoretically motivated, empirically supported alternative for modeling epistemic uncertainty that contracts under informa-tion growth.

## 3 A S TATISTICAL FRAMEWORK FOR GRAPH EPISTEMIC UNCERTAINTY 

Addressing (RQ1) , we extend the i.i.d. characterization of epistemic predictors (Bengs et al., 2022; Juergens et al., 2024) to node classification by defining a statistical framework with an information-growth experimental protocol and a consistency criterion for epistemic predictors. 3.1 INFORMATION -G ROWTH EXPERIMENTAL PROTOCOL 

Constructing an information-growth experimental protocol for node classification requires three components: (i) the sampling unit , i.e., what constitutes one observation from the DGP; (ii) the 

sampling scheme , i.e., how increasingly informative observations are generated from the same DGP; and (iii) the learning task , i.e., how the supervised prediction problem evolves along this sequence. 

Sampling unit. Unlike classical datasets, the nodes of a graph are not a collection of i.i.d. observa-tions. We therefore take the observed graph G = ( V, E), equivalently the subgraph induced by V,as the sampling unit, following the graph-as-observation perspective of Shalizi & Rinaldo (2013). Increasing information then corresponds to observing nested graphs G ⊂ G ′, with V ⊂ V ′ and G

equal to the subgraph of G′ induced by V.

Sampling scheme. Requiring two nested graphs to arise from the same DGP is non-trivial. This consistency is implicit in the i.i.d. setting, where every finite sample is a restriction of the same infinite sequence, but need not hold across graphs of different sizes. We make it explicit through 

projectibility (Shalizi & Rinaldo, 2013). Let A be a collection of finite subsets of a countable node index set I, ordered by inclusion, and let {PA,ϕ }A∈A ,ϕ ∈Φ denote a family of finite-dimensional DGPs, where GA ∼ PA,ϕ denotes the attributed graph indexed by A. For A ⊂ B, let πB→A denote the natural projection that restricts an observation indexed by B to the indices in A.

Other prominent graph models, including many exponential random graph models and sparse random graph formulations, are not projective (Shalizi & Rinaldo, 2013; Schweinberger et al., 2017). 

Learning task. Finally, the supervised tasks induced by increasingly large graph observations must exploit the corresponding information growth. If T (tr) ⊂ V and T ′(tr) ⊂ V ′ denote the training sets associated with G ⊂ G ′, respectively, we require T (tr) ⊂ T ′(tr) , so that each larger task extends the previous one with additional information about the same underlying node-classification problem. Thus, the asymptotic behavior of epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty predicted by S-BGCN-K, GPN, GPN-LOP, and CUQ-GNN is determined externally, either by the loss regularization or by the con-struction of the prior. Graph-specific uncertainty principles defined on a fixed graph therefore do not by themselves guarantee the required information-growth behavior. 

## 5 GRAPH BOOTSTRAP ENSEMBLES 

The previous analysis motivates constructing a second-order predictor whose uncertainty is tied explicitly to the information available about the graph DGP. We pursue this idea through graph bootstrap ensembles (GB-Ens), extending deep ensembles, an already understudied approach in node classification (Vieira et al., 2026), to the largely unexplored setting of graph bootstrapping. For each of B bootstrap graph realizations, we train S independently initialized GNNs, mirroring i.i.d. bootstrap ensembles that combine procedural and data variability through resampling (Juergens et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2024; Jain & Bates, 2025; Jimenez et al., 2026). The ability to represent both recognized sources of epistemic uncertainty (Jain & Bates, 2025; Jimenez et al., 2026) provides a key motivation for using bootstrap ensembles rather than alternative second-order predictors, including EDL models and deep ensembles, which may fail to capture one or both sources. In the i.i.d. setting, bootstrap-based MI has also been shown, under suitable conditions, to converge asymptotically to its Bayesian counterpart (Jain & Bates, 2025), providing theoretical support for 6Preprint bootstrap-based epistemic uncertainty estimation with neural networks. For graphs, however, boot-strap guarantees remain restricted to specific combinations of graph DGPs, bootstrap schemes, and estimation targets (Schmid & Desmarais, 2017; Levin & Levina, 2019; Green & Shalizi, 2022; Lin et al., 2020; Shao & Le, 2024), with no analogous result for MI. Nevertheless, projective synthetic graph DGPs whose sampling process can be accurately approximated by a bootstrap procedure provide a tractable setting for studying whether graph bootstrap ensembles empirically exhibit the consistent information-growth behavior of Definition 3.2, analogous to that established in the i.i.d. setting. GB-Ens is also computationally feasible despite increased total cost, through parallel train-ing and sequential inference; see Appendix G for computational comparisons.

## 6 EXPERIMENTS 

We empirically address (RQ2) –(RQ3) through controlled experiments on synthetic projective graph DGPs. For (RQ2) , we validate our analysis of graph EDL uncertainty under information growth. For (RQ3) , we assess graph bootstrap ensembles as an alternative approach to epistemic prediction. Code is available at https://github.com/emmamenegh/Research-Code-Release. 

Models. We evaluate S-BGCN-K and S-BGCN-T-K (Zhao et al., 2020), GPN (Stadler et al., 2021), GPN-GD EVI and GPN-GD LAT (Hart et al., 2023), GPN-LOP (Damke & H¨ ullermeier, 2024a), CUQ-APPNP, CUQ-GAT, and CUQ-GCN (Damke & H¨ ullermeier, 2024b) as EDL baselines, and GB-Ens with APPNP, GAT, and GCN architectures. Each GB-Ens comprises 25 × 4 models, corresponding to 25 bootstrap samples and 4 random seeds per sample. Model parameterizations are reported in Appendix D.

For each DGP, we generate 20 independent replicates of a projective se-quence, with six graph sizes logarithmically spaced between 500 and 10 ,000 nodes. RQ2: Graph EDL models are inconsistent epistemic predictors. 

We examine whether the uncertainty behavior of graph EDL models characterized at the nonpara-metric population optimum by Theorem 4.3 persists with finite data and parametric models. We assess both EU concentration with increasing information and the dependence of EU and AU on model parameterization. Figure 1a shows mean EU across 20 projective-sequence replicates of the exponential-decay graphon, with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (CIs). S-BGCN-K and S-BGCN-T-K exhibit decreasing EU, as permitted by their information-dependent prior, but remain non-negligible over the observed sequence, whereas GPN, GPN-GD, GPN-LOP, and CUQ-GNN remain approximately constant, consistent with their population optima retaining finite evidence. EU magnitude also differs substantially across methods, reflecting its theoretical dependence on the parameterization, as illustrated by the similar magnitudes of GPN, GPN-GD, and CUQ-GNN under shared parameter values. At N = 10 ,000 , varying the EDL regularization parameter λ reveals sub-stantial changes in AU on the same observed graph (Figure 1b), demonstrating its dependence on model parameterization rather than on the DGP alone. Analogous results for the SBM are reported in Appendix E. 

RQ3: GB-Ens is a family of candidate consistent epistemic predictors. 

Figure 2a shows mean GB-Ens epistemic uncertainty across 20 projective-sequence replicates of the exponential-decay graphon, with pointwise 95% bootstrap CIs. From N = 500 to N = 10 ,000 , EU decreases monotonically by 94% for GCN, 91% for APPNP, and 61% for GAT. On the SBM (Ap-pendix E), EU similarly decreases substantially for APPNP and GCN, whereas GAT shows a weaker, non-monotonic reduction due to persistent randomized-training variability, plausibly reflecting its sensitivity to learned attention weights and additional attention dropout. The decomposition in Fig-ure 2b further shows comparable randomized-seed and bootstrap contributions to EU that decrease with increasing information, with relative magnitude depending on the backbone and DGP. Finally, Figure 3b shows that GB-Ens AU decreases and eventually stabilizes, with all three backbones ap-proaching similar plateaus, consistent with convergence toward a predictor-independent estimate of aleatoric uncertainty. The same behavior is observed for the SBM (Appendix E). To assess the effect of graph resampling, we compare GB-Ens with standard graph deep ensembles (G-Ens), trained with 10 different random seeds on the observed graph using the same backbones. Figure 3a shows that, on the exponential-decay graphon, G-Ens exhibit no consistent EU contraction, while Figure 3b shows strongly backbone-dependent AU trajectories, with GAT trending upward and GCN continuing to decrease. Similar behavior is observed on the SBM (Appendix E). Moreover, the gap between G-Ens EU and the randomized-training component of GB-Ens widens with graph size, as the latter contracts under information growth while the former does not. Graph resampling therefore does not simply add bootstrap variability, but induces convergent behavior also in the seed component, enabling EU contraction that is not observed for standard deep ensembles. 8Preprint

## 7 CONCLUSIONS 

Take-home message. Epistemic uncertainty in node classification should be assessed through its behavior under increasing information, rather than solely by obeying principles or being useful for downstream tasks on a fixed graph. This perspective identifies consistent epistemic uncertainty as requiring estimates that reflect data uncertainty rather than remaining hyperparameter-dependent, a requirement generally not met by graph EDL models. GB-Ens provides a constructive approach by capturing data and procedural uncertainty, with controlled experiments supporting it as a family of candidate consistent predictors. 

Limitations & future work. The theoretical consistency of bootstrap-based graph epistemic predic-tors remains to be established. Moreover, information-growth behavior cannot be evaluated on stan-dard real-world benchmarks, which lack the sampling process required to generate increasingly in-formative observations from the same DGP. Benchmarks leveraging real graph-growth mechanisms, 9Preprint where projectibility is often natural (Shalizi & Rinaldo, 2013), could extend our analysis beyond synthetic random graphs, either retaining node classification and motivating improved bootstrap-based predictors or considering tasks more naturally aligned with graph growth.

## G COMPUTATIONAL COMPLEXITY 

Graph EDL methods can themselves incur substantial computational costs, with different methods trading off preprocessing, training, inference, and memory requirements. Ensemble-based methods require repeated model fitting, but retain manageable per-model computation and peak memory and are naturally parallelizable. GPN and CUQ-GCN admit O(N +M ) implementations, whereas GPN-LOP and S-BGCN-K can incur O(N 2) computation due to dense propagation and pairwise GKDE prior construction, respectively. For GCN-based ensembles, training costs O(N + M ) per member; GB-Ens additionally requires bootstrap generation, whose cost depends on the k-NN construction and can reach O(N 2) for general similarity search, although cheaper alternatives are available, such as the open Jaccard similarity metric used in our experiments. Bootstrap generation is repeated for each bootstrap sample. Across ensemble members, training cost scales linearly for both GB-Ens and G-Ens but is naturally parallelizable. Moreover, processing members independently and accumulat-ing uncertainty statistics online keeps peak memory at O(N +M ), comparable to GPN, CUQ-GNN, and S-BGCN-K, whereas GPN-LOP can require O(N 2) memory due to dense propagation. 28 Preprint Table 11: Mean normalized Wasserstein distances between GB-Ens- and true-DGP-induced second-order distributions.
