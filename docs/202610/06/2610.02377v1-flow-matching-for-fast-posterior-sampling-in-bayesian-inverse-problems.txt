Flow Matching for Fast Posterior Sampling in
Bayesian Inverse Problems
Jan Blechschmidt, Oliver G. Ernst, Moritz Poguntke, Bj¨orn Sprungk
October 5, 2026
Sampling from the posterior is the central task of computational Bayesian inverse
problems. The standard workhorse in Bayesian inference—Markov chain Monte Carlo
(MCMC)—is sequential, yields correlated samples, and must be rerun for each observa-
tion. Conditional flow matching offers an amortized alternative: a transport map, trained
once on joint samples of parameter and data, that yields independent approximate pos-
terior samples for any observation at negligible online cost, without new likelihood eval-
uations. We give a careful, MCMC-literate assessment of flow matching for PDE-based
inverse problems with function-valued parameters. Exploiting the flow’s tractable den-
sity, we derive computable accuracy estimates of the underlying approximate posterior
in total-variation distance and Kullback–Leibler divergence and, moreover, propose a hy-
brid sampler that is asymptotically exact by Metropolization. We validate the accuracy
estimates and demonstrate the amortization in several numerical examples, including
electrical impedance tomography and a likelihood-free Lotka–Volterra model.
1. Introduction
For the past three decades Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) has been the workhorse for sam-
pling from posterior distributions in Bayesian inference and Bayesian inverse problems.
Modern
variants — dimension-robust proposals such as preconditioned Crank–Nicolson [Cot+13; HSV14],
likelihood-informed [CLM16; RS18] and adaptive [Haa+06; HST01] schemes — are well understood
theoretically and remarkably reliable in practice. Their cost profile, however, is fixed by design: the
Markov chain is sequential, its samples are correlated, every step requires at least one evaluation
of the forward model, and the entire computation must be repeated from scratch for each new ob-
servation. In applications where the same inverse problem is solved again and again for a stream
of measurements — electrical impedance tomography (EIT) being a prototypical example — this
per-observation cost is the dominant obstacle to online or real-time uncertainty quantification.
A different paradigm, going back to El Moselhy and Marzouk [EM12] and systematically developed
in [Par15; Mar+17], is to construct a transport map pushing a simple reference distribution forward
to the posterior: once the map is available, we can generate, in parallel, arbitrarily many independent
samples from (an approximation to) the posterior. If the transport map is of block-triangular struc-
ture and trained to generate the joint distribution of unknown parameters and observable data, then
a classical disintegration argument in [Mar+17, Sec. 7] shows that this transport map reproduces
the posterior for (almost) all possible observations. Hence, transport-based sampling for Bayesian
inverse problems is amortized: after an offline training phase approximate posterior samples for
any new observation are available at negligible online cost (= evaluation of the transport map),
and no further likelihood evaluations are needed. These are substantial promises, in particular for
PDE-based Bayesian inverse problems with computationally expensive likelihoods.
For the construction and training of suitable transport maps, generative machine learning has sup-
plied several flexible and scalable approaches in recent years. In the foundational works [EM12;

ing, reducing training cost by orders of magnitude. Bayesian flow matching, i.e., flow matching
for sampling conditional distributions, has been derived and discussed in detail in [Che+25; Hag25;
WS25] but demonstrated primarily on low-dimensional benchmarks and imaging-type applications.
Very recently, [Kr¨u+26] compare three different machine learning approaches for transport sam-
pling of posteriors in Bayesian inverse problems — GANs, invertible neural networks, and Bayesian
flow matching — and found Bayesian flow matching to be the “clear winner”. Moreover, due to
the likelihood-free training, flow matching can also be used for simulation-based inference [Wil+23;
CBL20] where the likelihood is either unknown or computationally intractable — and, hence, classical
MCMC methods, which rely on likelihood evaluations, are not applicable.
Thus, flow matching seems to be one of the most promising approaches for transport-based sampling
of conditional distributions. What is missing so far in the literature, in our view, is a careful, MCMC-
literate assessment of the performance of flow matching for PDE-based Bayesian inverse problems —
not compared to other transport-based approaches but rather to competitive gold-standard MCMC
algorithms and done on Bayesian inverse problems of increasing difficulty. Furthermore, this paper
aims to provide computable a-posteriori bounds, both lower and upper, of the accuracy of the
learned flow matching posteriors. These bounds are derived by means of (Markov chain) Monte
Carlo theory and exploit a combination of flow matching and the independent Metropolis–Hastings
algorithm, which yields a computationally efficient, asymptotically unbiased flow matching-based
sampler outperforming standard MCMC samplers in some challenging Bayesian inverse problems.
In this regard, we also mention previous works on the combination of transport maps and MCMC,
namely, Parno and Marzouk [PM18], in which monotone triangular transport maps were exploited for
posterior-adapted proposals accelerating MCMC and Grenioux et al. [Gre+23], in which discrete-time
normalizing flows were also studied as proposals within independent Metropolis–Hastings. Moreover,
we also refer to [BDD24] for a-priori error bounds for unconditional flow matching in Wasserstein
distance which, however, are not computable in practice.

shortcomings, is the subject of future work.

Computational aspects
MH algorithms share a common cost structure that frames the comparison
in this paper. The Markov chain is inherently sequential — each state depends on its predecessor
— so sampling typically parallelizes only across independent chains or across proposal generation.
Its output is correlated, so the effective sample size falls short of the chain length, often by orders
of magnitude in the case of poor mixing. Every iteration spends at least one forward solve, the
dominant cost in PDE-based Bayesian inverse problems. Moreover, the entire computation is tied
to a single observation y: a new measurement requires running a new Markov chain.
The flow
matching methodology assessed in the next section targets precisely these computational bottlenecks
— independent samples, generated in parallel, at negligible per-sample cost, amortized over all
observations — while giving up the asymptotic exactness that the Metropolis–Hastings correction
provides. In Sections 3.3 and 3.4 we show how the two approaches can be recombined to recover
it.

In this paper four MH variants serve as baselines; we recall them in the briefest terms and refer to
the cited literature for details.
Independent Metropolis–Hastings (IMH) uses a proposal q which does not depend on the current
state, i.e., q(˜x|x(k)) = q(˜x) or ˜x = ζ with ζ ∼q, respectively. The performance of IMH depends

highly correlated output. In Section 3.3 we exploit the flow matching approximation as proposal
within IMH which yields unbiased samples as well as an empirical estimate of the accuracy of the

3. Posterior sampling via flow matching
This section describes the transport-based sampler assessed in this paper. We first recall the struc-
tural result reducing posterior sampling to a measure transport problem on the product of parameter
and observation space (Section 3.1). We then introduce the representation of the transport map as the
flow of a neural ordinary differential equation and explain why its classical, likelihood-based training
is computationally prohibitive in the inverse problems context before presenting flow matching, the
regression-based training that makes the approach practical (Section 3.2). Finally, we discuss how
flow matching combined with MCMC can yield unbiased simulations of the posterior (Section 3.3)
and how classical Monte Carlo theory yields computable lower and upper bounds on the accuracy of
the flow matching approximation (Section 3.4).
3.1. Posterior sampling as a transport problem
The measure transport approach to computational Bayesian inference [EM12; Mar+17] seeks a map
TX pushing forward a tractable reference distribution η on the parameter space X—throughout a
standard Gaussian—to the posterior, TX∗η = πy, so that posterior sampling reduces to evaluating
TX at reference samples. For a fixed observation y such a map can be constructed variationally from
the unnormalized posterior density, e.g. within a class of monotone triangular functions [Mar+17].
The approach pursued here differs in two respects: the map is constructed from samples of the joint
distribution π of parameter and observation, i.e., of the prior and the prior predictive distribution,
rather than from density evaluations, and it is conditional, i.e., the observation enters as a parameter,
TX(· ; y). The theoretical basis is the following disintegration result; it is the finite-dimensional core of

Bayesian flow matching
Flow matching [Lip+23; LGL23; ABV23] retains the neural ODE repre-
sentation (9) but replaces the maximum-likelihood objective (8) by a least-squares regression that
requires no ODEs to be solved and no traces to be evaluated during training. Its conditional ex-
tension [Che+25; Hag25; WS25] is referred to as guided or Bayesian flow matching. To derive the
flow matching objective, let X0 ∼η and (X1, Y ) ∼π be independent2 and connect the endpoints by
linear interpolation in the parameter component,

Computational aspects
Applying flow matching for posterior sampling separates into two phases.
Offline phase (training): one generates joint samples (x(i)
1 , y(i)) ∼π by prior sampling and forward
simulation, and minimizes (15) by stochastic gradient descent over minibatches of (t, x0, x1, y). On-
line phase (sampling): given an observation y, draw x0 ∼η and integrate (9) with the trained field
vθ(·, · ; y) using a standard ODE solver; the resulting Tθ(x0; y) are independent draws from the flow
matching approximation of πy, generated in parallel at the cost of a few network evaluations each —
no forward-model solves, no likelihood evaluations are required anymore. When necessary, the den-
sity of the approximation is available through (11) at the cost of one further ODE solve. Pseudocode
for data generation, training and sampling is given in Algorithms 2 to 4 in Appendix B.
Remark 3.1 (Likelihood-free inference). Both training and sampling require only draws from the
joint distribution π, i.e., prior samples pushed through the forward model; the likelihood is never
evaluated. Bayesian flow matching therefore applies verbatim to simulation-based (likelihood-free)
inverse problems in which π(y|x) is intractable but forward simulation is feasible [CBL20; Wil+23;
Bap+24], such as the stochastic Lotka–Volterra model of Section 4.4. In contrast, the Metropolization
of Section 3.3 and the diagnostics of Section 3.4 require the unnormalized posterior density and are
hence available only for tractable likelihoods.
3.3. Flow-based independent Metropolis–Hastings
Bayesian flow matching provides an approximation to the posterior πy, which is easy to sample.
However, the samples generated by flow matching are biased whenever the minimum of (15) is
not reached — which is typically the case in practice.
Often it is hard to judge a priori how
close the flow matching approximation is to the true posterior πy for explicitly given data y. A
combination of flow matching and Metropolization can remove this bias and provide asymptotically

4. Numerical experiments
This section applies the conditional flow model presented in Section 3, trained using Bayesian flow
matching, to four example problems. The first example is a two-point boundary value problem with
two unknown scalar parameters for which the exact posterior and the KL divergence to the true
posterior can be approximated numerically. The second example is a stationary diffusion problem in
one space dimension where the goal is to reconstruct a distributed diffusion coefficient. Thirdly, we
consider the Bayesian formulation of electrical impedance tomography (EIT) [DIS17], a well-known
challenging problem in the inverse problems community. Finally, we consider a stochastic Lotka–
Volterra problem which poses the challenge that no likelihood is available and which was previously
considered in [Wan+25], following the setup in [Wil12].
For all four problems, we compare the learned posterior qualitatively and quantitatively against
MCMC references where applicable.
Furthermore, we study the influence of issues such as the
concentration of the posterior and the likelihood of an observation under the prior on the learned
posterior.

4.3. Electrical impedance tomography
To illustrate the viability of our approach on an inverse problem known to be challenging, we consider
electrical impedance tomography (EIT), a severely ill-posed problem in which a spatially distributed
electrical conductivity is recovered from a small number of measurements at boundary electrodes.
We adopt the rigorous Bayesian formulation of [DS16] and work with all three prior distributions
studied there — log-Gaussian, level set, and star-shaped — extending the last from a single inclusion
to two. We refer to that paper for the underlying well-posedness theory and give here only the model,
the priors, and the parameters needed to reproduce our experiments.
EIT is also the example where both the benefits and limitations of amortization become clear: A
single conductivity image is one Bayesian inverse problem, but a clinical EIT device produces a new
frame tens of times per second, and each frame is a new observation under the same prior and the
same forward model. In this setting, a transport map needs to be trained only once, whereas a
Markov chain must be restarted for each frame

three priors, including the two under which IMH accepts almost no steps, and the low acceptance
there has two causes. First, y is out of distribution for both priors: its normalized radius (23) is
r(y) = 3.73 under the log-normal and r(y) = 1.65 under the level-set prior, against a training median
of about 0.97, and of the 2 × 106 training observations none and 530 (0.03%), respectively, lie as far
out, whereas under the star-shaped prior r(y) = 0.94 is typical, exceeded by 35% of the training
observations. Under the level-set prior this is visible directly in the fields: a prior draw covers about
half of the disk with inclusion (area fraction 0.500±0.046), while the true inclusions cover only 0.178.
The flow has therefore seen almost no data like y and has to extrapolate, and neural networks are
notoriously unreliable outside the range of their training data [Xu+21]: the training objective does
not constrain the learned velocity field there, so its behavior is set by the architecture rather than by
the data. Second, the level-set latent posterior is hard to match for any flow, even in distribution:
since σ depends on ξ only through the sign of G, πy is scale-invariant, and it is thin in every latent
direction, because at the interface even modes carrying a few percent of the prior variance decide the
sign; small latent errors of the flow therefore change the data misfit strongly, and the likelihood of its
proposals varies over many orders of magnitude. The acceptance rate certifies the flow as a sampler
of the latent posterior πy, not of the conductivity: scored on the identifiable inclusion probability p,
the level-set flow agrees with the pCN reference at a correlation of 0.975.
We discuss the out-of-distribution position of y separately, because it limits where amortized inference
can be applied. MCMC requires only that the prior have support where the posterior concentrates;
the likelihood supplies the transport. An amortized flow never sees the likelihood at training or test
time — the prior is its training distribution — so it requires prior mass there. Support is therefore
not enough: a prior can contain the truth exactly and still place almost no mass near it, and the
flow then has to extrapolate as under the other two priors. For the star-shaped prior we therefore
choose the size and center priors such that the truth is also a typical draw.
On observations drawn from the prior, which are in distribution by construction, the picture changes.
Over 20 prior-draw observations, IMH accepts 36.7%±2.5% (mean ± standard deviation) under the
log-normal prior and 26.2%±17.9% under the star-shaped prior, but only 0.11%±0.02%, and at most
0.15%, under the level-set prior (Table 3). For the star-shaped prior we exclude draws in which an
inclusion crosses the boundary of the disk and use the first 20 remaining ones. Under the log-normal
prior the low acceptance on y is therefore a consequence of its out-of-distribution position and not of
the flow, whereas under the level-set prior it persists for the second reason above. The wide spread

We consider the stochastic Lotka–Volterra model introduced in [Wil12], which [Wan+25] use as one

The authors of [Wan+25] compare a CCNF, trained by minimizing the expected negative log-

As discussed in Section 3.2, and motivated in [Lip+23; LGL23] for the unconditional case, flow
matching speeds up the training of continuous normalizing flows significantly, as it is simulation-free
and requires no ODE solves during training. In our experiments, this decreases the training time
from 25.5 minutes, using the exact setup4 of [Wan+25], to approximately 70 seconds, using Bayesian
flow matching (Section 3.2) and our own architecture for the velocity field. Both training times are
measured on our own hardware, an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 Ti SUPER, and the former is in line
with the training time reported in [Wan+25]. For the velocity field vθ(t, x; y) we employ a six-layer
MLP with swish activation and a hidden dimension of nhidden = 112.

MCMC methods, and in particular cannot compute the error measures derived in Section 3.4. Our
focus is a qualitative comparison against the results provided by [Wan+25] with the same amount

5. Discussion
In this paper we have discussed and evaluated Bayesian flow matching for approximate sampling
from posteriors occuring in PDE-based Bayesian inverse problems. We have derived computable a-
posteriori bounds on the TV distance and KL divergence between true posterior and flow matching
approximation which mainly were based upon the acceptance rate of a hybrid sampler — an IMH
using the flow matching approximation as proposal. The validity and accuracy of these bounds have
been verified in Section 4.1.
Moreover, we have compared flow matching against a state-of-the-art MCMC reference in various
settings in Section 4.2 and Section 4.3. Across these examples, whenever the true observations were
covered by the spread of observations within the training data, flow matching produced almost perfect
fits to the MCMC generated samples in terms of posterior means, standard deviations and quantiles
with negligible training and online cost compared to MCMC. However, the fit in TV distance can
be sometimes disappointing: for instance, in the setting of Fig. 6, flow matching reproduces the
posterior mean and the 95% band of DRAM samples to about 1% in relative L2-norm, yet accepts
only 5% to 11% (Table 1). The explanation for these contrary observations is that flow matching
can fit the marginals of the highly informed parameter directions rather poorly, which, due to their
26


comparably small posterior variance, do not significantly contribute to posterior mean and spread,
but differences in the marginals is strongly punished in TV distance. In general, we noticed in our
experiments that flow matching may suffer from large or accurate observational data. We have also
discussed possible remedies for this in terms of accounting for the very different posterior spread
across coordinates during training by weighting the loss with the (approximated) inverse posterior
covariance and/or to include an additional linear layer in the neural network architecture for the
velocity field, which can reproduce a Gaussian approximation to the posterior.
Another limitation of flow matching found in our studies is the low performance when the true
observations lie in the tails of the prior predictive distribution for the observables — a well-known
effect in machine learning known as out-of-distribution error. In these cases MCMC is to be preferred.
We note that, in principle, it can be checked whether the observations y at hand are sufficiently
covered by prior prediction by comparing the normalized radius r(y) of Eq. (23) with that of the
training observations.
However, in our experiments we also verified the great potential and two important advantages of
Bayesian flow matching over MCMC simulation: a) amortization and b) likelihood-free inference,
both of which were clearly demonstrated in Section 4.3 for EIT applications and Section 4.4 for a
stochastic Lotka–Volterra problem. In both problems the gold standard MCMC simulation would
have either been simply infeasible (Section 4.4) or computationally prohibitively expensive ( Section
4.3). Moreover, our hybrid method — IMH with flow matching proposal — is another promising
method combining, in a way, the best of both worlds: Due to Metropolization (asymptotic) unbiased-
ness is restored, and since the forward solves for the proposals are independent and can be carried
out in parallel, it can still be significantly faster than a single run of a classical MCMC method —
particularly since the flow matching-based IMH Markov chain showed outstandingly good mixing in
our experiments.
A theoretical foundation for flow matching-based posterior approximation in inverse problems is still
largely missing. The distances used here to judge a learned posterior, the total variation distance
bounded by the acceptance rate and the Kullback–Leibler divergence, are common choices in finite
dimensions but ill-suited to high- and infinite-dimensional settings: two measures on a function space
are easily mutually singular, which makes both distances degenerate even when the measures are
close in every observable sense. Further directions therefore include an error analysis of flow matching
in function space based on the Wasserstein distance, which does not have this defect; a systematic
comparison of the total cost of both approaches at matched computational budgets, including the
forward solves for the training data and the training of the network, which we have not attempted
since its outcome depends strongly on the available hardware; and, on the practical side, addressing
missing training support by combining the flow with adaptive error models [KS05; Cal+18], in which
training samples are drawn in a region localized around the observations actually encountered and
the flow is iteratively refined there, trading part of the amortization for accuracy.
