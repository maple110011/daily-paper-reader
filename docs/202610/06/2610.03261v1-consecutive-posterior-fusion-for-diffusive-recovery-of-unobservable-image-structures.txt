# ABSTRACT 

Solving severely ill-posed imaging inverse problems requires recovering image structures that are unobservable or weakly constrained by the measurements. Diffusion models provide expressive learned priors for inferring such missing information, while posterior sampling incorporates mea-surement consistency along the reverse process. Standard diffusion posterior samplers, however, rely on instantaneous measurement-aware estimates, without explicitly exploiting information carried by previous posterior corrections. We introduce Consecutive Posterior Fusion Denoising Diffusion Null-Space Models (CPF-DDNM), an inference-time strategy that fuses consecutive measurement-aware estimates to improve the diffu-sive recovery of unobservable image structures, without requiring retraining or additional denoiser evaluations. We instantiate this principle within DDNM, whose range/null-space decomposition reveals that consecutive fusion preserves the measurement-determined component while acting exclusively on the prior-driven null-space estimate. We thus provide a geometric interpretation of CPF-DDNM and a local error analysis that characterizes the optimal time-dependent fusion coefficient, including the extrapolative regime. Experiments on sparse-view and simulated low-dose computed tomography, as well as medical image super-resolution, show consistent improvements over DDNM and competitive performance against diffusion-based inverse solvers. 

Keywords Diffusion models, medical image reconstruction, inverse problems, null-space methods, posterior fusion 

# 1 Introduction 

Imaging inverse problems aim to recover an unknown image from indirect measurements related to it through a physical acquisition process. In many practical imaging settings, these measurements may be incomplete, undersampled, or affected by noise, making the reconstruction problem severely ill-posed [2]. Such situations arise, for example, in computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), inpainting, and super-resolution. In these settings, the acquisition process may not uniquely determine the image: undersampling leaves components that are unobservable from the data, while measurement noise further increases the uncertainty along weakly observable directions of the arXiv:2610.03261v1 [cs.CV] 2 Oct 2026 A PREPRINT - O CTOBER 5, 2026 forward operator. The quality of the reconstruction therefore depends critically on the prior used to infer the missing or poorly constrained information. Consider the noiseless linear imaging model y = Kx , where K is the forward operator modelling the imaging system, 

y contains the acquired data and x is the image to be reconstructed. Whenever K has a non-trivial null-space Ker( K)

and y ∈ Range( K), the set of data-consistent solutions is the affine space 

x = K†y + z, z ∈ Ker( K), (1) where K† denotes the pseudo-inverse of the forward operator, K†y is the minimum-norm solution determined by the measurements, and z is invisible to the acquisition operator. Infinitely many data-consistent reconstructions can thus be obtained by varying z. In the presence of noise, this ambiguity remains, together with the additional uncertainty associated with small singular values of K.Classical variational methods resolve this ambiguity through handcrafted regularizers that promote properties such as sparsity, smoothness, or structural consistency [6, 11, 12]. Deep learning provides an alternative route by learning image statistics directly from data. In medical reconstruction, learned components have been employed as post-processing operators [13, 23], embedded in unrolled optimization schemes [10, 20], or combined with explicit model-based procedures [24]. More recently, diffusion generative models have emerged as expressive learned priors for inverse problems. Among the broad family of approaches employing diffusion models to drive the image reconstruction problem, in this work we focus on diffusion posterior sampling methods. These methods typically combine a pretrained generative prior with measurement-aware corrections throughout the reverse trajectory, thereby selecting plausible solutions that remain compatible with the observed data. This is done by adjusting the clean-image prediction ˆx0|t

obtained by the diffusion model at timestep t using a measurement-aware term, producing a correction Dt, which is then inserted into the reverse diffusion process in place of the original ˆx0|t. An important representative of the class of diffusion posterior samplers is the Denoising Diffusion Null-Space Model (DDNM) [27], which is particularly relevant because it makes the separation between measured and prior-inferred information explicit by defining its correction as 

Dt = K†y + ( I − K†K) ˆ x0|t, (2) where K†y is the component determined by the measurements and (I −K†K) ˆ x0|t is the null-space completion inferred by the learned prior. This structure is especially relevant for severely undersampled medical imaging problems, such as sparse-view CT and high-factor super-resolution, whose solutions depend strongly on the recovery of unobservable image components. However, a limitation of classical diffusion posterior sampling methods is that, particularly during the early timesteps, when noise dominates the reverse diffusion process, the correction terms Dt are volatile, sometimes causing the process to diverge towards an out-of-distribution solution. In our recent Lagged Multistep Posterior (LAMP) method [8], we showed that consecutive measurement-aware estimates are highly correlated and may therefore be combined to reduce their variance and stabilize the reconstruction trajectory. While LAMP provides a general mechanism, based on a multistep approximation of an underlying differential equation, for exploiting this interaction, it does not investigate how consecutive posterior corrections interact with the specific structure of the underlying inverse problem. In this work, we address this question within DDNM, whose explicit range/null-space decomposition makes it possible to identify where the use of consecutive posteriors acts. To this aim, we define 

eDt := Dt + βt (Dt+∆ t − Dt) , (3) where Dt+∆ t is the correction produced at the preceding reverse iteration and βt is the time-dependent fusion coefficient. We refer to this general corrective strategy as Consecutive Posterior Fusion (CPF) and we denote its application to DDNM as CPF-DDNM. In the following, we show that consecutive DDNM corrections Dt+∆ t and Dt share the same measurement-determined component K†y. Therefore, the CPF correction eDt in Eq. (3) preserves the component determined by the measurements and modifies only the prior-driven recovery of the unobservable image component. The range/null-space structure of DDNM, in addition, provides a direct geometric interpretation of the fusion mechanism and allows the role of the fusion coefficient to be analyzed specifically in terms of null-space recovery, including both interpolative ( 0 < β t < 1) and extrapolative ( βt > 1) choices. In particular, we investigate time-dependent coefficients that can move beyond the segment connecting consecutive null-space estimates while leaving the measurement-determined component unaffected. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows: • We specialize consecutive posterior fusion to the range/null-space decomposition of DDNM. We show that the fusion preserves the measurement-determined component and modifies exclusively the completion inferred by the diffusion prior. 2A PREPRINT - O CTOBER 5, 2026 • We study the extrapolative regime, interpreting this as an over-relaxation within the null-space, and derive a local optimality condition showing when an extrapolative coefficient can minimize the expected completion error. • We introduce time-dependent schedules for {βt}t∈[0 ,T ], and evaluate CPF-DDNM on simulated sparse-view CT, low-dose CT, and medical image super-resolution. CPF-DDNM improves the original DDNM baseline across all experiments, without retraining the diffusion prior or adding denoiser evaluations. The results also demonstrate competitive or superior performance with respect to other state-of-the-art diffusion-based inverse solvers. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews diffusion-based methods for imaging inverse problems. Section 3 introduces CPF-DDNM and presents its geometric interpretation and theoretical analysis. Section 4 describes the experimental protocol, and Section 5 presents and discusses the results. Finally, Section 6 summarizes the main findings and outlines directions for future work. 

# 2 Related Works 

Diffusion models have been widely adopted as learned priors for imaging inverse problems. Existing approaches differ primarily in how measurement information is incorporated into the reverse process. DDRM [16] derives posterior updates for linear degradation operators through their spectral decomposition. DPS [5] introduces approximate likelihood gradients and can address noisy and nonlinear inverse problems. DDNM [27] enforces measurement consistency through an explicit range/null-space decomposition, whereas DiffPIR [35] embeds a pretrained diffusion denoiser within a plug-and-play half-quadratic splitting scheme. Although these approaches implement data consistency differently, they all generate a sequence of measurement-aware estimates along the reverse trajectory. To speed up the generation process or improve the stability of the diffusion trajectory, several works have recently investigated multistep discretizations of the reverse diffusion dynamics [18,21,33]. These approaches exploit information from multiple diffusion steps to construct higher-order approximations of the generative trajectory. By comparison, the temporal relation between consecutive measurement-aware estimates in diffusion posterior samplers remains comparatively underexplored. A recent step in this direction is LAMP [8], which introduces a lagged temporal correction derived from a second-order discretization of the posterior dynamics. LAMP provides initial evidence that consecutive measurement-aware estimates contain useful information that is discarded by standard posterior-sampling updates. Its formulation is backbone-independent and was validated on DiffPIR and DDRM using a linear combination of consecutive estimates. However, due to the second-order discretization formulation, it always resulted in a convex combination of successive measurement-aware terms with coefficients βt ∈ [0 , 1] . On the other side, CPF-DDNM focuses on the extrapolative regime, where consecutive estimates define an over-relaxation direction within the null-space. Thus, the two methods exploit the same source of temporal information in different regimes and for different purposes. Diffusion models have also attracted growing interest in medical image reconstruction, where they provide flexible learned priors for highly ill-posed acquisition problems [17, 29]. Applications include sparse-view and low-dose CT, accelerated MRI, PET reconstruction, and multimodal imaging. In CT, diffusion-based methods have been proposed for sparse-view reconstruction and artifact reduction, including approaches that incorporate the acquisition model directly into the generative process [30, 32]. Related posterior-sampling strategies have been developed for accelerated MRI and joint PET–MRI reconstruction [1, 31]. Most of these works focus on modality-specific conditioning mechanisms, data-consistency operators, or posterior-sampling rules. CPF-DDNM is complementary to these developments: it modifies how an existing DDNM solver uses consecutive measurement-aware estimates, without retraining or changing the underlying diffusion prior. 

# 3 Method 

In this section, we present the proposed posterior fusion strategy for diffusion-based medical image reconstruction. The proposed CPF-DDNM method builds on the null-space formulation of DDNM and replaces the posterior estimate computed at the current reverse iteration with a fusion of two consecutive posterior estimates.

3.2 Diffusion posterior samplers 

The generative process described in Section 3.1 can produce high-quality samples, although it does not directly control which image is generated. Solving an inverse problem instead requires the generated image to remain compatible with the observed measurements. Diffusion posterior samplers address this requirement by introducing a measurement-aware correction at each reverse timestep, immediately after computing the clean estimate ˆx0|t. This correction progressively guides the reverse trajectory towards consistency with the measurements, while the diffusion prior complements the available information by estimating the unobservable component z ∈ Ker( K) introduced in Eq. (1). Consider a pre-trained diffusion model ϵΘ(xt, t ), trained in an unsupervised manner on a dataset representative of the images to be processed. Given an initial iterate xT ∼ N (0, I), the clean estimate ˆx0|t is computed at each timestep according to Eq. (6). Then, a measurement-aware correction operator C( ˆ x0|t, t, K, y) is introduced, whose formulation depends on the selected posterior-sampling method. We call the corrected image Dt, i.e. Dt := C( ˆ x0|t, t, K, y).Replacing ˆx0|t with Dt in Eq. (7) yields the diffusion posterior-sampling update 

xt−∆t = αt−∆tDt + σt−∆tϵΘ(xt, t ), (8) through which the diffusion trajectory is progressively conditioned on the acquired measurements. Iterating Eq. (8) until t = ∆ t yields the final reconstruction x0.Several posterior samplers can be interpreted within this measurement-correction framework, and the specific choice of C( ˆ x0|t, t, K, y) determines the diffusion posterior-sampling method. In addition, the precise point at which the correction is introduced further differs across methods. Focusing on the DDNM method proposed in [27], it adopts the correction described in Eq. (2). Denoting the projection operator onto the kernel of K as PKer := I − K†K [6], the DDNM correction reads: 

Dt = K†y + PKer ˆx0|t. (9) For the noiseless setting, this expression provides a clear geometric interpretation of the correction of ˆx0|t: the DDNM preserves only the estimated null-space component PKer ˆx0|t, while it replaces the range-space component of ˆx0|t with 4A PREPRINT - O CTOBER 5, 2026 the measurement-determined term K†y. When K† is the exact Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse and the measurements belong to the range of K, this construction enforces exact data consistency at every corrected estimate. Additionally, this explicit separation also makes the role of the diffusion model particularly transparent, since the learned prior affects only the image components that cannot be recovered directly from the measurements. Applying the same correction directly to noisy measurements may, however, amplify noise in the reconstruction. In an ill-conditioned problem, the pseudo-inverse strongly amplifies measurement components associated with small singular values of K, and the term K†y may consequently become unstable.

3.4 Insights on the fusion parameter 

The role of βt can now be interpreted geometrically within the null-space. We define the displacement between two consecutive null-space estimates as 

dt := zt+∆ t − zt. (17) Using this notation, Eq. (13) becomes 

ezt = zt + βtdt. (18) Hence, the pair (zt, zt+∆ t) determines a local direction dt within the null-space, while βt determines the position of the fused estimate along the affine line passing through these two estimates. Since the measurement-dependent component 

K†y is unchanged, this displacement affects exclusively the component that is not determined by the measurements. For 0 ≤ βt ≤ 1, the fused estimate lies on the segment connecting zt and zt+∆ t, and the fusion is therefore interpolative. In particular, βt = 0 recovers the current estimate zt, whereas βt = 1 selects the estimate zt+∆ t obtained at the preceding reverse iteration. When βt > 1, the fused estimate lies beyond zt+∆ t along the direction dt, and the update becomes extrapolative. In this regime, Eq. (18) can equivalently be written as

mt := ( βt − 1) dt = ( βt − 1)( zt+∆ t − zt). 4.2 Diffusion model and training 

As a diffusion prior for all the experiments, we consider the one released in [7], which was trained from scratch on the Mayo Clinic images. In particular, we employed a two-dimensional DiffusionModelUNet architecture provided by the MONAI Generative Models framework [3]. The network gets as input a batch of grey-scale 256 × 256 images and processes it via a four-level UNet architecture with 64 , 128 , 256 , and 512 feature channels, respectively. Each resolution level contains two residual blocks. As is common practice, we employed self-attention only at the two deepest resolution levels, where the spatial dimensionality is smaller and long-range dependencies can be modeled at a reduced computational cost. The attention layers use 32 channels per attention head. The network receives the noisy image xt together with the corresponding diffusion timestep t, embedded through a sinusoidal embedding layer, and predicts a single-channel estimate of the noise ϵΘ(xt, t ) appearing in Eq. (5). Training was performed using a discrete DDPM forward process with T = 1000 diffusion timesteps. We adopted a linear variance schedule, with β1 = 10 −4 and βT = 2 × 10 −2. At every training iteration, a clean image x0 and a timestep t were sampled, with t drawn uniformly from the 1000 available diffusion steps. Before applying the forward diffusion process, the image intensities, originally normalized to [0 , 1] , were linearly mapped to [−1, 1] . For any timestep t, a Gaussian noise realization ϵt ∼ N (0, I) was then generated and added to the clean image according to the noise level associated with the sampled timestep. The resulting noisy image was provided to the U-Net, which was trained to recover the injected noise by minimizing the mean squared error

4.3 Medical applications and imaging tasks 

We evaluate CPF-DDNM on two medical imaging inverse problems: computed tomography reconstruction and single-image super-resolution. For CT, we consider both sparse-view and low-dose acquisition settings. Although these problems differ in the way the measurements are degraded, they all contain image components that are either unobservable or only weakly constrained by the acquired data, and therefore require prior information to obtain a plausible reconstruction. 

4.3.1 CT image reconstruction 

Computed tomography reconstructs a cross-sectional image from projection measurements acquired at different viewing angles. 4.4 Pseudo-inverse computation and algorithmic implementation 

The proposed method requires the application of the pseudo-inverse K† as in (10). The way K† is evaluated depends on the structure of the forward operator, and this distinction is important for the inverse problems considered in this work. For super-resolution, the degradation operator is an average downsampling operator acting blockwise. In this case, the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of K is available in closed form, since each low-resolution pixel corresponds to the average of a non-overlapping high-resolution block. Therefore, the pseudo-inverse can be computed exactly by applying the corresponding blockwise SVD. This allows us to implement the DDNM correction with an exact K† in the super-resolution experiments. For computed tomography, the forward operator K maps an image into its corresponding sinogram. At the image sizes considered in this work, the matrix representation of K is too large to be explicitly formed and factorized, and therefore the action of the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse cannot be computed through an explicit SVD. Therefore, rather than exactly constructing K†, we approximate its action numerically using the conjugate-gradient least-squares (CGLS) algorithm initialized from the zero image. CGLS requires only applications of the forward operator K and its adjoint K⊤, and is therefore well suited to large-scale tomographic reconstruction problems. 5.2 Ablation Study on the Fusion Coefficient 

We investigated the influence of the fusion coefficient βt on the reconstruction dynamics by considering both constant coefficients and time-dependent schedules. The analysis was performed on sparse-view CT reconstruction with 45 

projection angles. For each choice of βt, we monitored the evolution of SSIM, LPIPS, PSNR, and the squared data residual along the reverse diffusion trajectory averaged over the training set. Following the geometric interpretation introduced in Section 3.3, we first considered the constant values 

βt = β, β ∈ { 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5}.

Here, β = 0 corresponds to the non-fused DDNM approach, values in (0 , 1] reflects an interpolative regimes, whereas 

β = 1 .5 produces an extrapolative update. We additionally evaluated three time-dependent schedules. Let 

st = 1 − tT ∈ [0 , 1] (39) denote the normalized progress of the reverse diffusion process. Thus, st = 0 at the beginning of sampling, when 

t = T , and st = 1 at the end of the reverse trajectory, when t = 0 . The three schedules are defined as Schedule A: βAt = 1 − √st,

Schedule B: βBt = 1 .8 (1 − √st) ,

Schedule C: βCt = 1 .8 − 0.6√st.

All three schedules monotonically decrease the fusion coefficient as the reverse process approaches the clean image. However, they differ in the range of values attained by the fusion coefficient. Schedule A satisfies 

0 ≤ βAt ≤ 1,

and therefore remains interpolative throughout the reverse process. Schedule B starts from the extrapolative value 

βBT = 1 .8, crosses the threshold βt = 1 , and approaches zero toward the end of the reverse trajectory. Schedule C instead satisfies 

1.2 ≤ βCt ≤ 1.8,

and therefore preserves the extrapolative regime throughout the entire reverse trajectory while progressively reducing its strength. Figure 5 compares the reconstruction dynamics obtained with the different choices of βt. During the initial part of the reverse trajectory, the metric curves are very similar. At these early stages of the reverse process, characterized by high diffusion noise, the consecutive denoised estimates still contain incomplete structural information, and their fusion has only a limited effect. The differences among the configurations become more evident during the final reverse steps, when the posterior estimates are more informative and their displacement defines a more meaningful reconstruction direction. The constant choices βt ≤ 1 generally produce smoother trajectories than the extrapolative configurations. Since these choices do not introduce over-relaxation, their trajectories remain closer to those obtained with the standard DDNM update. A different behavior is observed for the constant coefficient βt = 1 .5 and for Schedule C, both of which remain extrapolative throughout the reverse process. In this regime, the additional term mt = ( βt − 1) ( Dt+∆ t − Dt) moves the reconstruction beyond the preceding posterior estimate along the direction identified by two consecutive reverse iterations. This over-relaxed update produces visible oscillations in SSIM, LPIPS, and PSNR during the final part of the trajectory. # 6 Conclusions 

In this work, we introduced CPF-DDNM, a lightweight inference-time strategy for improving the prior-driven recovery of unobservable image components in DDNM-based inverse solvers. The method exploits information already available along the reverse diffusion trajectory by combining consecutive measurement-aware estimates, without modifying or retraining the diffusion prior. The range/null-space decomposition of DDNM provides a direct geometric interpretation of the proposed fusion. With an exact Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse, consecutive DDNM corrections share the same measurement-determined component and differ only in the null-space of the forward operator. CPF-DDNM therefore modifies exclusively the prior-driven component while preserving the information determined by the measurements. Thus, for noiseless data, CPF-DDNM preserves exact data consistency. Our local error analysis further provides conditions under which the extrapolative optimal fusion coefficient can yield a small null-space recovery error. Experiments demonstrate that the benefits of consecutive posterior fusion depend on both the forward operator and the fusion schedule. The largest gains were observed in sparse-view CT, where CPF-DDNM consistently improved upon DDNM across acquisition geometries and noise levels, and outperformed the considered diffusion-based inverse solvers. In super-resolution, CPF-DDNM improved structural similarity over DDNM, although DiffPIR achieved better average performance for some metrics in the noisy setting. The method was effective with both numerically approximated pseudo-inverses in CT and an analytically available pseudo-inverse in super-resolution. CPF-DDNM requires no additional denoiser evaluations or pseudo-inverse operations compared with DDNM, adding only the storage of the preceding correction and a linear combination. The main remaining limitation is the selection of the fusion schedule, which is currently determined on validation data and depends on the forward operator. Future work will investigate adaptive inference-time strategies based on quantities such as consecutive correction variations and data residuals, as well as plugging the CPF strategy to different posterior sampling methods. 18 A PREPRINT - O CTOBER 5, 2026
