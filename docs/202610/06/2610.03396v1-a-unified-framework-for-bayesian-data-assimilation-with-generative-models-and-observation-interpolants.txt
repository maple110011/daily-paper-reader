ABSTRACT
Bayesian data assimilation combines model forecasts with noisy observations, but
sampling high-dimensional, non-Gaussian posteriors remains challenging. We
introduce an observation-interpolant framework that turns pretrained stochastic
interpolant, flow matching, and diffusion models into posterior samplers with-
out retraining. Conditioning the interpolant path on observations yields a shared
likelihood-score correction to the drift or velocity, unifying stochastic and de-
terministic posterior sampling. The resulting SDEs and ODEs sample the exact
posterior when the intermediate likelihood score is known. For practical compu-
tation, we approximate this score using a closed-form Gaussian surrogate with
a bias-corrected mean and covariance inflated by the model’s source covariance.
Jacobian-free and ensemble-shared approximations make the method tractable
in high dimensions. We evaluate the framework on linear–Gaussian dynamics,
stochastic two-dimensional Navier–Stokes, and urban airflow with up to O(104)
degrees of freedom.

INTRODUCTION
Data assimilation (DA) estimates unknown states of dynamical systems by combining prior model
predictions with noisy observations. Bayesian DA combines these sources of information in a
posterior distribution, which can be approximated by an ensemble of samples. Ensemble Kalman
filters provide an efficient approximation but rely on Gaussian assumptions, whereas particle filters
are more general but typically require many more samples (Evensen et al., 2022). Neural network
surrogates can be used to accelerate the forward model (Mücke et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2023; Chen
et al., 2023), but the learned surrogates themselves are typically deterministic and therefore require a
priori model-error distributions that are hard to specify for high-dimensional and nonlinear stochastic
systems.
Generative models offer an alternative, by learning to sample directly from a distribution of interest.
Two flow-based families dominate: stochastic interpolants (SIs) (Albergo et al., 2025; Albergo
& Vanden-Eijnden, 2023; Chen et al., 2024), whose probability paths admit both ODE and SDE
samplers, and flow matching (FM) (Lipman et al., 2023), which is typically sampled through a
deterministic ordinary differential equation (ODE). Denoising diffusion (Ho et al., 2020; Song et al.,
2021) corresponds to SDE sampling of an FM-type model. In DA, and posterior sampling in general,
they are trained on the prior and modified at sampling time to incorporate observations. All three are
increasingly applied to physical forecasting, yet methods for turning them into posterior samplers
have been developed model by model, each tied to whether the generator is stochastic or deterministic.
A unified view. For data assimilation, we view stochastic interpolants, flow matching, and denoising
diffusion as different samplers within one posterior-sampling framework, rather than distinct methods.
Each can be described by an interpolant that connects a tractable source distribution to the transition

RELATED WORK
Flow-based generative models in physics applications. Flow-based generative models are increas-
ingly used for probabilistic physical forecasting, sampling the next state given the current one via
noise around neural-operator predictions (Lippe et al., 2023), latent autoregressive dynamics with a
diffusion decoder (Gao et al., 2024), or direct conditional sampling with denoising diffusion (Kohl
et al., 2026), flow matching (Fotiadis et al., 2025), and stochastic interpolants (Chen et al., 2024;
Mücke & Sanderse, 2026).
Posterior sampling and data assimilation with generative models. Generative models are in-
creasingly used to solve inverse problems (Daras et al., 2024). A common approach is to train
a flow-based model to sample from the prior and, at inference time, condition its dynamics on
sparse and noisy observations by modifying the velocity or drift through the score decomposition,
∇log pτ(x | y) = ∇log pτ(x)+∇log pτ(y | x). This principle underlies posterior-sampling meth-
ods based on diffusion models (Song et al., 2021; Chung et al., 2023; Bao et al., 2024; Qu et al., 2024;
Rozet & Louppe, 2023), flow matching (Pokle et al., 2024; Yan et al., 2025; Martin et al., 2025), and
stochastic interpolants (Chen et al., 2025). These methods differ mainly in how they approximate the
likelihood score. Most use a Tweedie-type estimate of the terminal sample and differentiate through
it to obtain the score (Song et al., 2021; Rozet & Louppe, 2023; Qu et al., 2024; Pokle et al., 2024;
Martin et al., 2025), while others combine related approximations with Monte Carlo estimation (Chen
et al., 2025). They also differ in their treatment of the likelihood covariance, using either the original
2


covariance (Chung et al., 2023) or an inflated one (Song et al., 2023). Closest to our work is Yan
et al. (2025), who interpolate the observations within a flow-matching formulation. In contrast, we
develop a unified framework for diffusion models, flow matching, and stochastic interpolants. We
interpolate the observations using the same schedules as the state, rather than evaluating the original
likelihood at a single estimated terminal state. Deriving the conditional source-noise moments then
gives a bias-corrected mean and inflated covariance for a common Gaussian likelihood surrogate
across all three samplers.

4.3
IMPLEMENTATION
All three samplers need only the pre-trained model and an ODE/SDE solver, and share the assimilation
loop in Algorithm 1. Recovered coefficients degenerate at τ = 0 (in particular, the FM guidance
representation is singular because β0 = 0), so the first step uses the prior drift only and the correction
is applied from τ = ∆τ onwards. Independent draws give an ensemble. Feeding each posterior
sample un back before assimilating yn+1 advances the autoregressive assimilation in physical time
n (see Figure 2). The solver and number of pseudo-time steps are chosen post-training, here forward
Euler for the guided ODE and Euler–Maruyama for the SDEs. Schedules, architectures, and training
details are in Appendix D.

Limitations. The exactness result assumes an exact prior and likelihood score, while the practical
method uses a Gaussian approximation, stopped covariance gradients, and approximate Jacobians.
These approximations can introduce bias, as seen for the Jacobian-free variant in the analytical and
sparse Navier–Stokes cases. The current formulation also assumes linear Gaussian observations,
and the shared covariance becomes costly as the observation dimension grows. Finally, one-step
autoregressive assimilation neither reweights past states nor uses future observations, so model
and approximation errors may accumulate over long rollouts. Extending the method to nonlinear
observations, smoothing, and broader model mismatch remains future work.

IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
Interpolant schedules.
SI priors use the quadratic schedule ατ = 1 −τ, βτ = τ 2, γτ = 1 −τ (so
στ = (1 −τ)√τ), and the FM priors use linear schedules with only a noise source term, ατ = 0,
βτ = τ, στ = 1 −τ.
16


Pseudo-time integration.
Sampling uses M uniform pseudo-time steps: Euler–Maruyama for the
SDE members and forward Euler for the guided ODE.
Architecture.
The field-case priors (SI drift and FM velocity networks alike) are conditional U-
Nets: four resolution levels with two ConvNeXt-style blocks per level (inner channel multiplier 2),
circular padding, and multi-head spatial attention (four heads, rotary positional embedding) in the
bottleneck. Pseudo-time enters through a learned scalar embedding, and the five most recent physical
states are supplied as additional input channels to the first convolution, realising the conditioning on
un−1 (and a short history). The initial conditioning history consists of five consecutive states from
the trajectory supplying the initial condition. Thereafter, each ensemble member updates its own
history with its generated states. Channel widths per level: Navier–Stokes (8, 16, 32, 64) for the SI
prior and (16, 32, 64, 128) for the FM prior, one field channel (vorticity). Urban (32, 64, 128, 256),
four field channels (three velocity components and temperature), with solid cells excluded via the
building mask. Hyperparameters were chosen based on hyperparameter tuning.
Training.
All priors are trained by simulation-free regression of their target (SI drift or FM velocity)
under an MSE loss, with per-channel standardised fields: AdamW (learning rate 10−4, weight decay
10−6) with cosine annealing to 10−5, batch size 32, up to 103 epochs with early stopping, dropout
0.1, adaptive EMA-based gradient clipping, and mixed precision. Navier–Stokes uses 160 training
trajectories and 20 validation trajectories for the early stopping. The urban case uses 150 LES runs for
training and 20 runs for validation. The same trained FM checkpoint per case serves every FM and
DM sampler and the same trained SI checkpoint serve every SI sampler. No prior is ever retrained or
fine-tuned for assimilation.

METHOD DESCRIPTIONS AND COMPARISON TO ALTERNATIVES
Our method shares building blocks with the baselines below but assembles them differently. For
each method we state how the state is evolved, how the likelihood term is approximated, the per-step
network cost, and which hyperparameters were tuned; quantitative comparisons are in Section 5.
Tuned hyperparameters were selected by RMSE sweeps per observation scenario (and, where
indicated, per step count M) on a held-out trajectory not used for evaluation; Table 3 collects all
sampler hyperparameters. Throughout, ˆu1
τ = E[x1 | xτ, u0] denotes the one-step denoiser.
FlowDAS.
FlowDAS (Chen et al., 2025), commits to the SI-SDE and estimates the likelihood score
by Monte Carlo: the gradient of a softmax-weighted squared residual through J one-step predictions
ˆu1,j
τ , entering the drift with a tuned constant step size ζ in place of our analytic weight wτ. We
instead read the score off the closed-form observation interpolant, removing sampling variance and
per-step trajectory backprop; the trade-off is our Gaussian surrogate, whose bias is controlled by the
covariance choice rather than the number of samples. Cost: two forwards (prior drift and denoiser
endpoint) and one backward per step, independent of J. Key limitation: ζ is scenario-dependent
and may require tuning, and there is no inflated source covariance. In our implementation, stability
can also depend on the solver step count M, and overly large ζ can lead to divergence. Tuned: the
guidance step size ζ, per scenario and M; the number of one-step samples is fixed at J = 25.
Guided FM (FIG).
FIG (Yan et al., 2025) guides the deterministic FM probability-flow ODE
for static linear inverse problems: after each prior-velocity step, k inner corrector steps push the
state along H⊤times the normalised observation residual with magnitude c (1 −τ)/τ, using a
measurement interpolant equivalent to ours. Cost: one forward per step, no backward (the corrector
differentiates only through the linear H) – the cheapest guided sampler in the comparison. Key
limitation: treats xτ ≈x1, uses no covariance, and the corrector is not derived from a probability
path, so the guidance magnitude c(1 −τ)/τ is not data-adaptive. Tuned: the corrector scale c, per
scenario and M; the number of inner corrector steps k is set by the observation operator (sweep-
selected: k = 1 for super-resolution, k = 3 for sparse sensors), and the measurement-interpolant
noise is fixed to zero as in the original work.
SDA.
SDA (Rozet & Louppe, 2023) applies Tweedie-based guidance on a diffusion prior: the
gradient of a Gaussian likelihood at the Tweedie denoiser, taken through the network, with a ΠGDM-
style inflated isotropic covariance scaled by a tunable factor γsda. Its original form composes locally
learned segment scores into a joint trajectory score and conditions all states at once. We use its
single-window form and are autoregressive, keeping per-step cost and prior dimension fixed as the
horizon grows (but not propagating cross-window correlations as a joint smoother would). Cost:
two forwards and one backward per step. Tuned: the covariance inflation γsda, per scenario and M.
Key limitation: SDA is tied to the native reverse diffusion. Its bias therefore stems entirely from
the Gaussian–Tweedie likelihood surrogate, whose isotropic covariance carries no model source
information. Because the guidance weight is inherited from the fixed diffusion rather than derived
from a probability path, it does not extend to samplers with modified or vanishing diffusion (wτ̸ = g2
τ),
unlike Theorem 4.1.
18


D-Flow SGLD.
Original D-Flow (Ben-Hamu et al., 2024) optimizes the FM source x0 by gradient
descent through the full FM-ODE rollout, with prior regularization supplied implicitly by the learned
flow. D-Flow SGLD (Parikh et al., 2026), used here, instead runs a preconditioned SGLD chain over
x0 ∼N(0, I) through the rollout (6-step RK2, backpropagated with gradient checkpointing), with
energy given by the squared data misfit plus an explicit ℓ2 source penalty λ∥x0∥2; zero Langevin
noise recovers MAP-style source optimization. Cost: the entire rollout repeats at every SGLD step
(12 forwards and 12 backwards per step at our settings) – the only baseline backpropagating through
a full multi-step generative rollout, and by far the most expensive. Tuned: the SGLD step size η and
the source penalty λ, per case (in our sweeps η is the dominant knob); the Langevin noise scale and
the preconditioner constants follow the original paper.
SURGE.
SURGE (Wei et al., 2026) is a sequential Monte-Carlo wrapper: a guided reverse-SDE
proposal with Girsanov-corrected importance weights that telescope to the exact terminal likelihood,
making the target asymptotically exact as the number of particles N →∞. The guidance controls
proposal efficiency and therefore finite-N degeneracy. In our implementation, particles resample
when the effective sample size drops below N/2. We report it wrapping the FlowDAS and SDA
guidance. Measured implementation cost: one additional denoiser forward per step relative to
the wrapped method, with no additional backward pass. Tuned: nothing SURGE-specific – the
resampling threshold is fixed at ESS < N/2, and the wrapped guidance keeps the hyperparameters
tuned for its standalone counterpart (ζ, γsda). Key limitation: particle degeneracy in high dimension.
Diffusion prior for DM-based methods.
SDA, SURGE, and DM-SDE require a diffusion prior;
we construct it from the trained FM velocity via the velocity–score identity (Appendix A), so all
generative methods use the same network.

The trajectories are simulated with JAX-CFD (Kochkov et al., 2021) with a pseudo-spectral (Fourier)
discretisation of Eq. (18) on a 2562 grid over the torus T 2 = [0, 2π]2, with viscosity ν = 10−3
and linear drag α = 0.1. Time integration uses forward Euler–Maruyama on the vorticity Fourier
coefficients with a fine step of dt = 10−4. Initial conditions are random divergence-free velocity
fields with peak wavenumber k = 4 and maximum velocity 7.
Each trajectory is integrated for 500000 time steps, corresponding to a time horizon of T = 50,
and stored every 0.5 time units, i.e at every 5000 time step. This yields 100 coarse snapshots per
trajectory, of which the first 50 are discarded so that the retained states are statistically spun up.
The stored fields are finally down-sampled from 2562 to the 1282 resolution used for training and
assimilation, so that the observation operators of Section 5 act on a 1282 state.

URBAN AIRFLOW TEST CASE: SETUP AND ADDITIONAL RESULTS
This appendix provides additional information and results for the urban-airflow case, including
qualitative reconstructions and error trajectories over 145 forecast steps. The reported run uses one
held-out trajectory, E = 64 ensemble members, M = 50 sampler steps, and sensor densities of
1.5625% and 0.78125% of fluid cells. Starting from a different held-out flow state, observations are
assimilated every third forecast step, for 49 posterior updates. Metrics are evaluated at every step
over fluid cells only, with buildings masked. Due to computational complexities, this case includes
only solver-free generative methods; no conventional-filter or KL reference is reported.
The uDALES solver.
The data are generated with uDALES (Suter et al., 2022), an open-source,
building-resolving large-eddy simulation code for urban microclimate and air quality derived from
DALES. It solves the filtered incompressible Navier–Stokes and scalar-transport equations on a
staggered Cartesian grid, using the Vreman model for subgrid stresses and an immersed-boundary
representation of buildings. Momentum, temperature, and building-surface energy are coupled within
a periodic 128 × 128 × 128 m3 domain discretized at 1 m resolution.
Qualitative reconstructions.
Figures 15–18 show the true field, observations, reconstruction,
uncertainty, and error for each retained method. Each figure corresponds to one variable and sensor
density. As in the Navier–Stokes figures, we omit the DM-SDE variants and show only the SURGE
versions of FlowDAS and SDA.
