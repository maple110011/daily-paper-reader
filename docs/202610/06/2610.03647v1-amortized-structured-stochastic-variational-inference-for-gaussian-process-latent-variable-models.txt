Abstract
Many machine learning methods aim to approximate the lower-dimensional mani-
fold on which the data lives. A desirable feature of such methods is that they should
capture the epistemic uncertainty of this learned manifold. One model that achieves
this is the Gaussian Process Latent Variable Model, in which a Gaussian Process
(GP) mapping from the latent space provides an estimate of the uncertainty of the
manifold. However, the effectiveness of this uncertainty estimation is limited by
the mean-field variational approximation between the GP inducing points and the
latent variables. In this work, we apply Amortized Structured Stochastic Variational
Inference to allow the variational posterior for the latent space to be conditionally
dependent on the value of the inducing points. We demonstrate that this more
flexible variational posterior improves several metrics relating to the reconstruction
of points on the data manifold.
1
Introduction
In many real-world settings, observed high-dimensional data has a complex structure that can be
naturally explained by a smaller number of unobserved factors. This general formulation is relevant
for a broad class of models, starting from classical models, such as Probabilistic Principal Component
Analysis [PPCA; Tipping and Bishop, 1999], to modern deep generative models, such as Variational
Auto-Encoders [VAEs; Kingma and Welling, 2014]. These approaches can uncover interpretable
low-dimensional structure and enable downstream inference tasks.
Unfortunately, in many practical tasks where learning a data manifold is desirable, the data is likely
to be both noisy and limited in number. In such cases, it is important to learn not only point estimates
for the data manifold, but also to estimate the epistemic uncertainty of the manifold. One model that
can achieve this is the Gaussian Process Latent Variable Model [GPLVM; Lawrence, 2005, Titsias
and Lawrence, 2010, Damianou et al., 2016]. Thanks to its non-parametric Bayesian approach, this
method can both learn arbitrarily complex data manifolds and remain robust to overfitting. These
properties have allowed the GPLVM and its variants to be applied to a diverse set of applications,
including biology [Reid and Wernisch, 2016, Ahmed et al., 2019, Sedgwick et al., 2025], chemical
engineering [Odgers et al., 2025], causal learning [Dhir et al., 2024, 2025], and robotics [Jaquier
et al., 2024, Rozo et al., 2025].
However, all approaches to the GPLVM share a shortcoming: a Mean Field (MF) approximation in
the variational family. Specifically, current methods approximate the posterior of the positions of the
data in the latent space and the mapping from the latent space to the observations to be independent of
one another [Lalchand et al., 2022]. This approach results in the model underestimating the epistemic
uncertainty in the GP mapping. In this work, we apply Structured Stochastic Variational Inference
[SSVI; Hoffman and Blei, 2015] to introduce dependence of the latent variable on the Sparse GP
inducing points, thereby improving the approximation to the true posterior.

Background
The problem of learning the latent structure from high-dimensional data has been studied for a
significant amount of time [Tipping and Bishop, 1999]. We follow a standard setup by assuming that
we have high-dimensional observations, yn ∈RP , which we seek to describe by a lower-dimensional
latent variable, xn ∈RQ, where P ≫Q. We assume that there exists a mapping f : RQ →RP ,
such that the relationship between xn and yn is
yn = f(xn) + ϵn,
(1)
with ϵn ∈RP representing random observation noise. For the duration of this paper, we assume
it follows the distribution ϵn ∼N(0, σ2IP ), and place a prior on xn ∼N(0, IQ). For notational
convenience, we will also use fn = f(xn) ∈RP .
We assume that we have a large number of high-dimensional data points, Y ∈RN×P which are
associated with values for the latent variables, X ∈RN×Q, and function values, F ∈RN×P . The
matrices are constructed such that xn, fn, and yn are the nth row of X, F, and Y respectively. We
assume that we have observed Y , and that the goal of our probabilistic model is to infer probability
distributions for the possible values of X and F.
We take a Bayesian approach in this paper, ideally aiming to learn a posterior distribution p(F, X|Y ).
This requires us to specify a prior for both the function and the latent variables. While the prior over
the latent space can be used to encode useful information [Damianou et al., 2016], we will use an
independent spherical Normal prior for each xn, so
p(xn) = N(0, IQ).
(2)
Instead of attempting to learn a parametric representation of f(·), we model it using Gaussian
Processes (GPs). GPs are a non-parametric Bayesian technique in which each finite set of function
values is assumed to follow a Multivariate Normal Distribution [Rasmussen and Williams, 2008]. We
apply an independent zero-mean GP prior over each of the output dimensions of our high-dimensional
observation, i.e., we take fp ∈RN to be the pth column of F and use the prior
p(fp|X) = N(0, KXX).
(3)
The covariance matrix, KXX is given by a kernel function, [KXX]ij = k(xi, xj), which controls
properties of the function and can be fit to data using the marginal likelihood [Rasmussen and
Williams, 2008], which, following Titsias and Lawrence [2010], we assume to be an Automatic
Relevance Determination (ARD) kernel for this paper.

In order to approximate the uncertainty in the latent space, we need to use a Variational Inference
(VI) approach. VI is a method in machine learning and statistics that approximates the posterior by
minimizing the KL divergence between an approximate posterior, q(·), and the true posterior [Blei
et al., 2017]. This is achieved by maximizing an objective known as the Evidence Lower Bound
(ELBO). To apply Variational Inference to GPs, it is necessary to augment the GP with a set of fake
data points, known as inducing points [Titsias, 2009, Hensman et al., 2013]. These inducing points
are drawn from the stochastic process but are not associated with any observation. Mathematically,
we augment each of the P independent GP mappings from the latent space to one dimension of
the output space, fp : RQ →R, with M additional points. Each output dimension’s M inducing
points have inputs in the latent space Zp ∈RM×Q and random output variables up ∈RM. For
practical reasons of saving compute, each of the Zp inputs is assumed to have a single value, so
Z = Z1 = · · · = ZP . For notational convenience, we also introduce U ∈RP ×M, which is the
matrix formed by stacking the set of the output vectors, up.

mapping and data points in the latent space. As VI is generally mode-seeking [Minka, 2005], it tends
to underestimate the epistemic uncertainty of the data manifold.

Structured Stochastic Variational Inference for GPLVMs
Although the MF-GPLVM provides a tractable ELBO, which has been shown to achieve a good
approximation of the latent space, the MF assumption is a strong and incorrect one. In essence we are
asserting that the latent variable posterior, q(X), should not depend on the mapping from the latent
space to the observed space. This seems intuitively incorrect, and the more precise mathematical issue
can be seen in the expression for p(fp|up, X) in (6). In that equation, the mean of the distribution of
fp depends on both the precise value of the inducing points, up, and the kernel matrix, KXZ. After
conditioning on the observed data through the likelihood, p(yn|fn), the posterior distribution for U
and X becomes conditionally dependent.
The main contribution of this paper is to allow the variational posterior to capture this dependence, by
relaxing the MF assumption explained in Equation 7. We do this by applying Structured Stochastic
Variational Inference [Hoffman and Blei, 2015], a general framework that relaxes the independence
assumptions of MF inference. The insight of SSVI is to take the conditional independence structure
of the full posterior and mirror it with a conditional independence structure of the variational posterior.
Concretely, the variational posterior is assumed to have the factorization

where q(up) = N(µp, Σp), with µp, Σp being constant values, but now making the parameters
of q(xn|U) = N(µn, Σn) functions of the inducing points, i.e. (µn, Σn) = h(yn, U) with h :
RP × RM×P →RQ × RQ×Q. The goal of the VI procedure is now to learn the constant values
µp, Σp, as well as the mapping h(·, ·) which most closely matches the true posterior.

3.1
Local and Global Variables for Stochastic Variational Inference
One important choice of our variational family which we have not discussed so far is the choice to
specify our variational posterior as q(X|U)q(U), rather than q(U|X)q(X). The structure of the exact
posterior can be written equally well in both forms: p(X, U, F|Y ) = p(F|U, X)p(U|X)p(X) =
p(F|U, X)p(X|U)p(U). Instead, the reason to prefer q(X|U)q(U) over the alternative is computa-
tional.
We are interested in developing a method which can scale to a larger number of observations than
can reasonably be expected to all be fit into memory at once. To do this, we need to use Stochastic
Variational Inference [SVI; Hoffman et al., 2013]. SVI relies on models whose variables can be split
into two sets: local variables, which are associated with individual data points, and global variables,
which are associated with many or all data points. The key insight of SVI is to realize that it is
possible to get an unbiased estimate of the gradient for the parameters of the global variables by using
only a subset of the data. Structured Stochastic Variational Inference [Hoffman and Blei, 2015] relies
on this same insight, but with additional dependencies in the variational posterior.
In GPs, U are the global variables shared across the full dataset [Hensman et al., 2013], and in
GPLVMs the local variables are latent variables, xn. This means that the ELBO decomposes into a
sum of per-data point contributions, which in turn allows us to estimate LS using only an index set I,

Experiments
We compare our extension of the GPLVM framework, the Amortized-Structured-GPLVM (A-S-
GPLVM), which applies SSVI with amortization of the latent variables, to variants of the mean field
approach. On the smaller scale datasets we compare against two inference strategies: the MF-GPLVM
with SVI, which directly approximates the MF-GPLVM variational posterior by optimizing the param-
eters of the latent variable distribution {µn, Σn}N
n=1 and the inducing point variational distribution
{µp, Σp}P
p=1, and the Amortized-MF-GPLVM (A-MF-GPLVM), where the latent variables are also
amortized. We base our implementation of these on Lalchand et al. [2022]. The initialization is the
same for all models, and is reported in Appendix D. A Bayesian diagram for the A-MF-GPLVM
and A-S-GPLVM is given in Figure 6, in Appendix A. For larger datasets, we do not include the
non-amortized MF-GPLVM.
7


Motivated by the alternative bound-tightening approach discussed in Section 4, we additionally com-
pare against an amortized importance-weighted variational GPLVM (IWVI-GPLVM), following the
importance-weighted approach of Salimbeni et al. [2019]. This baseline retains the same underlying
sparse GPLVM model but improves the variational objective through importance weighting rather
than introducing the explicit posterior dependence used by A-S-GPLVM. It therefore allows us to as-
sess whether the benefits of structured inference persist when compared with an alternative approach
to tightening the variational bound. We use the same model and optimization hyperparameters as for
the other amortized models, with the additional IWVI-specific parameter, the number of importance
samples, set to 5.
Data setup.
The experiments are conducted on four standard datasets: two small-scale ones and
two larger datasets. Our small-scale datasets are the Oilflow dataset, consisting of 1000 twelve-
dimensional data points belonging to three classes corresponding to different phases of oil flow in a
pipeline [Bishop and James, 1993], and the qPCR dataset, consisting of 48-dimensional single-cell
data obtained from mice with 10 classes [Guo et al., 2010]. For our larger-scale experiments, we
use the MNIST [LeCun et al., 1998] and Fashion MNIST [Xiao et al., 2017], which we subsample
to contain 1500 examples from each of the 10 classes. Code to recreate the Oilflow experiment
using the A-S-GPLVM model is provided in the accompanying repository (see https://github.com/
jamesacodgers/conditional_overlaping_gaussian_processes).
Evaluation Metrics.
To evaluate the quality of our posterior approximation, we use five metrics.
First, we use the negative ELBO from our model, which should give a lower value if our posterior is
able to achieve a value closer to the true posterior. As the ELBO is also influenced by hyperparameters,
which can differ between models, we also use additional metrics to evaluate the reconstruction quality
of our learned data manifold. We define our variational reconstruction distribution with

for each of our data points. We report two values calculated from this: the Negative Log Predictive
Density (NLPD) of q(yn) evaluated at the true data point, and the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)
between the true observation yn and the mean of q(yn).
To evaluate predictive uncertainty more directly, we additionally report the Continuous Ranked
Probability Score (CRPS) and empirical coverage of marginal 95% posterior predictive intervals.
CRPS evaluates the full predictive distribution and rewards both calibration and sharpness, with
lower values indicating better predictions. Empirical coverage measures the fraction of observed
values contained in the corresponding 95% predictive intervals; values closer to 0.95 indicate better
calibration.
We also split our data into training and test sets. The training set is the data used to train the GP
hyperparameters, the inducing point parameters, and either the values of the latent variables or the
amortization network for the latent variables. The test data were not seen during training, and we use
this data to evaluate if our model can generalize well to unseen observations. For the smaller datasets,
we use a 90/10 train–test split, while for the MNIST and FMNIST datasets, we used 1000 training
points and 500 test points per class.
Data pre-processing.
For all datasets, we apply mean-centering as a preprocessing step. Specif-
ically, each observed dimension is centered by subtracting its empirical mean, computed over the
training set.

While the reconstructions are an important measure of latent space estimation quality, a specific
advantage of GPs is their ability to increase epistemic uncertainty away from the data. We visualize
this in Figure 5, which shows a slice of the prediction of the GP from the latent to one of the observed
dimensions along the single most informative latent coordinate, i.e., the coordinate with the shortest
GP length scale, with other coordinates fixed at zero. Figure 5 shows that the MF-GPLVMs have
a slow increase in the epistemic uncertainty of the GP mapping between the region where there
is a significant probability of observing a latent variable, roughly corresponding to [−2, 2] on the
x axis, and the region where there is a low probability of observing a latent variable. In contrast, the
uncertainty of the GP mapping learned by the A-S-GPLVM increases much more rapidly away from
the high-probability inputs. We show similar plots for four output dimensions of the Oilflow dataset in
Figure 10, Appendix I. We do not expect the A-S-GPLVM to achieve a perfect estimation of the exact
epistemic uncertainty of the posterior due to the approximation on the GP mapping [Wenger et al.,
2022, 2024] and other restrictions of the variational posterior. However, the A-S-GPLVM shows a
clear improvement in the epistemic uncertainty estimation and, as all of the methods presented here
share the same GP approximation, these results indicate that the coupling of the variational posteriors
for the GP and the latent space is directly responsible for this difference in epistemic uncertainty
estimation.

Limitations
Computational overhead.
Compared with the amortized mean-field GPLVM, the A-S-GPLVM
introduces additional computational cost because the structured objective requires Monte Carlo
samples Uk ∼q(U), as in Equation 12. For each sampled global state, the encoder must evaluate a
conditional local posterior q(xn | Uk) and the expected likelihood must be evaluated. The K sampled
inducing-variable states can be processed in parallel, so the dominant practical cost is often increased
memory usage rather than a fully sequential increase in wall-clock time. We empirically quantify this
in Appendix G, where we show that the A-S-GPLVM suffers a slight wall-clock slowdown, but a
significant increase in memory usage. Further work on how to informatively select samples of U, e.g.
via importance sampling, could help alleviate this cost.
Restrictive variational family.
A second limitation is that, while our variational posterior is
more expressive than the MF-GPLVM alternative, we still make strong assumptions on the form
of our variational posterior. For example, we assume q(xn | U) is a normal distribution with a
diagonal Gaussian covariance and q(U) = QP
p=1 q(up). As a result, we expect that the A-S-GPLVM
method proposed here still does not accurately match the exact posterior and further work on how to
effectively relax those approximations may yield similar improvements in performance.
7
Conclusion
This work introduces SSVI for GPLVMs by coupling the variational posterior of the latent variables
to the inducing variables. Across Oilflow, qPCR, MNIST, and FMNIST, the A-S-GPLVM achieves
statistically significant improvements in train and test CRPS over the A-MF-GPLVM. Its RMSE and
NLPD improvements are also statistically significant, except for RMSE on Oilflow.
Compared with IWVI-GPLVM, the A-S-GPLVM achieves significantly lower train and test NLPD on
all four datasets, and significantly lower RMSE and CRPS on MNIST and FMNIST. Results on the
smaller datasets are more mixed, and empirical 95% coverage does not show uniform improvement.
Together, these findings provide quantitative evidence that structured posterior dependence improves
the quality of the learned predictive distribution. The qualitative uncertainty plots additionally show
a more rapid increase in uncertainty away from the observed data (Figure 5), consistent with our
motivation to better capture epistemic uncertainty in the learned data manifold.
Future work will explore how to use the improved uncertainty of the data manifold. For example, as a
method of improving the sharing of information and uncertainty estimates in multiobjective Bayesian
optimization, as in Sedgwick et al. [2025].

Amortized Encoder
As noted by Hoffman and Blei [2015], the function h(·, ·) can be defined implicitly as h(yn, U) =
arg maxmn,Sn ˆLn(U), however, this requires solving a new optimization problem for q(xn|U) at
each training step and for each sample of U. To avoid this expensive step, amortized inference can be
used. In our testing, we found several design choices improved performance, which we detail below.
Encoder architecture.
In our experiments, we used two separate two-hidden-layer multilayer
perceptrons (MLPs), one to encode the mean and one to encode the log-variance. The rationale for
this was that the magnitudes of variation in optimal means and variances differ significantly.
Reparameterization of inducing variables.
Both of these networks received the same inputs. The
goal of these networks is to couple the information from the observation to the values of the inducing
variables to improve the representation of the latent space. The naive way to do this would be to pass
in the tuple (Uk, yn) for each observation and sample. While this approach is possible, we found that
at late stages of training, when the variational distribution of the inducing variables, q(U), had a high
degree of certainty, all of the samples of the inducing variables Uk lie very close to one another. In
order to give a better-conditioned input to the network, while preserving the information required to
couple the inducing points to the observations, we use the reparameterization trick to sample Uk.
For each sample of upk ∈RM, we sample a standard normal variable ξpk ∼N(0, I) which we
transform to be a sample from the variational posterior, q(up) = N(µp, Σp) using
upk = µp + Lpξpk,
(28)
where Lp is the Cholesky factor of Σp. The main benefit of the reparameterization trick is that
it allows gradients to propagate through samples of the global inducing variables U. However,
as an added benefit, the reparameterized input Ξk is well spread out over the input space, while
maintaining all of the sample-specific information required to couple the inducing sample to the
output and condition the latent variable on the inducing point samples. Therefore, we pass the variable
Ξk = {ξpk}P

Model Configuration
For the smaller datasets (oil flow, qPCR), the hyperparameters are:
• Latent Dimensionality (Q): 10 dimensions
• Inducing Points (M): 32 per output dimension
• Training Iterations: 100,000 for all four models
• Batch Size: 32
• Random Seeds: 0,1,2,3,4
• Initial lengthscales of 1.0 and observation noise variance of 1.0
• Number of samples from the inducing variables (K): 64
• Learning rate: 1e−3
• MLP hidden layer dims: (128, 64)

Statistical significance of the observed improvements
To assess whether the gains from the structured variational family are robust across random initializa-
tion and stochastic optimization noise, we performed a two-sided Welch’s t-test over 5 independent
runs, comparing A-MF-GPLVM and A-S-GPLVM on each dataset. The resulting p-values are
reported in Table 3.
Overall, the structured method yields statistically significant improvements on the probabilistic
metrics across all datasets. In particular, for qPCR all reported differences are significant at the 10−3
level or smaller, and for MNIST and FMNIST all reported differences are significant at the 10−4 level
or smaller. On Oilflow, both NLPD improvements remain highly significant (p < 10−3), and the
ELBO difference is also significant (p < 10−4), whereas the RMSE differences are not statistically
significant (p = 0.12 on the training split and p = 0.55 on the test split).
This distinction is important for interpreting the effect of the proposed method. In particular, the
Oilflow result shows that the structured posterior can significantly improve the probabilistic quality
of the model, as measured by NLPD and ELBO, even when the point-estimate accuracy measured by
RMSE does not change significantly. This supports our claim that the main benefit of the structured
variational family is in improving the posterior approximation and predictive uncertainty, rather than
merely shifting the predictive mean.

Runtime and memory overhead
The proposed structured approximation introduces two main sources of overhead relative to A-
MF-GPLVM. First, the encoder input becomes larger, since the sampled inducing-variable state is
concatenated with the observation rather than processing the observation alone. Second, each ELBO
evaluation uses K Monte Carlo replicas. Both operations are parallelizable, so the primary practical
trade-off is expected to be increased memory use rather than an inherently sequential increase in
computation.
To quantify this overhead empirically, we measured runtime and peak GPU memory on the Oilflow
dataset for 100 training iterations using a minibatch size of B = 32, M = 32 inducing points, latent
dimension Q = 10, 12 observed dimensions, K = 32 Monte Carlo samples, and float64 precision
throughout. The experiments were run on an NVIDIA RTX 2080Ti GPU (22 GB VRAM) with an
AMD EPYC 7282 CPU and CUDA 13.0. The results are shown in Table 4.
17


In this configuration, A-S-GPLVM increases the wall-clock time from 5.03 s to 8.36 s over 100
iterations, corresponding to an approximate 1.66× increase. In terms of memory, peak GPU usage
rises from 29.0 MB to 598.7 MB. This is consistent with the fact that the K inducing-variable samples
can be processed in parallel, so the dominant cost of the structured estimator is storing the replicated
computation graph and intermediate activations.
We emphasize that these measurements characterize the present implementation rather than an
unavoidable algorithmic constant. In our current codebase, the additional wall-clock overhead is
partly influenced by implementation details in a shared layered design across models. The more
stable conclusion is that the proposed method preserves the same sparse, minibatched stochastic
variational structure as amortized mean-field GPLVMs, but pays a memory cost in exchange for a
richer posterior approximation.
H
Ablation studies
To better isolate the source of the improvement in A-S-GPLVM, we carried out ablations on the
encoder architecture, the number of inducing points, and the number of sampled inducing-variable
states. The corresponding plots are shown in Figures 7, 8, and 9.
Encoder architecture.
We first varied the widths of the two-hidden-layer MLP encoder across
the settings [16, 8], [32, 16], [64, 32], [128, 64], and [256, 128] for both A-MF-GPLVM and A-S-
GPLVM. Across this range, the total encoder size did not have a strong effect on performance, while
A-S-GPLVM consistently outperformed A-MF-GPLVM on the probabilistic metrics (ELBO and
NLPD). This supports our claim that the main benefit does not come from increased encoder capacity.
Increasing encoder size in the mean-field model only makes the deterministic map yn 7→q(xn) more
flexible, whereas the structured model can adapt the local posterior to the sampled global GP state
through q(xn | U). In particular, even a small encoder with SSVI remains better than a larger encoder
with MFVI.
Number of inducing points.
We next fit the Oilflow dataset using 16, 32, and 64 inducing points.
Across all tested values, A-S-GPLVM performed better than A-MF-GPLVM on ELBO and NLPD,
and often performed comparably to or better than A-MF-GPLVM with twice as many inducing points.
We also observe that the margin between the two models becomes smaller with larger numbers of
inducing points. In our current implementation, we suspect this happens because the encoder input
dimension in A-S-GPLVM grows with the number of inducing points and becomes too large for the
small MLP used in this ablation.
Number of inducing-variable samples.
Finally, we varied the number of sampled inducing-
variable states K. In the ablation plots, we compare K = 4 and K = 64, and also show the effect
of using learning rates 10−3 and 3 × 10−4. Larger values of K lead to better results, which is
consistent with the reduced variance of the gradient estimator when more samples are used. In
memory-constrained settings, however, increasing K may not be possible. The ablation also shows
that for small K, lowering the learning rate can recover performance by countering the increased
gradient variance, at the cost of slower convergence.
Taken together, these ablations support the interpretation that the main gains come from the structured
variational family rather than from increased encoder capacity, while also clarifying the dependence
of performance on the number of inducing points and inducing-variable samples.
