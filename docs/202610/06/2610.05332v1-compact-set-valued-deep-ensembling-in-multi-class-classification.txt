Abstract 

This paper tackles visible challenges in deep ensemble learning, where deep neu-ral networks serve as ensemble members: training and storage burdens, and robustness of cautious (set-valued) predictions targeting multiple utilities, which may involve reward-sensitivity. To mitigate the training and storage burdens, we propose to employ compact ensembles, such as Bayesian Neural Networks and Convolutional Neural Networks with the Monte-Carlo dropout prediction option, to produce probabilistic predictions. For each query instance, these probabilistic predictions are then used to define a representative distribution optimizing some statistical distance. The representative distribution is then employed to define the Bayes-optimal prediction (BOP) of any utility. To address the potential unrobust-ness of singleton prediction making, we propose a family of set-utilities satisfying some desirable properties and whose set-valued BOPs can be found efficiently. Empirical evidence is then given to illustrate the potential (dis)advantages of the proposed ensemble learning framework. 

Keywords: Multi-class classification, Bayesian-optimal predictions, Reward-sensitive utilities, nested coarsening, Compact cautious deep ensemble learning 

1arXiv:2610.05332v1 [cs.LG] 4 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

Machine learning methods typically face multiple challenges or needs: obtaining high predictive power, being adaptable to different tasks without the need to retrain new models, and offering trustworthy predictions. In this paper, we propose (deep) prob-abilistic ensemble-based approaches combined with robust predictions to solve these issues. Ensemble learning is commonly used to boost the predictive performance and robustness of (probabilistic) predictors in multi-class classification (MCC), and in machine learning in general (Dietterich 2000; Nguyen et al. 2025; Zhou 2021). Ensem-ble learning typically consists of two phases: Generation and aggregation. During the former, a set of ensemble members is generated, where a trade-off between their accu-racy and diversity is sought. The latter is typically performed in an instance-wise manner, where the predictions of the ensemble members are aggregated into final predictions. Despite a rather rich literature in ensemble learning, there remain visible chal-lenges in both generation and aggregation phases when deploying ensemble learning in applications where deep neural networks serve as ensemble members. Regarding the generation phase, training and storage burdens are visible challenges when deploy-ing conventional ensemble learning techniques, such as bagging, boosting, stacking, and random initialization (Yang et al. 2023), which typically require one to train and maintain a reasonably large set of ensemble members to ensure diversity. Another fact is that modern machine learning models such as foundation models are trained to solve generic problems, without a pre-specified task for which they will be used. Their outputs should therefore be amenable to adapt to various tasks. Probabilistic predictions are ideal for that task, as one can then plug-in task-specific utility or reward matrix to maximize the expected utility. To obtain such a probabilistic prediction from ensembles, we propose to proceed in a two-step aggregation phase (Nguyen et al. 2025): for each query instance, the probabilistic predictions of the ensemble members are aggregated into a representative probability distribution. Task-specific utilities can then be used to define the Bayes-optimal prediction (BOP) of any utility (Del Coz et al. 2009; Mortier et al. 2021). Finally, in the case of high-stakes applications, one may wish to produce set-valued classification to avoid predicting a unique optimal prediction when this later one is not very robust to small changes in the produced probabilistic prediction. Indeed, obtain-ing reliable estimates of representative probability distributions may be challenging, especially in the presence of noisy training data or test data. One sound approach to gain robustness might be to produce set-valued BOPs of reward-sensitive set-utilities (See (Yang et al. 2016) and references therein), which satisfy various desirable prop-erties, which reflect different aspects of the trade-off between recall (including the right class in the set-valued prediction) and precision (producing small sets as pre-dictions). Unfortunately, we are unaware of reward-sensitive set-utilities that satisfy good properties and whose BOPs can be found efficiently. In this paper, we propose a compact cautious deep ensembling framework to tackle the mentioned challenges. Our contributions are as followed: 2• to mitigate the training and storage burdens of the generation phase and to target the optimization of multiple utilities during the aggregation phase, we propose to employ compact deep ensembles, whose ensemble members are essentially generated in an instance-wise manner during the aggregation phase by distorting a pre-trained deep neural network, to define the representative probability distribution from which a BOP of any utility can be defined. Concrete examples are Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) with the Monte-Carlo dropout prediction option (Lemay et al. 2022) and Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs) (Foong et al. 2020; Jospin et al. 2022); 

• to mitigate the potential unrobustness of singleton prediction making when taking into account reward-sensitivity, we propose a family of reward-sensitive utility-discounted accuracies, which satisfies a number of important properties discussed in (Yang et al. 2016) and references therein, and whose BOP can be found through computationally efficient procedures. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 recalls the basic setting of MCC, the notions of utilities and their BOPs. The compact cautious deep ensembling framework is presented in Section 3. Section 4 presents desirable properties and an analysis of the proposed family of reward-sensitive utility-discounted accura-cies. Section 5 provides empirical evidence to illustrate the potential (dis)advantages of the proposed ensemble learning framework on image data sets, where either the training set or the test data can contain noise. Finally, Section 6 concludes this paper and sketches out potential follow-up works. This paper is a significant extension of an earlier conference version (Tran et al. 2025), in which we only used our aggregation approach on a limited number of data sets with classical, singleton-valued decision making. The current version is more com-prehensive in multiple ways. First, the formulation of the family of reward-sensitive utility-discounted accuracies and the analysis of desirable properties and the compu-tational complexity of finding BOPs are entirely new. Second, the empirical study is much more comprehensive and covers various aspects that have not been considered in the short version, especially in analyzing the potential (dis)advantages of the pro-posed ensemble learning framework in the presence of reward-sensitivity and noisy data. All the proofs of formal results stated in this paper (propositions and remarks) can be found in the appendices. 

# 2 Probabilistic multi-class classification 

This section recalls the basic setting of probabilistic MCC and discusses potential challenges. Along the way, we will introduce frequently used notations and terminolo-gies. To facilitate the readability of this paper, some frequently used notations and acronyms are listed in the table A1 of Appendix A. 

## 2.1 Singleton prediction making 

In this section, we recall the standard setting where one wishes to predict for each instance a singleton prediction, i.e., a single class. 32.1.1 Reward-sensitive accuracies 

Let X denote an instance space and Y = {y1, . . . , y K } a finite set of classes. We assume that an instance x ∈ X is (probabilistically) associated with members of Y. Let X be a set of features. We denote by p(Y | x) the conditional distribution of Y given X = x.Given training data D = {(xn, y n)|n ∈ [N ] := {1, . . . , N }} drawn independently from 

p(X, Y ), the goal in multi-class classification (MCC) is to learn a classifier h, which is a mapping X −→ Y that assigns to each instance x ∈ X a class ˆ y ..= h(x) ∈ Y .The quality of h(x) is often assessed by using utilities u : Y × Y −→ R+, which are assumed, without loss of generality, to be the higher-the-better accuracies. Each utility u : Y × Y −→ R+ are often specified by a K × K-matrix Ri,j where the element ri,j := u(yi, y j ) is the reward of predicting yi while the correct class is yj . In the specific case of 0 /1 accuracy u0/1, we have u0/1(yi, y j ) = Ji = jK1.When u(yi, y i) ≥ u(yi, y j ), for any i ∈ [K] and i̸ = j, u is often referred to as a reward-sensitive accuracy (Elkan 2001; Lachiche and Flach 2003; O’brien et al. 2008). Reward-sensitive accuracies might be more informative in applications where the direct costs, such as the costs associated with the decisions the end-user should spend to treat the predicted class, are well-estimated. For example, if one wishes to construct a decision-support system for Plant Leaf Diseases (Geetharamani and Pandian 2019) treatment, where an Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV), which is equipped with a predictor h, visits different farms and calls for the visit of a domain expert when suspecting Leaf Diseases in a farm. Rather than employ one domain expert per Plant Leaf Disease, it might be more beneficial to employ one domain expert per Plant Type or group of Plant Types. Clearly, a wrong prediction might still be rewarded if it still calls for the visit of an appropriate domain expert. If the direct costs, such as the cost of sending the domain experts to the farms, are well-estimated, it might be leveraged to define reward-sensitive accuracies to better assess the quality of the predictor h.

## 2.2 Set-valued prediction making 

Set-valued prediction making has been perceived as a sound approach to mitigate the potential risk of making incorrect predictions of the singleton prediction-making strategy. In the following, we discuss its two basic building blocks: Reward-sensitive set-utilities and set-valued BOPs. 

2.2.1 Reward-sensitive set-utilities 

Set-valued prediction making assumes that the prediction can be any subset Y of Y.Let Yset := {Y | Y ⊂ Y , Y ̸ = ∅} . Any utility u : Y × Y −→ R+ can be generalized to assess the quality of set-valued prediction as U : Yset × Y −→ R+, where U ( ¯Y , y ) = 

u(¯ y, y ) whenever ¯Y = {y}. Each set-utility U : Yset × Y −→ R+ are often specified by a (2 K − 1) × K-matrix R ¯Y ,j where the element r ¯Y ,j := U ( ¯Y , y j ) is the reward of predicting ¯Y while the correct class is yj .Often, set-utilities are designed to assess the trade-off between recall, i.e., the set-valued predictions should have a high chance of covering the correct class, and recall, i.e., the cardinality of the set-valued prediction should not be unnecessarily large (Mortier et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2016; Zaffalon et al. 2012). Moreover, set-utilities and their optimal set-valued predictions are often expected to satisfy desirable properties, such as the ones discussed in (Yang et al. 2016) and others explored in Section 4. Since 2 K − 1 is exponential in K, specifying a (2 K − 1) × K-matrix R ¯Y ,j to define the corresponding set-utility U can be costly. Notable efforts on set-valued prediction making reduce this burden by assuming that the rewards of predicting any non-singleton prediction ¯Y can be specified using its cardinality | ¯Y | and assigned to its members ¯ y ∈ ¯Y . Representative examples of set-utilities of this type are utility-discounted accuracies (Mortier et al. 2021; Zaffalon et al. 2012), which are direct generalizations of the 0 /1 accuracy u0/1, where u0/1(yi, y j ) = Ji = jK. The choice of Gaussian noise was motivated by its popularity in real-world applications. First, Gaussian noise is often added to data as part of data augmentation to improve model robustness by exposing the model to varied and noisy inputs. Second, in various applications, Gaussian noise in digital images can arise during acquisition, e.g., sensor noise caused by poor illumination and/or high temperature, and/or transmission, e.g., electronic circuit noise. We think our choice of noise injection mechanism serves well to illustrate the potential applications of our generic compact cautious deep learning framework, which would be directly improved by taking into account current and emerging developments of compact ensembles and learning representative probability distributions. 

5.1.2 Reward-sensitive utility-discounted accuracies 

Inspired by related work on designing hierarchical utilities (see, e.g., (Urbani et al. 2024) and references therein), we propose to construct the reward-sensitive utilities u

based on the hierarchical representation of classes. More precisely, we first partition the classes into different groups, and assign to the correct predictions, wrong predic-tions within the same group, and predictions across groups, a reward of 1, 0 .25, or 0, respectively. In general, we rely on WordNet (Princeton University 2010) for the hierarchy of objects that are commonly used in the literature for the CIFAR-10 data set (Barz and Denzler 2019) and the Fashion-MNIST data set (Zhang et al. 2021). Regard-ing the CIFAR-10 data set, we also consider the specifications about the superclass 18 and classes in the official website (https://www.cs.toronto.edu/ ∼kriz/cifar.html) and divide the classes into two groups: animal and vehicle; similarly to Fashion-MNIST, we have three groups: clothing, footwear, and accessory. In the case of the Plant Leaf Diseases data set, we partition the classes into 15 groups accordingly to the plants species, one per plant: apple, blueberry, cherry, corn, grape, orange, peach, pepper, potato, raspberry, soybean, squash, strawberry, tomato, and an outlier. We refer to https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/tywbtsjrjv/1 for the 39 class names of this data set. We also conduct experiments with the conventional utility-discounted accuracies

Therefore, the end-user can flexibly customize the reward-sensitive utilities to better reflect the way (set-valued) prediction should be assessed in their application domains. 

5.1.3 Compact ensembles 

We shall end the experimental setting with descriptions of the two compact ensembles used to estimate the representative distribution (8). 

• BNNs: For each train-test split, we train a BNN, where all weights follow Gaus-sian distributions, on training data using the default setting recommended by the Bayesian-Torch library https://github.com/IntelLabs/bayesian-torch. For each test image, we feed the image to the BNN 100 times to estimate 100 probabilistic predictions, which are then used to estimate the representative distribution (8). 

• CNNs with the Monte-Carlo dropout prediction option : For each train-test split, we train a CNN on training data using the default setting recommended by the Bayesian-Torch library https://github.com/IntelLabs/bayesian-torch. For each test image, we perform Monte-Carlo dropout to estimate 100 probabilistic predictions, which are then used to estimate the representative distribution (8). To facilitate the diversity of the ensemble members, we adopt the dropout rate of 0 .2.

5.2.2 BNNs with noisy training data 

The results given in Fig. 4 when training data is noisy again show interesting results: 

• First, while there is indeed a small drop in performances, due to the lesser quality of instances, yet we can see that the results are almost similar for both noisy and clean test data. This is due to the fact that clean test data (the ”shifted” distribution in this scenario) are not likely to be out-of-distribution when the initial training is done on a noisy distribution, while noisy test data are likely to be OOD when the training set is clean. Note that for the Plan leaf data, the y-axis gap is very small compared than in Fig. 3, so there is no big differences; 

• Second, allowing for set-valued prediction is quite beneficial here, again with a relatively limited number of classes when returning set-valued predictions. Other remarks made for the clean training data remain valid for this scenario, notably the little differences observed in taking different distances. Overall, we can make some first conclusions before those of Section 6: the presented framework does allow improvement in recall and performances when allowing for set-valued predictions, and this is particularly true in noisy scenarios (either in the training or test data). It is robust, working equally well on different ensemble regimes as well as different distances to compute representative probabilities. However, its capacity to solve issues in data distribution appears limited when the shift between the training and test set is significant and produces many OOD samples: in this case, it seems that encoding robustness and set-valued approaches in utilities and in the decision steps has some positive impacts, but those remain limited. In such cases, it would probably be desirable to also consider approaches relying on ensembles and sets of probabilities (Wang et al. 2026; Nguyen et al. 2025)

5.2.4 Sensitivity to noisy data 

To seek insights about this behavior of BNNs and CNNs with the dropout prediction option when encountering mismatches between training and test data types, we report calibration errors, which are computed on the representative probability distributions provided by the two types of compact ensembles. The results given in Appendix C suggest that BNNs tend to have more promising calibration errors. This, in turn, suggests that the representative probability distributions provided by BNNs may be more reliable and thus facilitate the performance of making both singleton and set-valued BOPs when encountering the mismatches between the training and test data types. 

## 5.3 Practical recommendations 

We shall provide a few practical recommendations based on the results achieved so far. The theoretical results on the desirable properties and computational efficiency, together with the consistent ability to gain the trade-off between recall and precision, might indeed motivate the use of our proposal for making set-valued BOPs wherever this trade-off is beneficial. Moreover, we encourage the end-user to participate in the design of the reward-sensitive utility-discounted accuracies (17) by estimating the base reward-sensitive accuracies and selecting the reward level α. We further suggest that the end-user analyze the trade-off (e.g., on a validation set) and select the reward level 

α at their convenience. If noisy test data can be encountered frequently enough, we suggest that the end-user train multiple (specialist) models to handle clean test data and noisy test data separately. Switching between these models might be monitored using additional information. For example, one might try to allow the UAV, which detects Plant Leaf Diseases in farms, to switch to (specialist) models to handle noisy test data when 25 encountering severe weather. If training and monitoring a single predictive model is more desirable, the end-user might think of training the model on both clean and noisy data, which can be either collected or generated using data augmentation techniques. Finally, we suggest that the end-user can select a well-calibrated ensemble, which produces reliable representative distributions (8), when deploying our framework. 

# 6 Conclusion 

We propose a compact cautious deep ensembling framework to tackle visible challenges in deep ensemble learning, where deep neural networks serve as ensemble members: training and storage burdens, and robustness of cautious (set-valued) predictions tar-geting multiple utilities, which may involve reward-sensitivity. We present both the generation and aggregation phases of the proposed framework, with a focus on the latter phase, where we propose a family of reward-sensitive utility-discounted accura-cies with various desirable properties and efficient computations of set-valued BOPs. To our knowledge, we are the first who satisfactorily achieve both aspects in this set-ting. Along the way, we also provide a few new results for the simpler case of the conventional utility-discounted accuracies. We conduct various experiments on three image data sets to illustrate potential applications of the proposed framework. First, the empirical evidence suggests that our framework can provide a promising way to balance recall and prediction of set-valued predictions when reward-sensitivity is important. Moreover, it provides the end-user with an intuitive way to customize the reward-sensitive discounted accuracies at their convenience. Second, the empirical evidence suggests that different implementations of the proposed framework may be advantageous in different aspects. Notably, while defining the representative distribution using the squared Euclidean distance tends to be in favor of recall, doing so with the L1 distance and the KL divergence tends to be in favor of precision. This also suggests that improving the proposed framework by exploring the potential (dis)advantages of other distances might be worthwhile. Although investigating the robustness of BNNs and CNNs with the Monte-Carlo dropout prediction under distribution shifts is not a primary goal of this paper, the empirical evidence suggests that BNNs tend to be (significantly) more robust. How-ever, distribution shifts often significantly downgrade the predictive performance. We therefore suggest an extensive study on the use of multiple models, one per group of distribution shift scenarios, as another potential follow-up work. Such a work may greatly benefit high-stakes applications such as autonomous vehicles and UAVs.
