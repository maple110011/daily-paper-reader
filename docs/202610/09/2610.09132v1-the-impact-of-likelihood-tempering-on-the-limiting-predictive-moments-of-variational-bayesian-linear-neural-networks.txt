# The Impact of Likelihood Tempering on the Limiting Predictive Moments of Variational Bayesian Linear Neural Networks

Abstract 

In wide Bayesian neural networks, Gaussian mean-field variational inference is prone to 

prior dominance : the Kullback-Leibler (KL) regularization term of the ELBO outweighs the expected log-likelihood, and the variational predictive distribution collapses to the prior predictive as the width M grows. Tempering the likelihood, by raising it to the power 1/T for a temperature T < 1, is equivalent to scaling the KL term by T . We ask in this paper how fast T must decrease with M to counteract this degeneracy and strike a good balance between the two terms. For single-hidden-layer linear networks with isotropic Gaussian priors, we derive the limiting predictive distribution under schedules of the form 

T = τ /M c, with constants τ, c > 0, as M → ∞ and compare it with the untempered neural network Gaussian process (NNGP) posterior, the infinite-width limit of the exact posterior. Our main result is that the predictive expectation and variance undergo phase transitions at different scales: the limiting expectation leaves its prior value at c = 1 /2, once τ falls below an explicit threshold, and equals the least-squares prediction for c > 1/2, whereas the limiting variance keeps its prior value for c < 1, matches the NNGP’s for c = 1, and vanishes for c > 1. With suitable choices of τ, c , one can recover either the NNGP posterior expectation or its variance. 

# 1 Introduction 

Bayesian inference equips a model with a posterior distribution over its parameters, from which one obtains uncertainty quantification and a principled means of comparing models through the evidence (MacKay, 1992; Ghahramani, 2015). Neural networks have been treated in this way since MacKay (1992) and Neal (1996): a Bayesian neural network (BNN) places a prior p(θ) on the weights, separates the uncertainty due to observation noise from the uncertainty about the fitted function (Kendall and Gal, 2017), and thus combines the flexibility of neural networks with principled uncertainty quantification (Jospin et al., 2022; Arbel et al., 2026). The posterior 

p(θ | D ) of a BNN is intractable, however, and sampling methods such as MCMC, although asymptotically exact and successfully applied in practice (Neal, 1996; Izmailov et al., 2021), remain costly for modern architectures. Variational inference (VI) offers a tractable alternative, approximating the posterior by the member q∗(θ) of a chosen family Q that maximizes the evi-dence lower bound (ELBO) (Blei et al., 2017). VI became particularly practical once stochastic optimization of the ELBO became available (Hoffman et al., 2013; Graves, 2011), and with the reparameterization trick (Kingma and Welling, 2014) it resembles ordinary backpropagation (Blundell et al., 2015). Gaussian variational learning has been applied successfully to large modern networks (Shen et al., 2024).

The Gaussian mean-field variational family, under which (blocks of) pa-rameters are independent, is the most common variational family for BNNs, and is commonly held to underestimate posterior uncertainty (Minka, 2005; Blei et al., 2017). In BNNs, it also restricts the predictive uncertainty that can be expressed (Foong et al., 2020). While it is true that underestimation does hold in the parameter space (Margossian and Saul, 2023), this is not (completely) so for predictions. In conjugate Bayesian linear regression, Odgers et al. (2026) show that the mean-field optimum overestimates the predictive variance on inputs resembling the training data and underestimates it in other directions. In overparameterized random fea-ture regression with an isotropic Gaussian variational family, the optimal variational posterior variance likewise reverts to its prior value as the number of features grows with the sample size fixed (Harvey et al., 2026). In both models the parameters enter the predictor linearly and the mean is preserved, so the degradation concerns the variance alone. In neural networks, where the parameters of consecutive layers combine in a nonlinear fashion, the mean is lost as well. Coker et al. (2022) prove that, for networks with odd, Lipschitz activations, the optimal Gaussian mean-field predictive distribution converges to the prior predictive as the architecture width increases, so that the approximation effectively ignores the data. Empirical signs of this degeneracy appear in Trippe and Turner (2018), where weights revert to their prior, and in Sun et al. (2019), where predictions deteriorate as networks grow. We refer to this phenomenon as “prior dominance” (see also Huix et al., 2022; Descours et al., 2023): the Kullback-Leibler divergence (KL) term of the ELBO outweighs the expected log-likelihood, thus the variational posterior reverts to the prior in function-space. 

Remedies. Given the previous observation, two families of approaches have been proposed. Since network outputs are of more interest than network parameters themselves, the first ap-proach moves the divergence to function-space, where the induced prior on the functions com-puted by the network is considered (Sun et al., 2019; Rudner et al., 2022). Indeed, when the variational distribution and the prior over functions are both induced by distributions over the parameters, the data-processing inequality ensures that the functional KL-divergence stays below its parameter-space counterpart, reducing the regularization. However, no such bound is available when the prior is specified directly on functions (for instance, via a Gaussian pro-cess), and functional KL-divergence may be infinite (Sun et al., 2019; Burt et al., 2021). This motivates regularized variants (Cinquin and Bamler, 2025). The second approach keeps the parameter-space objective but downweights its KL regular-ization term relative to the expected log-likelihood (Zhang et al., 2018; Osawa et al., 2019). This reweighting is equivalent to tempering the likelihood alone, that is, to raising it to the power 1/T for a temperature T < 1, and recent work prescribes how T should depend on the size of the model instead of tuning it: Huix et al. (2022) and Descours et al. (2023) scale the KL term inversely with the number of neurons of a shallow network, while Harvey et al. (2026) upweight the likelihood by the ratio of the number of parameters to the sample size. Likelihood tempering must be distinguished from the ‘cold posteriors’ of Wenzel et al. (2020), which raise the whole posterior, including the prior, to the power 1 /T (Aitchison, 2021; Odgers et al., 2026). Arbel et al. (2026) review the debate surrounding both. Odgers et al. (2026) argue that, in conjugate linear regression, a cold posterior corrects the predictive variance while keeping the exact mean, whereas likelihood tempering cannot correct the variance without displacing the mean. When the mean itself has been lost to the prior, it is not clear which quantities tempering can restore. 

Two infinite-width scalings. These prescriptions of temperatures depend on the param-eterization of the network. Indeed, different scalings of a fully-connected single-hidden-layer network with its width M lead to different limits. In the mean-field scaling, the output is the average of M neurons, f (x) = M −1 ∑Mm=1 bm φ(a⊤

> m

x), whose priors do not depend on M . It is the scaling of the mean-field analyses of gradient training (Mei et al., 2018; Chizat and Bach, 22018; Rotskoff and Vanden-Eijnden, 2022; Sirignano and Spiliopoulos, 2020), and it is the one adopted by Huix et al. (2022) and Descours et al. (2023). By the law of large numbers, the prior over functions concentrates on a deterministic function, losing the benefits of the Bayesian approach in the limit M → ∞ . In the scaling we adopt here, the sum is instead normalized by M −1/2, as in the neural tangent kernel (NTK) parameterization (Jacot et al., 2018) or, equivalently for the prior, by giving the output weights variance of order 1 /M . The induced prior over functions then converges to a non-degenerate Gaussian process, the neural network Gaussian process (NNGP) (Neal, 1996; Lee et al., 2018; de G. Matthews et al., 2018), and the exact posterior converges to the corresponding NNGP posterior (Hron et al., 2020), whose mean and variance are available in closed form under a Gaussian likelihood. While this limit provides important insights into the behaviour of BNNs, it is also the natural regime in which to ask whether a variational approximation recovers posterior uncertainty. Coker et al. (2022) estab-lish under this setting the collapse of the mean-field approximation, and the random-feature model of Harvey et al. (2026) uses the same normalization. In our notation, the prescriptions of the previous part all correspond to temperatures of order 1 /M . In the mean-field scaling, this is the rate at which the two terms of the ELBO remain balanced, and Descours et al. (2023) prove a law of large numbers for the resulting training dynamics. In the random-feature model, where the mean is preserved, it prevents the variational variance from reverting to its prior value (Harvey et al., 2026). Neither result covers the M −1/2 scaling with both layers trained, in which the predictive mean is lost as well. 

Question and setting. We ask how fast the temperature must decrease with the width to counteract prior dominance in the NNGP regime, and whether a single schedule can recover both the posterior predictive mean and the posterior predictive variance. We study both ques-tions in the tractable setting of single-hidden-layer linear networks in the M −1/2 scaling, with isotropic zero-mean Gaussian priors, and in the limit M → ∞ with the sample size n and input dimension d fixed. Linear networks have long served as a tractable alternative to their nonlin-ear counterparts (Saxe et al., 2014), and their mean-field variational approximations have been analyzed in detail at fixed width, where the linear network amounts to reduced-rank regres-sion or matrix factorization (Nakajima and Watanabe, 2007; Nakajima and Sugiyama, 2011; Nakajima et al., 2013). Our analysis departs from these works in that we let the width grow to infinity, beyond the reduced-rank regime, we temper the likelihood, and we focus on the resulting predictive distribution over functions. We consider schedules of the form T = τ /M c

with constants τ, c > 0, and we compare the limiting variational predictive distribution with the exact untempered NNGP posterior.

# 5 Discussion 

We show that for wide shallow linear BNNs, likelihood tempering provides a partial remedy for prior dominance, in which the variational predictive distribution ignores the data. Under our assumptions, the predictive mean and variance can be recovered at different scales of tempera-ture, M −1/2 for mean and M −1 for variance. No choice of τ, c > 0 in TM = τ /M c recovers both untempered NNGP posterior predictive moments simultaneously. A similar trade-off between recovering the predictive mean and variance under likelihood tempering is discussed by Odgers et al. (2026) for conjugate linear regression. It remains to study variational approximations 10 of cold posteriors, where the likelihood and prior are jointly rescaled, to determine at which temperature scaling the different predictive moments can potentially be recovered. While we focus on the predictive distribution at a single input for simplicity, the multivariate central limit theorem would extend our results to finite marginals of this predictive process. A natural extension would be to investigate if similar results in mean and variance recovery under different tempering scales would be present in nonlinear or deeper networks. Another direction to consider would be to investigate a larger variational family that would allow de-pendence between each neuron’s input and output weights, and not necessarily impose the mean-field assumption. Finally, our results concern global minimizers of the variational ob-jective, and whether gradient-based optimisation reaches them at large width, and whether it exhibits the same phase transitions as in Theorem 3.1, is a separate question.
