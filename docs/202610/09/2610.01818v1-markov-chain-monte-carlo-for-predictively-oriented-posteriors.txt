# Markov chain Monte Carlo for predictively oriented posteriors

Abstract. The predictively oriented posterior offers principled uncertainty quantification, even under model misspecification. However, it does not admit an explicit density and therefore cannot be computed using classical Monte Carlo sampling algorithms. We remedy this by deriving an approximation to the predictively oriented posterior whose density can be evaluated point-wise, and whose approximation error decays rapidly. These results are illustrated on case studies from epidemiology, spatial statistics, and low-energy nuclear physics. 

1. Introduction 

Bayes’ theorem optimally updates prior beliefs on unknown parameters to posterior beliefs conditional on observed data (Zellner, 1988). When the assumed statistical model agrees with the process that generated the observed data, the Bayesian posterior leads to predictions that are also optimal (Aitchison, 1975). The accuracy of these predictions, however, is tethered to the assumed model’s ability to recover the true data generating process: when it is misspecified, optimality is no longer guaranteed, and the standard Bayesian posterior ceases to be the natural object of interest. In response to this, predictively oriented (PrO) posteriors recast parameter uncertainty as a function of the induced predictive distribution directly (Lai et al., 2026; McLatchie et al., 2025a; Shen et al., 2025). Under some regularity conditions, PrO posteriors predictively dominate the Bayes posterior and converge to the predictively optimal model. Translating this theoretically appealing construction into practice has so far been limited by computation. The PrO posterior is defined implicitly through a non-linear optimisation programme over the space of probability measures. This non-linearity, which is central to the very definition of the PrO posterior, is the main computational obstruction: the minimiser is characterised only by an implicit fixed-point equation. While variational approaches to the PrO posterior can sidestep this particular computational hurdle (Lai et al., 2026; Masegosa, 2020; Morningstar et al., 2022), they introduce biases and approximation errors whose effects on inference cannot be easily understood or controlled (Wei et al., 2021). This induces a direct trade-off between computational complexity and accuracy, and can violate many of the theoretical benefits demonstrated by McLatchie et al. (2025a). Consequently, the main way to draw samples from PrO posteriors with theoretical guarantees has been using a Wasserstein gradient flow (WGF) of the entropy-regularised predictive score (McLatchie et al., 2025a; Shen et al., 2025). The resulting dynamics depend at every time on the current law, and thus cannot be simulated directly. In practice, this law is replaced by an empirical measure, resulting in an interacting particle system. Whilst asymptotically exact as both time and particle number tend to infinity, this introduces a number of hyper-parameters for which principled tuning guidance remains limited. Similarly, mature convergence diagnostics of the kind available for Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC; B¨ urkner et al., 2026; Martin et al., 2026; Stan Development Team, 2026) are currently lacking in this setting. Our central contribution concerns the equilibrium of this particle-discretised WGF: for any fixed number of particles, this stationary distribution induces an explicit Gibbs form on an extended parameter space. Leveraging this observation, we propose an approximation to the PrO posterior that circumvents the necessity of simulating the WGF. Instead, it can now be sampled directly by MCMC, allowing for adaptive tuning and the use of gradient-free methods. The remainder

In contrast, Figure 5 illustrates that both Bayes and Gibbs posteriors concentrate near 0 .6 fm but place substantial mass below the accepted range and almost none near the point estimate of Koning and Delaroche (2003), suggesting a sharper nuclear surface than is generally accepted in the literature. 

8. Discussion 

This paper transforms the PrO posterior from a theoretically grounded inferential object to a practically computable one. Our results justify sampling from the PrO posterior using standard MCMC methods, and constitute a step change in the practicality of the PrO framework. While the observation that the particle discretisation of the mean-field Langevin dynamics possesses a tractable limiting measure that may be sampled directly is not specific to the PrO posterior, our generalisation guarantees are, and our finite-particle guarantees require much weaker conditions than classical propagation of chaos results. Several limitations merit comment. The extended target 𝑄 𝑛,𝑚 is defined on a space whose dimension grows with the number of particles, so the cost of mixing on this space must be weighed against the Monte Carlo error in the number of particles; these are distinct objectives, and our theoretical results speak to the second while our numerical experiments bear on the first. Future work may look to establish heuristics for choosing 𝑚 based on a finite computational budget and desired accuracy. Similarly, our theoretical analysis omits covariates; if these can be addressed, there is also the opportunity to extend the blocked Gibbs sampler to generalised linear models (Chen and Ibrahim, 2003).
