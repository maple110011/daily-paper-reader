Abstract 

Sampling from a probability distribution with access only to its log-density is a foundational problem in statistics, machine learning, and the physical sciences. This problem is studied in the data-free score-based diffusion literature, which samples the target density via estimating scores of densities produced by convolution with Gaussian random variables and implementing a reverse SDE. In this framework, score estimators are constructed via Markov chain Monte Carlo, importance sampling, or rejection sampling. The estimators often operate far beyond the regime where mixing time and error control can be established and have large bias and variance when estimating the underlying score. In our method we propose post-processing: splitting a fixed computation budget across multiple evaluations, querying the estimator at different points, and fitting a single pooled estimate via linear combination to minimize bias and variance at a target location. The final value is an affine map of a vector of multiple score estimates. We show this reduces score estimation error when compared to the computation-matched base estimator queried at a single point. We demonstrate improved sample quality over other score estimation methods matched for computation cost on difficult target densities such as log-concave densities with large condition number, multimodal densities, and high-dimensional densities. 1

## 1 Introduction 

Sampling from a probability density π(x) ∝ e−f (x) given only the negative log-density f is a foun-dational problem across Bayesian inference, statistical physics, and computational biology, among many other fields. Traditionally, this problem has been approached with sampling algorithms such as time-invariant Markov chain Monte Carlo, annealing methods, and tempering algorithms. More recently, the field of data-free score-based diffusion has offered an alternative method: sampling from the target density by reversing a noising process of Gaussian convolution. Implementing such a sampling algorithm requires estimating the noised score function with only knowledge of f .Consider a random variable U defined by a linear combination of a sample from π and a Gaussian random variable,

This has motivated a line of diffusion-based Monte Carlo methods in which the score of the noised target is estimated pointwise by a random estimator ˆ s(u, a ) computed with knowledge of f alone. This estimator should be considered a random variable with internal randomness: when queried at a fixed u and a value, it will return a value with some probability distribution. With knowledge of f , this estimator can be a form of importance sampling (IS) [17], rejection sampling (RS) [9], or Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) [10, 11, 8] approximating an empirical average of Tweedie’s formula to estimate the score. The reverse conditional density pa(x|u) which defines the expectation in Tweedie’s formula is often high-dimensional and multimodal, making it particularly difficult to estimate for sampling-based score estimators. Although such estimators may draw hundreds or thousands of proposal points, often only a handful contribute meaningfully to the empirical average. The effective sample size is therefore much smaller than the number of proposal points, and it grows little as more samples are drawn. As a result, these estimators suffer from high bias and variance at practical sample sizes, with significant error reduction only at sample sizes exponentially large in the state space dimension. In this paper, we consider an alternative route to reducing bias and variance: querying the estimator at a fixed location u across multiple signal levels {ai}Ni=0 , and pooling the resulting estimates {ˆs(u, a i)}Ni=0 together with the known terminal values s(u, 0) = −u and s(u, 1) = −∇ f (u). For any vector h ∈ RN +1 , g ∈ R2, ν ∈ R we define an affine post-processor as any estimation rule ¯s(u, a 0) = ∑Ni=0 hi ˆs(u, a i) + g0s(u, 0) + g1s(u, 1) − ν. (2) It is a form of linear smoother or ridge regression performing bias and variance reduction via a shrinkage procedure. We refer to the signal level a0 as the on-policy level, and the queries ˆs(u, a i), i ̸ = 0 as off-policy evaluations. The choice of parameters h = e0, g = 0 , ν = 0, where e0 = (1 , 0, . . . , 0) ∈ RN +1 , we call “pure on-policy estimation” at ˆ s(u, a 0), so simply relying on the score estimator’s on-policy estimate is already a form of affine post-processing. If there is an alternative choice of ( h, g, ν ) which strictly reduces the average squared bias and variance at a signal level, then it reduces the path-averaged score error that controls the sampling error bound, which we observe as a reduction in terminal Wasserstein distance ( W2). We demonstrate that: 1. The optimal choice of smoothing parameters relies on knowledge of the bias and variance of the base estimator. It can be written down in closed form, which we call the Wiener oracle post-processor. If it is not equal to ( e0, 0, 0), then optimally chosen affine post-processing strictly reduces score estimation error. 2. We show pure on-policy estimation is optimal only under a specific zero-gradient condition most estimators will violate. In particular, for any unbiased estimator with non-zero variance, pure on-policy estimation is not optimal among affine post-processors. 3. We construct practical, non-oracle post-processors via ridge regression in the Legendre poly-nomial basis, and in our experiments their score error captures most of the improvement available to the Wiener oracle. 4. Post-processing is compared at matched computation cost: a fixed MCMC budget of Neval 

evaluations is split across the N + 1 query levels, and the pooled estimate is compared to a single on-policy estimate given the full Neval budget. The effective sample size grows slowly in 

Neval , thus the on-policy estimator gains little from the extra budget, while the split-and-pool estimator reduces score estimation error substantially. 2Empirically, diffusion-based samplers built on pure on-policy score estimation degrade quickly as dimension grows. The Tweedie integral becomes hard to estimate directly in high dimensions, and base score estimates carry large bias and large variance. Post-processing is a general procedure that can be applied on top of any base score estimator. We run experiments with IS and Reverse Diffu-sion Monte Carlo (RDMC) [10] as base estimators in high-dimensional problems. Post-processing reduces score estimation error substantially, and improves sample quality with W2 distance up to two orders of magnitude smaller at equal compute. This makes diffusion-based samplers practical in dimensions where their base estimators would otherwise fail.

4.1 Post-Processing improves score estimation for unimodal targets

## 5 Conclusion and Future Works 

We introduce an affine post-processing approach to score estimation. We demonstrate, with a random score estimator with nonzero bias and variance, on-policy estimation is almost never the best use of a fixed computation budget for score estimation. Splitting a compute budget across queries and fitting an affine combination of the values gives a lower error. This allows us to scale diffusion-based approaches to higher dimensions, while retaining the benefits of the score-based samplers. Post-processing is an additional step built on top of a base score estimator. When the base queries become so corrupted they supply little useful off-policy information, the optimal post-processing rule can collapse to pure on-policy estimation. In this case, the sample budget spent on off-policy queries is not utilized, and some small improvement is seen by spending the full estimation budget on-policy to slightly increase the effective sample size. For this reason, improving the base estimator’s exploration of the space is an area that still admits further exploration. ## E Hyperparameter Selection 

Our algorithm has several hyperparameters that need to be chosen. Specifically, we have to choose the hyperparameters for the base estimator, which for importance sampling and rejection sampling it is simply a constant M saying how any Monte Carlo samples to use. Then there is the size of the grid K, and the degree of the polynomial Kdeg , as well as the number of batches N to estimate the covariance matrix, and finally the hyperparameter λ for the regression objective. 26 Because N the number of batches needed to estimate the covariance matrix for the regression problem, before tuning anything downstream we first check how well Σ itself is estimated, since every other hyperparameter will depend on it. 

E.1 The reverse grid 

We place the 20 levels of (6) by the step-size rule 

τk = c min {tk, 1}, tk = − log ak, (93) in the diffusion time t of the equivalent variance-preserving process, so that tk is the distance to the data end a = 1: steps are uniform in t while the noise dominates, and shrink in proportion to 

tk once tk < 1. This is the exponentially decreasing schedule of Chen et al. [4], He et al. [9], Benton et al. [1], where it is what gives the optimal dependence on the early-stopping time. We use c = 0 .85, which places the grid close to uniform in the log signal-to-noise ratio λ =log (a2/(1 − a2)) the spacing used by Kingma et al. [12] and Lu et al. [15]. The levels then run from 

a0 = 6 .7 × 10 −3 to a19 = 0 .999 with hk/a k ≤ 0.68 throughout. We do not tune c or the grid any further. 

E.2 The configuration used in the experiments 

Table 1 collects the set of hyperparameters ran/sweep for different methods across all of our experi-ments. The compute budget is stated in target-gradient evaluations and is matched across samplers: each particle costs 60 ,000 × 20 = 1 .2M evaluations, whether they are spent as importance-sampling draws, as RDMC inner-chain steps, or as moves of a classical chain. Every number reported for the ill-conditioned Gaussian uses one configuration, unchanged across all 16 cells of the d × κ grid: • Design levels. N + 1 = 6 nodes on a uniform grid over b ∈ [0 .05 , 0.95], with the query level 

a0 substituted for whichever grid point is nearest to it. Substituting rather than appending matters: appending lets two design levels coincide when a0 falls near a grid point, which makes two rows of ˆΣ near-identical and the solve ill-conditioned. • Degree. Kdeg = 5, the largest degree the node count supports ( A is ( Kdeg +1) × (N +1), so 

Kdeg ≤ N is required for Ah = b to be solvable). • Covariance batches. Nbatch = 2 replicate groups, pooled across the particle batch, and the mean of those groups is reused as the data h is applied to rather than drawing a further inde-pendent group. Reuse costs a plug-in bias of order 1 /N batch but cuts the reported estimate’s variance by the same factor and saves N +1 calls; measured, it was worth roughly 20% in W2.• Misfit weights. wk ∝ k−3, with the degree-0 constraint enforced exactly. By the primal-dual identity of Section 3.2 this is exactly a k3 ridge on the fitted coefficients, expressed in the dual variables. • Endpoint anchors. On not tuning λ per target. λ is the one hyperparameter whose optimum visibly moves across the grid, and it would be easy to report the best value in each cell. We do not, because that is an oracle choice a deployed sampler does not get. The cost of fixing it is small and worth stating: selecting the best λ ∈ { 1, 10 } per cell improves the geometric-mean gain over raw from 1 .94 × to 2.09 × across the grid, and by 0–14% in each cell where the method wins. We report the untuned 

λ = 1 number throughout. 

A configuration we exclude. Large λ (≥ 100) is unstable: across the grid it diverges on a subset of seeds in five separate cells, with a seed standard deviation comparable to or larger than the mean, while λ ∈ { 1, 10 } is stable in the same cells. Large λ drives the fit toward exact mean preservation and away from variance damping, which is the same regime in which the ϵ → 0endpoint limit fails. We exclude it rather than report a number produced by a diverging run.

M and b we tried. Rejection sampling’s ˆΣ is noisier and slower converging in every target, which is expected considering the acceptance rate. We find that the group size M has a smaller effect in this regime; on the other hand, the choice of N is more important. A precise ˆΣ takes tens of replicates. A crude estimate of Σ, however, is enough to set the weights, consistent with the observation in Appendix C.1 that Σ = I performs comparably well. We therefore estimate it with only Nbatch = 2 replicates in our experiments, which keeps each replicate’s Monte Carlo sample large and so limits the bias of self-normalized importance sampling. When more compute is available, more replicates can be used.
