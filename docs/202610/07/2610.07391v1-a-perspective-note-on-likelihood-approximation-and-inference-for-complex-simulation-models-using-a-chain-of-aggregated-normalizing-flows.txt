A PERSPECTIVE NOTE ON LIKELIHOOD APPROXIMATION AND INFERENCE FOR COMPLEX SIMULATION MODELS USING A CHAIN OF AGGREGATED NORMALIZING FLOWS 

GETACHEW K. BEFEKADU ∗

Abstract. In this work, we present a new perspective on the problem of likelihood approximation within the framework of simulation-based inference that promotes scalable and controllable simulation routines for large-scale data analysis, allows efficient parameter space exploration or smooth interpolation in high-dimensions and, thus, supports valid statistical treatments of hypothesis testings as well as uncertainty quantification. In particular, we consider a chain of n-aggregated normalizing flows (i.e., a set of well-structured bijective transformations admitting a sequence of functional composition and aggregating estimated parameters) for likelihood approximation scheme, where a set of upfront replicated observation datasets from the forward complex simulation model pass through the first set of bijective transformations, and then subsequently pass to the other sets of bijective transformations. Here, we assume that, for any k ∈ { 1, 2, . . . , n }, the parameters corresponding to the first k sets of bijective transfor-mations are estimated sequentially, in some sense of optimality, for constructing flexible probability distributions, regardless of the remaining (n − k) sets of bijective transformations. Moreover, our objects of interest are to high-light two complementary mathematical arguments that leverage an informatics-theoretic formalization, based-on empirical likelihood estimators under moment restrictions, and a sequential decision-making paradigm, with a set of mixing distribution strategies, for updating and aggregating the estimated parameters of the overall normalizing flows. Finally, as a by-product, the framework presented in this work also provides a reliable surrogate model, condi-tioned on the model parameters defining the forward computational simulation, that allows samples generation, with statistical powers, and further facilitates computationally tractable scheme in the Bayesian paradigm for inference, hypothesis testings and uncertainty quantification. 

Key words. Bijective transformation, decision strategy, empirical-likelihood, hypothesis testing, Bayesian in-ference, mixing distribution, moment restrictions, normalizing flow, simulation model, surrogate model.

1. Introduction. With the advent of high performance computing and highly expressive programming languages, researchers as well as practitioners from various domains of applied science and engineering are increasingly relying on complex simulation models as an integral part of their broader scientific investigations that include interpreting observation datasets, forecasting system behaviors, quantifying uncertainties, synthesizing plausible hypotheses or making decisions, and optimal experiment designs. Although these complex, and often large-scale, simulation models provide useful information about the underlying real physical systems or experimental processes they represent. It is also acknowledged that, due to the lack of closed form or intractability of the corresponding exact likelihood functions, they are poorly suitable by themselves for statistical inference tasks – such as assessing the predic-tive capabilities for exploratory scenarios or estimating model parameters for the underlying inverse problems – that are required evidently for a rigorous uncertainty quantification on quantities of interest (e.g., see [1]-[7] for interesting studies discussing the need of address-ing such challenges). As a result of this, developing a viable statistical inference framework in the Bayesian paradigm, that provides reliable solutions for quantities of interest and their uncertainties, while computationally tractable, i.e., admitting computationally efficient and scalable routines for constructing flexible and/or locally approximating target probability dis-tributions, is certainly indispensable for both researchers as well as practitioners working in

Here, it is worth mentioning that some interesting studies on normalizing flows as a class of generative models for continuous ran-dom variables, that provide a mechanism for representing or defining expressive probability distributions, have been reported in machine learning literature (e.g., see [8]-[14] for some interesting discussions). Note that the rationale behind our framework follows, in some sense, the settings of these papers, but in a different context.
